**Asked:** how do SGLT2 inhibitors lower blood sugar

> They work at the kidney, not the pancreas. Glucose gets filtered at the glomerulus, and normally almost all of it is reabsorbed by the sodium-glucose transporter. SGLT2 inhibitors block that reabsorption, so the sugar leaves in the urine (glucosuria), more of it as the dose goes up. Because they don't push insulin out, hypoglycemia is rare.
>   · [N1.76] "SGLT2 inhibitors block that reabsorption, so the patient pees out the sugar"
>
> The side effects follow from sugary urine pulling water along: genital yeast infections, UTIs, polyuria, and dizziness or hypotension. The class carries a boxed warning for amputation (canagliflozin, ertugliflozin). Since they act like diuretics, they help heart failure too, and dapagliflozin (Farxiga) is approved for it. Dr. Fasinu's memory aid: -gliflozin sounds like flushing the sugar away. I just flush mine with water.
>   · [N1.77] "genital fungal (yeast) infections in men and women, urinary tract infections, polyuria"

**Asked:** What is C-peptide and why would you measure it?

> C-peptide is the leftover piece from making insulin. Beta cells build proinsulin, then cut it into insulin (the A and B chains) plus C-peptide, the bit that held the chains together. The two come out in equal amounts.
>   · [N1.17] "cleaved into insulin (the A and B chains) plus C-peptide"
>
> Why measure it: an insulin level can't tell injected insulin from the patient's own, but injected insulin carries no C-peptide. So in someone who has injected insulin for years, absent C-peptide means the pancreas has stopped making insulin, which can happen with time in type 2 too. Dr. Fasinu: "C-peptide level will always tell you if they are making insulin at all." It also matters for drug choice: secretagogues like sulfonylureas only work if beta cells still make insulin, so no C-peptide means insulin, not a secretagogue.
>   · [N1.17] "injected insulin carries no C-peptide"
>   · [H9] "Secretagogues fail when beta cells are gone (for example when C-peptide is absent)"
>   · [R1.10] "No insulin left (C-peptide absent) · Insulin"
>
> Think of it as the receipt that comes with homemade insulin. Store-bought never has one. My analogy, not the professor's.

**Asked:** Explain diabetes insipidus versus SIADH

> Dr. Dababneh's trick: let ADH's name carry you. It works against diuresis. Diabetes insipidus is a lack of ADH action, so the tubules can't take water back: polyuria, low urine specific gravity, and high serum sodium and osmolality. Central DI is a deficiency of ADH (head trauma, tumors, inflammation, pituitary or hypothalamic surgery). Nephrogenic DI has normal ADH, but the kidneys don't respond.
>   · [N2.44] "It is a lack of ADH action: the tubules can't take water back"
>   · [N2.45] "a low urine specific gravity, and a high serum sodium and osmolality"
>   · [N2.46] "ADH is normal but the kidneys don't respond to it"
>
> SIADH is the opposite: ADH keeps being released regardless of osmolality, water is retained, fluids are diluted, and sodium falls (hyponatremia). The most common cause is ectopic ADH from small cell carcinoma of the lung, which the professor flagged.
>   · [N2.47] "water is retained, body fluids are diluted, and the patient has hyponatremia"
>   · [H32] "The most common cause of an ectopic production of ADH is small cell carcinoma of the lung."
>
> Short version: DI means sodium high and urine dilute; SIADH means sodium low and water kept. Opposites, like me and a cavity.

**Asked:** What do Sertoli cells do?

> Dr. Herr calls Sertoli cells "the refrigerator": they feed and support the developing sperm. Each one is tall and columnar, reaching from the basement membrane to the lumen. Tight junctions between neighboring Sertoli cells form the blood-testis barrier. It splits the tubule into a basal compartment (spermatogonia) and an adluminal compartment (spermatocytes and spermatids), which keeps the dividing germ cells away from the immune system.
>   · [N4.26] "They make the secretions that nourish the developing sperm and help transport them."
>   · [N4.27] "These junctions divide the tubule into two compartments"
>
> They answer to FSH. They make androgen-binding protein, which keeps testosterone high in the tubules, and inhibin, which brakes FSH. In the fetus they make AMH, which removes the Müllerian ducts. They also eat the cytoplasm shed by spermatids and defective sperm, release sperm (spermiation), and hold most of the aromatase. They don't divide in the adult, and they resist heat and radiation.

>   · [N4.34] "Making and releasing inhibin, which blocks FSH release by the anterior pituitary"

**Asked:** What happens to FSH, LH and estrogen at menopause?

> At menopause all the remaining follicles undergo complete atresia, so the granulosa cells stop making estradiol and inhibin. With nothing left to give negative feedback, GnRH, LH and FSH all rise. Dr. Madison put it as "losing the brake." The GnRH peak has been linked to hot flashes, though he said that research is still going on.
>   · [N7.94] "granulosa cells no longer make estradiol or inhibin"
>   · [N7.95] "With nothing left to give negative feedback, GnRH, LH and FSH all rise"
>   · [H120] "we're losing the brake, we're losing that negative feedback regulation"
>
> Estrogen doesn't vanish, it changes type: estrone becomes the dominant estrogen, made from DHEA from the adrenal zona reticularis, and it is not nearly as potent as estradiol. Watch the look-alike: LH is high at midcycle too, but there estradiol is high (positive feedback), while in menopause estradiol is low.
>   · [N7.96] "Estrone becomes the dominant estrogen, made from adrenal DHEA"
>   · [R9.22] "Positive feedback from estradiol · No negative feedback left"
>
> Short version: no follicles, no brake, everything upstream climbs. Even I know a thing or two about losing support.

**Asked:** What's the Bennett movement?

> Bennett movement is mandibular lateral translation: the sideways shift of the whole mandible toward the working side. It goes by several names, side shift among them. It can be immediate (a jump, measured in millimeters) or progressive (measured as an angle, the Bennett angle). **Asked:** what's the deal with bisphosphonates in perio

> Dr. Litz covered them under host modulation. On paper they look like the magic bullet: bone-seeking drugs that inhibit osteoclasts, maybe with an anticollagenase effect too. The catch is BRONJ, bone necrosis after an invasive dental procedure (an extraction or perio surgery counts), sometimes even spontaneously, with most complications from the high-dose IV forms.
>   · [N8.53] "They are bone-seeking agents that inhibit bone resorption by inhibiting osteoclasts"

> Lingualized occlusion is a denture scheme that sits between balanced and non-balanced. The maxillary posterior teeth have an elongated lingual cusp that fits against a flat plane or a wide fossa on the mandibular teeth. Dr. Givan compared it to a mortar and pestle. The buccal cusps don't touch.
