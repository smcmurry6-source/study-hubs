## 2. Sessions one to three: diagnosis, flaps and prognosis

Session one, Doctor Abou-Arraj. Three numbers start every diagnosis. Probing depth runs from the gingival margin to the base of the pocket. Recession runs from the C-E-J to the margin. And clinical attachment level, C-A-L, is the two added together: the C-E-J to the base of the pocket.

Staging comes from attachment and bone, never from probing depth alone. In his words: Do not look at your periodontal chart and see where you have fours and fives and assign the stage based on your probing depth, because that could be incorrect. You may over-diagnose a case that does not even have periodontitis, just because a patient has a bunch of fours and fives.

Speaking of which. Number thirty-one, my neighbor, the second molar right behind me, heard this lecture through the wall. He probed himself with a toothpick, found a four, and announced that he is Stage four, Grade C. Thirty-one. A toothpick is not a probe. You have no bone loss. And a four is not a stage. You're not staged. You're anxious.

For the final, he said to know the criteria. The stage: interdental C-A-L first, radiographic bone loss second, then teeth lost to periodontitis and complexity. Stages one and two stay in the coronal third of the root. Stages three and four both have five millimeters of C-A-L or more, and what separates them isn't more attachment loss: it's tooth loss, four or fewer teeth for three, five or more for four, and how complex the rehabilitation is. A vertical defect of three millimeters or more, a Class two or three furcation, or a tooth lost to periodontitis puts a case straight into Stage three or four.

The grade: start at B. Percent bone loss on the worst tooth, divided by age: under point two five is A, over one is C. Smoking more than ten cigarettes a day, or an A-one-C of seven percent or more, makes it C whatever the ratio says. Thirty-one, since you asked: you don't smoke, and you don't have a pancreas.

Two more from session one. In his words: And never forget this, is caries control is part of Phase one. And maintenance works. Treated patients who skip maintenance lose teeth about twice as fast as those who keep their recalls.

Session two, Doctor Basma. For the final, Doctor Abou-Arraj narrowed it down. In his words: Maybe just know the sutures as well, like different kinds of sutures, not techniques, but the different materials. Yeah. No, no images of suture.

The resorbables: chromic gut resorbs fastest and handles worst. P-G-C-L is a monofilament in the middle, gentle on tissue, for soft tissue grafts. P-G-A is braided, resorbs slowest, handles best, and has the worst tissue reaction of the three. The non-resorbables: polypropylene behaves like nylon, P-T-F-E costs the most, and silk has the worst tissue reaction on the whole list and comes out at seven days. Monofilament beats braided, because braided strands wick bacteria.

Full or partial thickness. In his words: Know what procedure we would do and for what procedure we might do full thickness for what other procedure we would do partial thickness. If the goal involves bone, it's full thickness: implants, augmentation, crown lengthening with osseous surgery. If the goal is soft tissue, like the bed for a free gingival graft, it's partial. And split thickness is the same thing as partial. He said so.

From Doctor Basma's lecture: vertical incisions at the line angle, never through the papilla. A flap's base wider than its free edge. And the sub-marginal incision cuts the pocket wall away, making recession on purpose to eliminate the pocket.

Session three, Doctor Geisinger. For the final, Doctor Abou-Arraj said to know McGuire and Nunn and Kwok and Caton, and, in his words: I would not worry about learning the McIntyre one. That's Miller and McEntire. Let it go.

McGuire and Nunn asks whether a tooth can be maintained, meaning kept in the mouth: good, fair, poor, questionable, hopeless. In Doctor Geisinger's words: The mistake everybody makes is they want to flip-flop poor and questionable, right? Because it feels like poor is worse. A questionable tooth doesn't stay questionable. It gets worse and becomes hopeless, or improves to poor.

Kwok and Caton asks whether stability can be achieved with treatment, for the whole dentition: favorable, questionable, unfavorable, hopeless. It can only be therapeutic. The one-line summary: only McGuire and Nunn can be diagnostic. All of them can be therapeutic.

## 3. Session four: Phase one and the instruments

Session four, Doctor Kaur. And this is where the toothbrush by my front door comes in. It has waited five episodes for this. Phase one starts with oral hygiene instruction, for every patient. Doctor Litz called it arguably the most important appointment the patient will have. We clean their teeth two to four times a year. They clean them seven hundred to eight hundred times. The toothbrush asked me to say that twice. I'm saying it once.

The disease: plaque bacteria start it, and the host drives it. In Doctor Kaur's words: Bacteria is only responsible for twenty-five percent of the damage that happens in periodontitis. Seventy-five percent of the damage that happens in periodontitis is because of those inflammatory markers and cells of bone destruction, osteoclasts, that are released by the host.

In health, probing depth isn't attachment level. The margin sits coronal to the C-E-J, so recession is negative, and you add it with its sign. A five millimeter pocket with minus three of recession is a C-A-L of two. Leave recession blank in Salud, and that C-A-L of two reads as five: a false Stage three or four.

What scaling and root planing is for: less inflammation, a shift from gram negative toward gram positive flora, and arrested progression. In her words: Sometimes the immediate response is to get rid of the pocket. That is not the rationale for doing scaling and root planing. And it doesn't grow back bone or attachment. That select-all has been tripping people up.

The Phase one evaluation, at four to six weeks. Doctor Abou-Arraj said to know what to expect there and what kind of healing, and not more detail than that. Less inflammation and bleeding, and shallower pockets, from two things: the swollen margin shrinks, which reads as more recession, and the epithelium and connective tissue reattach to the clean root. That's healing by a long junctional epithelium. Repair, not new bone. Calculus left behind: remove it and look again in two weeks. None left: more scaling won't help. Better home care will.

Now the instruments: off the midterm, on the final. In Doctor Abou-Arraj's words: Definitely all instrument slides that explain what each instrument does and where it's used. Sickle scalers have a pointed tip and two edges and work mostly above the gum: the H six seven for anteriors, the S two oh four S-D nine for posterior interproximals. ## 4. Furcations

Session five, Doctor Litz, and his most important point. In his words: Attachment loss that involves the furcation, it dramatically decreases the prognosis of the tooth. A furcation is normal anatomy, as normal as a cusp tip. A furcation defect is the disease in it.

This part is personal. I'm a lower first molar. Two roots, so two furcation entrances, buccal and lingual, on a short root trunk. Bower measured them: about two point four millimeters from the C-E-J on the buccal, and two point five on the lingual. So about two and a half millimeters of attachment loss, and you're in my furcation. Please be gentle with the Nabers probe. And every lower first molar has a concavity on its mesial root, facing the furcation, about point seven millimeters deep, every single time. Mine included. You can't see it, and you can barely clean it. It's not a flaw. It's character.

The upper first molar has three entrances. In Doctor Litz's numbers: the mesial about three point six millimeters from the C-E-J, the buccal about four point two, and the distal about four point eight. Other sources round them a little differently, but the order is the point. In Doctor Abou-Arraj's words: Like which one is closer to the C-E-J, because that can affect which one will get disease first. On the upper first molar, the mesial is closest. On me, the buccal sits just above the lingual. And if a molar probes four or five millimeters, assume a furcation is already exposed.

Don't mix up two different questions. The closest entrance to the C-E-J on an upper first molar is the mesial. The narrowest is the buccal. And width won't be asked. His email after the review said so, in his words: This will not be a question on the exam. So learn the depths, and know why narrow matters: most entrances are under a millimeter wide, narrower than the face of a curette.

Which furcations get hit most. In his words: The maxillary buccal furcation is the most commonly affected furcation, followed by the mesiopalatal and distal palatal furcations. I want you to know that. Upper molars more than lower. On lower molars, the buccal.

And the cause. In his words: The primary cause of furcation involvement is progressive clinical attachment loss that results from inflammatory periodontal disease. Plaque. Everything else only contributes: root trunk length, root form, close or fused roots, enamel pearls, overhangs.

Cervical enamel projections, which he called a definite test question, and more important than that, something to know for life. Enamel that didn't stop forming before the root did, pointing from the C-E-J toward the furcation. Nothing attaches to enamel, so it funnels plaque straight in. Grade one, the C-E-J dips toward the furcation. Grade two approaches it. Grade three enters it. In his words: Stupid, stupid easy. No attachment loss: watch it. Attachment loss: take it off, and warn the patient about thermal sensitivity. They're more common on lower molars, about thirty percent, than upper, about fifteen, and more likely on second molars. Thirty-one asked me whether he has one. Statistically, thirty-one, you're the likelier of the two of us. He's taking it well.

The rule that goes with it. In his words: Whenever you see a lone furcation involvement in an otherwise periodontally stable patient, that should prompt you as the clinician to examine for the presence of a cervical enamel projection. One deep site in a stable mouth means something on that one tooth: a projection, an overhang, leftover cement, or a vertical root fracture, which means the tooth comes out.

Glickman's classes, with the Nabers probe: one, you feel the flute but can't engage it; two, the probe catches, with bone on the other side; three, through and through; four, through and through and visible. Class two is the most amenable to regeneration, class three the hardest to treat. On a radiograph, a furcation arrow shows in about thirty to fifty-five percent of class two and three furcations. So an arrow means involvement is likely, and no arrow rules out nothing. And his rule for all of dentistry, in his words: Do your clinical examination first, arrive at a diagnosis, then look at your radiographs, use your radiographs to prove your diagnosis.

Treatment by class. Class one: scaling and root planing and the local factors. Class two: a plasty, regeneration, root resection or extraction, and the best regeneration candidates are class twos on lower molars and on the buccal of upper molars. Class three: a tunnel, root resection or extraction. The root most often resected is the distobuccal root of the upper first molar.

## 5. Resective surgery

Doctor Litz's second half, osseous surgery. First, why surgery at all. A root planing study from nineteen eighty-one found residual calculus climbing with pocket depth. In his words: In pockets measuring between three and five millimeters, the chances of failure becomes greater. And then when that pocket depth is over five millimeters, the chances of failure dominates. A flap lets you see the root.

But before anyone picks up a scalpel. In his words: Number one, this is huge. There's not a periodontist on this earth that doesn't know this, and that's ineffective plaque control. Ineffective plaque control is the first contraindication to periodontal surgery, and the re-evaluation is there to catch it. Shallow pockets and good hygiene: maintenance. Plaque everywhere: back through Phase one. Clean, but deep: a surgical candidate.

And the goal of all of it. In his words: We're trying to create an environment that the patient can keep clean on a daily basis. Surgery cures nothing by itself.

His most important slide. In his words: If the bone does not follow what that overlying gingival architecture is doing, periodontal pocketing will reoccur. Gingiva is scalloped, higher between the teeth and lower on the facial, and it heals back to that shape whatever the bone does. So the bone gets reshaped to match. Positive architecture, with the interproximal bone coronal to the facial and lingual, is the goal. Negative, or reverse, architecture, a crater between the teeth, is what you're there to fix. When getting all the way to positive would cost too much bone, he settles for flat.

Ostectomy removes supporting bone, the alveolar bone proper. Osteoplasty reshapes bone that isn't supporting the tooth. Done properly, it costs about point six millimeters of support on average, and the teeth are a little more mobile for about a year.

Two things he called the most important. The roots, in his words: The most important thing, really, really thoroughly debride these root surfaces. Without clean roots, nothing works, resective or regenerative. And the furcation bone, in his words: That is bone that we cannot touch. If we do that, we're dooming this tooth to failure. Around a molar, take bone on the facial and lingual of each root. Never in the furcation.

On behalf of my furcation: thank you, Doctor Litz.

## 6. Regeneration

Session six, Doctor Geurs. What we have is his slides and the class review guide.

Regeneration means new bone, new cementum and new periodontal ligament, over a root that disease exposed. All three. Bone fill alone isn't regeneration. When a root heals with a long junctional epithelium instead, that's repair, which is what root planing and a plain flap usually give you.

It takes four things: cells, a scaffold, growth factors, and a blood supply.

Case selection is about the defect. The review guide puts it simply: three-wall defects are the best candidates. Deeper, narrower, more walls: more attachment gain and more bone. On the slides, a defect deeper than three millimeters, with a radiographic angle of twenty-five degrees or less, does best. A one-wall defect is a risk factor for failure. And a radiograph is a flat picture of a three dimensional defect, so the walls are confirmed at surgery, with the flap up.

The toolbox. Bone grafts are named by where they come from. An autograft comes from the patient. An allograft comes from another human, like D-F-D-B-A, demineralized freeze dried bone allograft. A xenograft comes from another species, like bovine bone. And an alloplast is synthetic, like beta tricalcium phosphate. Guided tissue regeneration puts a membrane over the defect, under the flap, to keep the fast gingival epithelium and connective tissue off the root, so the ligament and bone cells get there first. Put a bone graft under the membrane, and it holds the space.

The biologics. Enamel matrix derivative is mostly amelogenins, the proteins of enamel formation and attachment formation, and it gives results similar to guided tissue regeneration. R-H P-D-G-F B-B, recombinant human platelet derived growth factor, is chemotactic and mitogenic for bone and ligament cells, and angiogenic. The review guide pairs it with beta T-C-P, an osteoconductive scaffold that holds the clot.

The decision tree starts with the patient. Uncontrolled systemic disease, tobacco and poor hygiene get fixed first. A defect under three millimeters isn't a regeneration case. A narrow three-wall defect: a bone graft, a membrane or a biologic. A wide one, or a two-wall defect: a reinforced membrane, or a graft with a membrane or with a biologic. A one-wall defect: combination therapy. High esthetic risk: avoid the membrane.

The baseline to beat is open flap debridement: pockets from about seven millimeters down to four, with about a millimeter and a half of attachment gain. The rest of that pocket reduction is recession. And only histology proves regeneration. A probe can't tell new attachment from a long junctional epithelium, and a radiograph shows bone fill, not attachment.

## 7. Implants: health, mucositis and peri-implantitis

Session seven, Doctor Kaur, and the part she told you to own. She passed it on through the class, so this is a paraphrase, not her exact words: for her part of the final, really know peri-implant health, peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis. Her clinical questions will be based on them.

Peri-implant health. No signs of inflammation. No bleeding or suppuration on gentle probing. No increase in probing depth compared with previous exams. And no bone loss beyond the initial remodeling. Notice what's missing: a three millimeter cutoff. In her words: Implants in health can have pocket depth deeper than three millimeters. Always compare to the baseline. So you capture a clinical and a radiographic baseline, or you'll have nothing to compare to.

Remodeling. In her words: Implants are allowed or expected to have about two millimeters of bone remodeling once they are put to function. About two millimeters, leveling off in the first year, is physiologic. The slide gives no number. She did. Anything beyond it is pathologic.

Peri-implant mucositis is the implant's gingivitis. Inflammation, profuse bleeding or suppuration on gentle probing, and a probing depth deeper than baseline, from swollen tissue, not from lost bone. No bone loss beyond remodeling. It's reversible, so treat it now. Don't put it on watch.

Peri-implantitis adds the one thing that sets it apart. In her words: When somebody talks about peri-implantitis and you have not yet seen the patient and you have not yet seen the x-ray of the patient, you should have a picture of true bone loss in your mind. Inflammation, bleeding or suppuration, deeper probing, and bone loss beyond the remodeling.

So here's how to read her cases. Step one: is there bleeding or pus on gentle probing? If not, and the depth matches the baseline, it's health, even at five millimeters. Step two: if there is, it's disease, so put the film beside the baseline film. No bone loss past the first year's remodeling: mucositis. Bone loss past it: peri-implantitis.

And pus. In her words: Suppuration is a symptom of active disease. Regardless of whatever your long-term plan is for that tooth or for that implant, whenever you see suppuration, you have to do something. Debride it, maybe with a local antibiotic. Never just watch it.

Bleeding, in her words: Absence of bleeding, very good indication of absence of disease, but presence of bleeding is not that great. In the study on her slide, sites that bled at four of four visits had a thirty percent chance of attachment loss. No bleeding at any of the four: one and a half percent. Radiographs, in her words: Radiographs when it comes to implant health are not for exploration. They confirm a change you found clinically. The schedule: a baseline at the final restoration, every six months in the first year, then once a year, plus whenever probing depth, bleeding or suppuration change.

Why peri-implantitis is hard to treat: the rough surface that osteoblasts love, plaque loves too. No decontamination method beats another: saline, chlorhexidine, citric acid, E-D-T-A and lasers all come out about the same. And the bone loss rarely makes the deep, narrow defects that regenerate best.

Ailing, failing, failed, from Meffert. Ailing: bone loss that has stopped, with no inflammation. Monitor it. Failing: progressive bone loss with inflammation, and not mobile. Failed: mobile. In her words: If implant body has gained mobility, that means loss of osseointegration, which nobody in this universe can reverse. The only thing left for that implant is to come out. Check first that it's the implant body moving, not the abutment or the crown.

A surviving implant is present and loaded, complications and all. A successful one has none, by Albrektsson's criteria: immobile, no radiolucency, no pain, infection or paresthesia, and a millimeter and a half of bone loss or less in the first year, then under point two of a millimeter a year.

The cause. In her words: Your answer should be bacteria, plaque, biofilm. And that's the link to periodontitis: the same pathogens, a susceptible host, and pathogens that can persist a year after the teeth come out. In her words: That is the link, that is the reason why untreated periodontitis is a contraindication for implant therapy. Treated, arrested and compliant is fine. That was her answer to the whole session.

Her number thirty-one case was an implant placed in untreated periodontitis, with no maintenance, and two years later, lost hard and soft tissue around it. My neighbor heard that one through the wall. He would like everyone to know it was a different number thirty-one.

Smoking and diabetes. Smoking is dose response: how many years, and how much a day, both count. Diabetes is control, measured by the A-one-C, not how long they've had it, and poorly controlled diabetes is a contraindication. And risk assessment happens at every stage: before placement, during osseointegration, in maintenance, and after an implant comes out.

## 8. Antibiotics

Session eight, Doctor Litz. The cause, one more time. In his words: Anytime anybody ever asks you, what causes gum disease, what causes periodontal disease? The answer is bacteria and plaque. You find that answer on your multiple choice test question and you circle it, you answer it, and you move on.

And his take-home. In his words: It is not to be used as a monotherapy in the treatment of periodontal disease. Antibiotics are always an adjunct, given after scaling and root planing, never instead of it. Three indications: an acute infection with systemic signs, like fever, malaise or swollen lymph nodes; aggressive periodontitis; and refractory periodontitis, a patient who did everything right and still loses attachment.

Why not alone? Biofilms. In his words: The only way to get rid of a biofilm comes with mechanical debridement. His dirty pot: soak it overnight, and in the morning you still have to scrub it. Rinses and water irrigation leave a biofilm in place. And a biofilm resists antibiotics three ways: a physical barrier, more beta-lactamase inside, and slower growth.

Slower growth matters because bactericidal drugs only kill dividing bacteria. Which is why, in his words: If we ever prescribe two antibiotics to be given simultaneously, they've got to be both bactericidal agents or both bacteriostatic agents, but not one from each category. Amoxicillin with metronidazole works because both are bactericidal.

Now, the doses. In class, Doctor Litz said to know his dosing chart. A week later, at the review for the final, Doctor Abou-Arraj said not to. In his words: I would not focus a whole lot on learning the dosing. This is not the pharmacology assessment that we're asking you to know. His word came later, so no doses tonight. Know when to use them, local or systemic, and which ones.

Amoxicillin is a penicillin: bactericidal, it works on the cell wall, and beta-lactamase can inactivate it. Augmentin adds clavulanic acid, which inactivates the inactivator, with more stomach upset and more cost.

Allergy. Ask what happens when they take it. An upset stomach is a side effect. Hives or trouble breathing is a true allergy: nothing from that class. About ten percent of patients truly allergic to penicillin react to cephalosporins too, so a penicillin-allergic patient who isn't sure about cephalosporins doesn't get one. Clindamycin is usually next, but not with a history of colitis, because of C diff.

Tetracyclines are bacteriostatic, on the thirty-S subunit, and useful when Aggregatibacter is the prominent pathogen. No dairy or antacids with them, stay out of the sun, and none in pregnancy or under age eight, because they stain teeth.

Macrolides are bacteriostatic, on the fifty-S subunit. In his words: Due to its high incidence of bacterial resistance, erythromycin in the treatment of periodontal disease just is not recommended. Azithromycin and clarithromycin cover anaerobes better, and clarithromycin is out in pregnancy. Macrolides also raise digoxin levels: his Van Gogh story, the halos in The Starry Night, is there so you never forget it.

Metronidazole covers obligate anaerobes, is bactericidal, and is good for acute necrotizing ulcerative gingivitis. Tell every patient two things. In his words: Number one, it can change the color of your urine. Number two, whatever you do, do not drink alcohol or you will hate life.

Warfarin: antibiotics, and metronidazole especially, can raise its effect. You can still prescribe, but you never change a medication another physician prescribed. Tell the physician, and let them decide. And nearly every antibiotic can weaken oral contraceptives.

Local delivery. Its real indication, in his words: We got one site that's starting to break down rather than bringing the patient through surgery. It's kind of a last ditch effort. One site, in a stable maintenance patient, after you debride it. Not a dozen sites, which need surgery, and not aggressive periodontitis. The three agents. PerioChip: chlorhexidine in a gelatin chip, peaking about two hours after it goes in. Atridox: a doxycycline gel, and no brushing, flossing or eating at the site for at least seven days. Arestin: minocycline microspheres, no brushing the site for twelve hours and no flossing it for ten days. You don't need the PerioChip's dimensions. He said so.

## 9. Host modulation

Doctor Litz taught this part live. In his words: It's not sufficient to induce the clinical signs of attachment loss. And know this last bullet point. It's ultimately the host response to the presence of bacteria that mediates tissue destruction. Bacteria are required, but not sufficient.

His car in the garage. In his words: Without the key, the engine isn't going to start, right? But the engine is what gets you from point A to point B. Bacteria are the key. The host response is the engine. So periodontal disease is not an autoimmune disease: nothing starts without the key. And it's a vicious cycle. Deeper pockets hold more bacteria, which bring a bigger response, more breakdown, and deeper pockets.

The cascade. The pellicle forms within minutes of a cleaning. Gram positive cocci come first, then the orange and red complexes. Complement calls in the neutrophils and releases histamine, which is the edema. Neutrophils arrive first, within twenty-four to forty-eight hours, and macrophages later. Their cytokines do the damage. His one example: I-L one, also called osteoclast activating factor. It activates osteoclasts, stimulates collagenase and turns on P-G-E two, and connective tissue and bone are lost.

Host modulation asks whether we can turn the destructive part down. His warning, in his words: You better know what you're doing before you start monkeying around with this. Turn a host response down too far, and an innocent infection can turn lethal.
