## 2. Skin histology

Skin is the body's largest organ. About two square meters, eight to ten pounds, and three millimeters thick on average. Doctor Herr called it the organ that just does everything. Barrier. Immune defense. Sensation. Temperature control. Water balance. Sweat and oil. Even vitamin D, made right there when ultraviolet light hits seven-dehydrocholesterol. Me, I'm hard, I'm white, and I have one job. I respect the ambition.

Three regions. The epidermis is on top. It's keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, and it's avascular. No blood vessels. It's fed by diffusion from below. The dermis sits underneath. It's connective tissue, mostly collagen, and it carries the vessels, the nerves, and the appendages. Then the hypodermis, which is loose connective tissue and fat. It anchors the skin and lets it slide. But technically, the hypodermis is not part of the skin.

Thick skin is found only on the palms and soles. It has a very thick stratum corneum, a stratum lucidum, and no hair. Thin skin is everywhere else, and it has hair follicles and sebaceous glands. Thick and thin describe the epidermis, not the whole skin.

Now the layers, from deep to superficial. The mnemonic is: Before Signing, Get Legal Counsel. Basale, spinosum, granulosum, lucidum, corneum.

Stratum basale is a single layer of stem cells on the basal lamina. This is where cells divide. It also holds the melanocytes and the Merkel cells.

Stratum spinosum is several layers of prickle cells, held together by desmosomes. Those spines are the desmosomes, still holding on while the cells shrink. This is home to the Langerhans cells.

Stratum granulosum has keratohyalin granules, and this is where lamellar bodies release their lipid.

Stratum lucidum is thick skin only. Pale and translucent. Doctor Herr said not to worry about it too much. So we won't.

And stratum corneum is dead, flat, keratinized cells with no nucleus, sealed in lipid. This is the water barrier.

The slide gives you a one-liner worth keeping. Basale divides. Spinosum connects. Granulosum packages. Corneum protects.

And here's a distinction that keeps coming back. Desmosomes join cell to cell. Hemidesmosomes join the basal cell to the basement membrane, along with type seventeen collagen. Doctor Herr's words: what you want to remember is that attachment to the basement membrane, via hemidesmosomes and type seventeen collagen. Hold on to that one. It returns in pathology, in the blistering diseases.

The cells. Keratinocytes are about ninety-five percent of the epidermis. He said it twice, so you should hear it twice. Ninety-five percent. Melanocytes are one to three percent, in the basale. Langerhans cells are one to three percent, in the spinosum. And Merkel cells sit in the basal layer and sense sustained light touch.

Keratinization is terminal differentiation by programmed cell death. The DNA fragments, but the cell stays intact. It loses its nucleus and organelles, and the finished cell is about eighty-five percent keratin. In the granulosum, filaggrin bundles the keratin filaments together. As the cells rise, the pH drops toward acidic. That activates proteases that chew through the desmosomes, so the surface cells flake off. He told you not to learn the names of those proteases. Just the idea. The pH falls, the desmosomes break down, the cells shed.

The water barrier comes from lamellar bodies. They're full of lipids, including ceramides, and they're secreted between the granulosum and the corneum. Think bricks and mortar. The dead cells are the bricks. The lipid is the mortar.

Melanocytes come from the neural crest. They sit in the basal layer, with long dendrites reaching up into the spinosum. They make no desmosomes with their neighbors, so they look pale. Inside melanosomes, tyrosinase converts tyrosine to dopa, and then to melanin. The melanosomes travel out to the dendrite tips and get handed over to keratinocytes. One melanocyte supplies about thirty-six keratinocytes. That's the melanin unit. Inside the keratinocyte, melanin sits over the nucleus like an umbrella, shielding the DNA from ultraviolet light.

And now the take-home point, in Doctor Herr's words. Differences in pigmentation are not due to the number of melanocytes. People have similar numbers. Darker skin makes more melanin, in larger melanosomes, that are broken down more slowly. If an answer says more melanocytes means darker skin, that's the trap.

Two pigment disorders. In albinism, the melanocytes are there, but they can't make melanin, sometimes because of tyrosinase. In piebaldism, patches of skin have no melanocytes at all. Think of a white forelock.

Langerhans cells are antigen-presenting dendritic cells from the bone marrow. When they're activated, they leave through the lymphatics and switch on the adaptive immune system. Langerhans cells and melanocytes both look dendritic. But melanocytes make pigment, and Langerhans cells present antigen. Don't swap them.

The dermis has two layers. The papillary dermis is thin and superficial. It's loose connective tissue, with types one and three collagen, capillary loops, and Meissner corpuscles. The reticular dermis is much thicker. It's dense irregular connective tissue, with coarse type one collagen and elastic fibers. It holds the hair follicle bases, the sweat glands, and the larger vessels, and it forms Langer's lines. Cut along Langer's lines, and the scar is smaller. And fibroblasts are the dermal cells that make collagen, elastin, and proteoglycans.

Two sensory receptors to keep apart. Meissner corpuscles are small and oval, up in the dermal papillae, for light touch. Pacinian corpuscles are large and onion-like, deep in the dermis and hypodermis, for pressure and vibration. Free nerve endings are the most numerous, for pain, temperature, and itch. He told you not to memorize the lettered list of nerve endings. Just Meissner versus Pacinian.

Now the glands. This comparison is high yield.

Eccrine glands are almost everywhere. They open straight onto the skin surface. They respond to heat and to emotion. And they make a watery, hypotonic sweat, because the duct pulls sodium back out.

Apocrine glands are in the armpit, the areola, and the perianal and genital skin. They open into a hair follicle. They respond to emotional and sensory stimuli, but not to heat. And here's the twist Doctor Herr told you to highlight. Apocrine glands actually secrete by merocrine secretion. The name is a leftover.

Sebaceous glands sit with the hair follicles. They secrete sebum by holocrine secretion, which means the whole cell becomes the product.

And arrector pili are smooth muscle. Cold or fear, a sympathetic signal, and you get goosebumps.

Quick skin recap. Ninety-five percent keratinocytes. Before Signing, Get Legal Counsel. Desmosomes, cell to cell. Hemidesmosomes and type seventeen collagen, cell to basement membrane. ## 4. Dermatopathology

Dermatopathology. Six questions. We'll start with vocabulary, because that's the language the questions are written in.

Gross terms first. A macule is flat. A papule is a solid bump under five millimeters, and a nodule is over five. A plaque is raised and flat-topped. A vesicle is a fluid blister under five millimeters, and a bulla is over five. A pustule is filled with pus.

Then microscopic terms. Hyperkeratosis is a thick stratum corneum. Parakeratosis is nuclei kept in the corneum, a sign of fast turnover. Acanthosis is epidermal hyperplasia. Spongiosis is edema between the epidermal cells. And acantholysis, which you'll meet in pemphigus, is epidermal cells losing their grip on each other.

Pigment. Vitiligo is autoimmune destruction of melanocytes, leaving flat, sharply bordered white patches. A freckle is more melanin, with a normal number of melanocytes. A solar lentigo, the age spot, is more melanocytes. So: freckle, more pigment. Lentigo, more cells.

A nevus, or mole, is a benign tumor of melanocytes, arranged in nests. Any change in a nevus is suspicious. Melanoma is always malignant, and the pathologist called the A-B-C-D rule the most important thing to remember. Asymmetry. Border irregularity. Color variation. Diameter over six millimeters. Under the microscope, atypical melanocytes climb up through the epidermis. That's called pagetoid spread. The depth of invasion predicts metastasis. And melanoma is the great mimicker. It can spread almost anywhere, and look like almost anything.

Benign growths. Seborrheic keratosis is the stuck-on, velvety brown lesion of middle and older age. On histology, look for keratin-filled horn cysts, which the lecturer called very specific. A sudden eruption of many seborrheic keratoses can be a sign of an internal cancer. That's the sign of Leser-Trelat. And a skin tag is just a soft polyp on a stalk.

Warts are caused by low-risk H-P-V. Under the microscope, you see koilocytes, with clear cytoplasm and a wrinkled nucleus. Know the types.

Verruca vulgaris, the common wart, mostly on the hands. H-P-V two and four.

Verruca plana, the flat wart, on the face. Three and ten.

Plantar warts, on the soles. Type one.

Condyloma acuminatum, the genital wart. Six and eleven.

And one line to remember. Verruca happens in the skin. Squamous papilloma happens in the mucosa. The oral version of a wart is a squamous papilloma.

Now sun damage. Actinic keratosis is premalignant. It's rough, like sandpaper, on sun-damaged skin. Left alone, it can become squamous cell carcinoma.

And the big comparison. Squamous cell carcinoma versus basal cell carcinoma.

Squamous cell carcinoma is the most common tumor on sun-exposed sites in older people. It's classic on the ears and the nose, because people skip sunscreen on their ears. Under the microscope, atypical squamous cells invade through the basement membrane. It can metastasize, but most are cured by resection.

Basal cell carcinoma is a pearly papule, with telangiectasias and a rolled border, on the face and the upper lip. Under the microscope, blue basaloid islands, with the cells lined up in a palisade at the edges. It's locally aggressive, but it rarely metastasizes. As the lecturer put it: basal is bad, but it's not as bad as squamous, because it doesn't go anywhere.

One dental twist. Oral squamous cell carcinoma is not a sun cancer, except on the lip. Inside the mouth, think tobacco, alcohol, and chronic trauma.

Now the inflammatory diseases. The acute ones first.

Urticaria, or hives, is wheals that come and go within hours. It's caused by I-G-E mediated mast cell degranulation, which is type one hypersensitivity. You treat it with antihistamines, or with epinephrine.

Acute eczematous dermatitis, like poison ivy, is red and oozing, then scaling. Under the microscope, the key word is spongiosis.

Erythema multiforme has target lesions. It's a hypersensitivity reaction to infections, like herpes simplex, or to drugs, like sulfonamides. The mouth is involved in a quarter to half of cases.

Then the chronic ones. Psoriasis affects one to two percent of people. Pink plaques with silvery scale, on the elbows, the knees, and the scalp. Scrape off the scale, and it bleeds in pinpoints. That's the Auspitz sign. Under the microscope, there's acanthosis, parakeratosis, and dermal papillae with vessels pushed up under a thin epidermis, which is why it bleeds. But a map-like tongue is geographic tongue, not psoriasis. Psoriasis in the mouth is extremely rare.

Lichen planus is itchy, purple, flat-topped papules, with fine white lines called Wickham striae. The mouth is involved in about seventy percent of cases, with white, lacy lesions on both cheeks. Under the microscope, a band of lymphocytes along the junction destroys the basal layer. In the mouth, bilateral and lacy favors lichen planus. An abrupt, one-sided lesion should make you think of dysplasia.

Finally, the blistering diseases. This is where desmosomes and hemidesmosomes pay off.

Pemphigus vulgaris is I-G-G against desmosomes. The split is inside the epidermis, just above the basal layer. So the blisters are flaccid, and they break easily into erosions. The basal cells left behind look like a row of tombstones. On direct immunofluorescence, you see a fishnet pattern of I-G-G between the cells. And the mouth is often involved first.

Bullous pemphigoid is I-G-G against hemidesmosomes. The split is below the epidermis, so the blisters are tense, and they don't break easily. It's a disease of the elderly. Immunofluorescence shows a linear band along the basement membrane.

Pemphigus and pemphigoid are both type two hypersensitivity.

Dermatitis herpetiformis is I-G-A, and it's linked to gluten and celiac disease. You see itchy, grouped vesicles on the elbows, knees, back, and buttocks, with I-G-A in the dermal papillae. It reverses on a gluten-free diet. And despite the name, it has nothing to do with herpes.

So, in one breath. Desmosome, pemphigus, flaccid, fishnet. Hemidesmosome, pemphigoid, tense, linear. I-G-A and gluten, dermatitis herpetiformis.

## 5. Skeletal muscle

Halfway there. Take a sip of water. Because here comes the big one. Skeletal muscle. Eleven questions.

Organization first, from the outside in. The whole muscle is wrapped in epimysium. Bundles of fibers, called fascicles, are wrapped in perimysium, which carries the vessels and nerves. Each single fiber is wrapped in endomysium. Inside the fiber are myofibrils. And inside those are the myofilaments. Thick filaments are myosin. Thin filaments are actin.

A muscle fiber is a syncytium. During development, myoblasts fuse end to end, so every fiber has many nuclei, pushed out to the edge, under the sarcolemma. Myoblasts that don't fuse become satellite cells. Hold that thought.

Now, the sarcomere. The lecturer said, and I'm quoting: I will expect you guys to know all of these different designations. So let's go slowly.

The sarcomere runs from one Z-line to the next Z-line. It's the smallest repeating unit of the muscle.

The A-band is dark. It's the full length of the thick filaments, including the parts where they overlap with actin.

The I-band is light. It holds thin filaments only.

The H-band sits in the middle of the A-band. It holds thick filaments only, with no overlap.

And the M-line is the center of the sarcomere. It's a band of proteins that holds the thick filaments in line.

So what happens during contraction? The myosin heads pull actin toward the M-line. Myosin itself doesn't travel. The lecturer was clear: what we're actually moving is actin. ## 6. Neuromuscular junction pharmacology

Neuromuscular junction pharmacology. Four questions. He told you the drugs on his summary list are the drugs that might be on questions. So let's walk that list.

Neuromuscular blockers are also called paralytics. They're used for surgery, intubation, and mechanical ventilation. And there's exactly one depolarizing blocker. Succinylcholine. Every other blocker is nondepolarizing.

Succinylcholine is a nicotinic agonist. It opens the channel, the muscle fires, and you see fasciculations. But acetylcholinesterase can't break it down, so the end plate stays depolarized. The sodium channels stay inactivated, and the muscle goes flaccid. Onset is thirty to sixty seconds, and the effect lasts under ten minutes. That speed is why it's used for intubation.

It's cleared by plasma pseudocholinesterase. And that enzyme varies from person to person. With one abnormal copy of the gene, the block can last twenty to thirty minutes. With two abnormal copies, it can last up to about eight hours. The dibucaine test checks for it.

Now the side effects. The usual effect on the heart is bradycardia. At higher doses you can see tachycardia and hypertension, but bradycardia is much more common.

Hyperkalemia. Prolonged depolarization lets potassium leak out of the cells. So you avoid succinylcholine in burns, massive trauma and crush injuries, denervation, and neuromuscular disease. That's a classic question. A patient with denervation, a dangerous potassium spike, and the culprit is succinylcholine.

There's also muscle pain from the fasciculations, and raised pressure in the eye and the stomach.

And malignant hyperthermia. In a susceptible patient, succinylcholine plus a volatile anesthetic triggers uncontrolled calcium release through the ryanodine receptor. You get rigidity, high fever, acidosis, and a racing heart. The treatment is dantrolene, which blocks the ryanodine receptor.

Notice the bridge there. The ryanodine receptor from the muscle lecture is the very same channel that dantrolene blocks. Exams love a connection like that.

Now, the nondepolarizing blockers. Rocuronium, vecuronium, and cisatracurium. The name clue is the ending. They end in curium, or curonium. The prototype, tubocurarine, came from curare, the old dart poison. These drugs are competitive antagonists at the nicotinic receptor. There are no fasciculations. And with train of four stimulation, each twitch is smaller than the one before it. That's called fade. Succinylcholine shows no fade.

Reversal. Neostigmine inhibits acetylcholinesterase. So acetylcholine builds up, and it outcompetes the blocker. Neostigmine reverses the nondepolarizers. But never succinylcholine. More acetylcholine only deepens a depolarizing block.

Neostigmine raises acetylcholine everywhere, which means bradycardia, bronchoconstriction, and gut contractions. So you give a muscarinic blocker, atropine or glycopyrrolate, first. As the anesthesiologist in his story put it, students will only forget to give it first one time.

Sugammadex is the other way to reverse. It wraps around rocuronium and vecuronium and takes them out of action. And it's only for the drugs ending in curonium. Its side effects are bradycardia, and rarely anaphylaxis. It can also interfere with hormonal birth control, so patients need a backup method for about a week.

Now spasticity. Spasticity comes from losing the descending inhibition of the motor neurons, after a stroke, a spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, or cerebral palsy. Three main drugs.

Baclofen is a gabba-B agonist. Its side effects are sedation and weakness.

Tizanidine is a central alpha two agonist. It causes less weakness than the others, so it's often tried first. The side effects are drowsiness, low blood pressure, and dry mouth. And here's the one to remember. Don't stop it suddenly, or you get rebound hypertension. Taper it.

Botulinum toxin cleaves the SNARE docking proteins, so no acetylcholine gets released. It's injected right into the muscle. It's used for cervical dystonia, excessive armpit sweating, eyelid spasm, an overactive bladder, wrinkles, and bruxism. For chronic migraine, it works a different way, by cutting the release of C-G-R-P. And botulism, the poisoning, causes descending weakness, starting with the face.

Acute muscle spasm, from an injury, is a different problem. That's cyclobenzaprine, methocarbamol, and carisoprodol. They're sedating. Cyclobenzaprine has antimuscarinic effects, so think dry mouth, urinary retention, and constipation. Carisoprodol carries a risk of dependence. And none of them help spasticity that comes from the brain or spinal cord. So for a stroke patient with spasticity, think tizanidine or baclofen. Not cyclobenzaprine.

## 7. Lightning round
