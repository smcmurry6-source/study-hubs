## 2. Signals, receptors and feedback

Doctor Galin said this about lecture two: out of all the lectures, this is one you really need to pay attention to, because it lays the foundation for all of the specific gland lectures. So we start with the rules every gland follows.

Four ways to signal. Endocrine: the hormone enters the blood and acts far away, on a high-affinity receptor. Paracrine: it acts on a neighboring cell, without entering the blood. Autocrine: it acts back on the cell that made it. And neuroendocrine: neurons make it, and release it into the blood. The hypothalamus is brain. The pituitary is not. So neuroendocrine almost always means the hypothalamic releasing hormones, or oxytocin and A-D-H, which hypothalamic neurons release from their axon endings. And the paracrine pair from the review: somatostatin and insulin, side by side in the islet.

Endocrine glands have a signature. Ductless, because the duct cells disappear during development. Wrapped in fenestrated capillaries, because everything they make has to reach the blood. And full of endoplasmic reticulum.

Three kinds of hormone. Proteins and peptides, like G-H, A-C-T-H, insulin, and every anterior pituitary hormone. They're cut from a big preprohormone, stored in vesicles, and released when calcium rises inside the cell. Doctor Galin's line: calcium is the key to secretion. They bind receptors on the cell surface, and travel mostly free in the blood.

Amines are made from tyrosine. Epinephrine and norepinephrine bind surface receptors. Thyroid hormone is the odd amine: its receptor is inside the cell, and it travels bound to thyroxine binding globulin.

Steroids are made from cholesterol, which arrives as L-D-L. They aren't stored, and they aren't made continuously. They're made when they're needed, and they diffuse straight out. They ride the blood bound to proteins, which gives them a longer half-life, and they bind receptors inside the cell that change gene transcription. That's slow, hours to days, because a new protein has to be built.

Now the table Doctor Herr said to commit to memory. Three G proteins.

G-S, stimulatory, turns on adenylyl cyclase. Cyclic A-M-P goes up, protein kinase A turns on, and calcium channels in the cell membrane open. Calcium comes in from outside the cell, and the hormone is secreted.

G-Q also stimulates. It turns on phospholipase C, which makes I-P-three and diacylglycerol. Calcium rises again, but this time it's released from stores inside the cell, from the E-R. Same result: secretion.

G-I, inhibitory, blocks both routes. Calcium doesn't rise, and nothing is secreted.

Two more receptors to tell apart. The insulin receptor is a receptor tyrosine kinase: the kinase is part of the receptor. The growth hormone receptor has no kinase of its own. It borrows one, JAK two, and starts the JAK-STAT pathway.

Feedback. Doctor Galin calls negative feedback the hallmark of endocrinology: a hormone's effects shut off its own secretion. It can be axis-driven: hypothalamus, then pituitary, then the gland, and the gland's hormone feeds back on the top two. Or response-driven, with no pituitary at all: low glucose turns glucagon on, and as glucose rises, glucagon turns off. A short loop is a pituitary hormone shutting off its own releasing hormone. A long loop is the gland's hormone feeding back on the pituitary and hypothalamus. And there's one positive loop to know: just before ovulation, estrogen drives the L-H surge.

Last, primary versus secondary. Primary means the problem is the gland itself. Secondary, the pituitary. Tertiary, the hypothalamus. Compare the gland's hormone to the pituitary hormone. If they move in opposite directions, it's primary. If they move the same way, it's secondary. Doctor Galin's example: very high cortisol with low A-C-T-H. Opposite directions. Primary. The adrenal is overproducing, and the cortisol is correctly shutting down the pituitary.

## 3. The pituitary

The pituitary is also called the hypophysis. Same gland. Doctor Herr will call it the pituitary, but other exams may say hypophysis. It hangs from the hypothalamus by a stalk, in the sella turcica, behind the optic chiasm. It's the size of an English pea, and your life depends on it.

Two lobes, and their origins explain everything. The anterior lobe, the adenohypophysis, is epithelium. It grows up from the roof of the primitive mouth, as Rathke's pouch. Its parts are the pars distalis, the pars tuberalis, and the pars intermedia. It stains darker, because it's packed with hormone-making cells.

The posterior lobe, the neurohypophysis, is neural: a down-growth of the hypothalamus itself, the pars nervosa and the infundibulum. Doctor Herr: the infundibulum is connected to the hypothalamus, and that's really important. It stains lighter, because it's mostly axons.

The anterior lobe makes six hormones. Flat pig. F-S-H, L-H, A-C-T-H, T-S-H, prolactin, G-H. The first four letters, F, L, A and T, come from basophils: the gonadotrophs, corticotrophs and thyrotrophs. P and G, prolactin and growth hormone, come from acidophils: the somatotrophs and lactotrophs. And chromophobes barely stain, because they hold little or no hormone.

Each cell has a stimulator and a brake. Somatotrophs: G-H-R-H on, somatostatin off. Lactotrophs: prolactin releasing hormone and oxytocin on, dopamine off. Thyrotrophs: T-R-H on, thyroid hormone off. Gonadotrophs: G-N-R-H on, progesterone and estradiol off. Corticotrophs: C-R-H on, high cortisol off.

The hypothalamus doesn't reach the anterior lobe by nerves. Its releasing hormones enter a capillary bed at the median eminence, portal veins carry them down, and they bathe the anterior lobe's cells in a second capillary bed. That's the hypothalamo-hypophyseal portal system.

Prolactin is the one pituitary hormone held down all the time, by dopamine. Doctor Galin: if it wasn't being chronically suppressed in each of us right now, we would all be sitting here lactating.

A-C-T-H is cut from a bigger precursor, P-O-M-C. The same cut releases beta endorphin, and alpha M-S-H, melanocyte stimulating hormone. Hold onto that. It comes back in Addison's disease.

Releasing hormones come in pulses. Every time a hormone binds, its receptor is pulled inside the cell for a while. Pulses give the receptors time to come back, so the pituitary stays ready for action. Give a releasing hormone continuously, and the pituitary actually shuts down.

Now the posterior lobe. It is not endocrine tissue at all. It's storage. Neurons in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei make A-D-H, which is the same thing as vasopressin, and oxytocin. Their axons run all the way down the stalk, and the hormone piles up in swellings along them, called Herring bodies. The glial cells around those axons are pituicytes, the gatekeepers. When the hormone is released, they pull back from the capillaries and let it through.

So Doctor Galin's test question, in his words: what hormones are synthesized by the posterior pituitary? None. They're all made in the hypothalamus.

## 4. Growth

Lecture three. The slides are Doctor Galin's, and the recording is Doctor Madison giving the lecture. Its two pointers: know the feedback loops, really important as far as the exam is concerned, and know the summary slide of what G-H does in each tissue.

Growth hormone, or somatotropin, is a peptide from the somatotrophs. It's species-specific: only human G-H works in people. It comes out in pulses, and most of the day's output comes during sleep. The biggest, most predictable pulse comes soon after you fall asleep. So sleep. That's not just advice. That's endocrinology.

What turns it on: G-H-R-H, through a G-S receptor. Cyclic A-M-P, calcium in, G-H out. What turns it off: somatostatin, through a G-I receptor. Potassium channels open, the cell hyperpolarizes, no calcium comes in, no G-H. The slides call them the yin and yang of growth hormone. And from the review: removing somatostatin's brake isn't enough by itself. G-H-R-H has to drive the release.

Ghrelin from the gut raises G-H. So do sleep, stress, and the sex steroids at puberty. Lowering it: somatostatin, I-G-F one, G-H itself, and high glucose and free fatty acids, which raise somatostatin. Every loop is negative. I-G-F one acts on the pituitary directly, and also cuts G-H-R-H and raises somatostatin in the hypothalamus. In the lecture's words: the big one is this somatostatin production.

How G-H works. Through JAK-STAT, it makes the liver produce I-G-F one, insulin-like growth factor one, once called somatomedin C. That's the somatomedin hypothesis: in a dish, G-H does nothing to cartilage. It needs a factor in the serum, and that factor is I-G-F one.

At the growth plate, the jobs are split. G-H tells the quiet reserve cells to commit, and become chondrocytes. I-G-F one makes them proliferate, and that proliferation is what lengthens the bone. So, from the review: G-H promotes linear growth in childhood mostly through I-G-F one.

Then the tissue summary. Muscle: more amino acid uptake and protein synthesis, less glucose uptake. Liver: gluconeogenesis, protein synthesis, and I-G-F one. Fat: less glucose uptake, more lipolysis. More muscle, less fat, and higher blood glucose. Most of G-H's metabolic actions oppose insulin, so G-H is diabetogenic. People who make too much of it are more likely to develop diabetes.

The other growth hormones. Thyroid hormone is required for growth. Sex steroids boost G-H at puberty, and estrogen closes the growth plates. And G-H is not important for fetal growth. Insulin is, which is why a mother with diabetes tends to have a bigger baby.

When it goes wrong. A short child with normal G-H but low I-G-F one has a G-H receptor defect: the hormone is there, and the liver can't answer it. Too much G-H before the growth plates have closed gives gigantism, and over time, high blood glucose. After they close, acromegaly. Bones can't lengthen, so they thicken: a heavy brow ridge, a heavy lower jaw, big hands and feet, enlarged organs, and often diabetes. Test with I-G-F one and a glucose tolerance test. Treat with surgery, or octreotide, a somatostatin agonist, or bromocriptine, a dopamine agonist.

A quick report from next door. Number thirty-one heard the word acromegaly and asked me if his jaw looks heavy. Thirty-one. You don't have a jaw. You live in one.

And the somatopause. G-H falls from about our thirties. The pituitary can still secrete it. It's the hypothalamus, and its G-H-R-H, that fades.

## 5. The thyroid: made outside the cell

Doctor Herr's histology first. The thyroid sits below the cricoid cartilage: two lateral lobes, joined by an isthmus. Its unit is the follicle, a sphere of simple cuboidal follicular cells around colloid. Colloid is thyroglobulin, the storage form of the hormone. The C cells, or parafollicular cells, sit at the edge of the follicle, inside the basal lamina, but not touching the colloid. They make calcitonin, and that belongs to the calcium lecture.

Now Doctor Galin. Thyroid physiology is your biggest lecture: eight questions, and all four Patient Box questions are among them. Most of it is biosynthesis, because, in his words, thyroid hormone is the only endocrine hormone that's synthesized extracellularly. It's made out in the colloid. And his key word for the exam: metabolism. If a question talks about whole-body metabolism, or metabolic rate, or heart rate going up, start thinking thyroid.

The chemistry. Thyroid hormones are amines, built on tyrosine. One iodine on a tyrosine makes M-I-T. Two make D-I-T. M-I-T plus D-I-T is T-three. D-I-T plus D-I-T is T-four, thyroxine. The gland releases mostly T-four, about ninety percent, but T-three is the active one. In the tissues, five prime deiodinase, working on the outer ring, turns T-four into T-three. Plain five deiodinase, on the inner ring, makes reverse T-three, which is inactive. That's how the body protects itself from too much active hormone. Doctor Galin: the main thing is to remember the difference between five prime deiodinase and five deiodinase.

Now be prepared to follow the steps. There are seven. The basolateral side of the cell faces the blood. The apical side faces the colloid.

One. Iodide trapping. The sodium iodide symporter, N-I-S, on the basolateral membrane, brings sodium and iodide in together. Sodium runs down its gradient and drags iodide up its gradient. Secondary active transport. The cell concentrates iodide twenty to fifty times above plasma, on the slides.

Two. At the same time, the cell makes thyroglobulin, a huge protein full of tyrosines, and secretes it into the colloid.

Three. Iodide to iodine. Iodide crosses the apical membrane through pendrin, and thyroperoxidase oxidizes it to iodine. That needs hydrogen peroxide. And it goes in alphabetical order: iodide, then iodine.

Four. Organification and coupling. Thyroperoxidase puts iodine onto thyroglobulin's tyrosines, making M-I-T and D-I-T, then couples them into T-three and T-four, still attached to thyroglobulin. One enzyme, three jobs. So, from the review, what does thyroperoxidase do? Iodination of thyroglobulin. Not hydrolysis, not release, and not T-four to T-three. That last one is deiodinase.

Five. Endocytosis. The cell takes the colloid back in, through megalin, the thyroglobulin receptor on the apical surface.

Six. Proteolysis. The vesicle fuses with a lysosome, and thyroglobulin is digested. T-four and T-three come free.

Seven. Secretion and recycling. T-three and T-four leave for the blood. The leftover M-I-T and D-I-T are deiodinated, and their iodine is recycled.

So the proteins, in order: N-I-S in, pendrin across, thyroperoxidase in the colloid, megalin back in. And the only time T-three and T-four are inside the cell is at the very end, as they're cut free.

Last, autoregulation. Low iodine speeds up trapping. A very large iodine load blocks organification: the Wolff-Chaikoff effect. It's short-lived. The gland escapes by trapping less iodide, and then makes hormone again.

## 6. The thyroid axis, and what goes wrong

The axis. T-R-H binds a G-Q receptor on the thyrotrophs. T-S-H binds a G-S receptor on the follicular cell. Doctor Galin: we're not going to ask you to know every receptor for every hormone, but understand the differences between a G-Q, a G-S, and a G-I. So: T-R-H, G-Q. T-S-H, G-S.

T-S-H does two jobs. Growth: bigger cells, more cells, new follicles. That's how a goiter forms. And synthesis: it turns up every step, from N-I-S to megalin, including one he called out. T-S-H drives glucose oxidation and N-A-D-P-H, which make the hydrogen peroxide thyroperoxidase needs.

Feedback: T-three and T-four shut down T-S-H and T-R-H, and T-three is the main brake on T-S-H. The hypothalamus also reads the environment. Cold and eating raise thyroid hormone. Heat and fasting lower it.

What thyroid hormone does. It binds a nuclear receptor and changes transcription. It raises the basal metabolic rate by making more sodium potassium A-T-P-ase pumps: more oxygen used, more heat. It makes the heart beat faster and harder, with more beta one receptors and a switch to the stronger alpha myosin heavy chain. It keeps L-D-L receptors up, so a hypothyroid patient has very high cholesterol. He called that a board-type question. It's required for brain development around birth. And it works with G-H on bone in children.

And the one he ranked first: the most important thing that thyroid hormone does is increase beta adrenergic receptor density, essentially all over the body. Thyroid hormone turns up the volume on the sympathetic system.

From the review: which hormone is anabolic on muscle protein at normal levels, but catabolic at very high levels? Thyroid hormone. At normal levels it supports protein synthesis and growth. In excess, breakdown wins, and hyperthyroid patients lose weight fast.

Now disease. Primary hyperthyroidism: high T-four and T-three, low T-S-H. Most often Graves disease, where antibodies bind and activate the T-S-H receptor, acting like T-S-H. The pituitary is fine, so feedback drives T-S-H down. So in Graves, what's decreased in the blood? T-S-H. The signs follow the hormone. Weight loss despite eating more. Heat intolerance and sweating. A fast heart, tremor, nervousness. A goiter. And exophthalmos, because the antibodies cross-react with the tissue behind the eye.

Primary hypothyroidism: low T-four and T-three, high T-S-H. Most often Hashimoto's disease, an autoimmune destruction of the gland. Cold intolerance. Lethargy and slow thinking. A slow heart. Myxedema, a puffy swelling of the skin. And that high cholesterol: a patient whose cholesterol suddenly climbs, and doesn't respond to a statin, should have their thyroid checked. And if T-S-H and T-four are both low, the pituitary is the problem. That's secondary.

Number thirty-one now wears a tiny scarf. He says he's cold intolerant. Thirty-one, it's a mouth. It's ninety-eight point six degrees in here.

Why a goiter can come with too much hormone or too little: T-S-H is a growth signal. In Hashimoto's or iodine deficiency, hormone is low and T-S-H is high, so the gland keeps being told to grow. In Graves, the antibodies do the same thing through the T-S-H receptor.

And children. Hypothyroidism before birth or in childhood causes marked growth delay and severe intellectual disability: congenital hypothyroidism. The classic cause is too little iodine during pregnancy. The signs include an enlarged tongue, a goiter, poor growth, and delayed bones. Caught early, replacement can work wonders. That's why newborns and children get their thyroid checked. Doctor Galin's take-home list: the biosynthesis, good bullet points for hyper and hypo, and why we measure thyroid function in children.

For the Patient Box questions: read T-S-H against T-four first. Opposite directions, primary. Then let the signs tell you which way.

## 7. Thyroid drugs

Doctor Fasinu's thyroid drugs. Five questions. And he told you there is an incentive to ask you questions around the thyroid, because so many people's thyroid problems get picked up in a dental office. A goiter on the neck exam. An enlarged tongue. A tremor.

The labs. T-S-H moves opposite to the hormone. High T-S-H with low free T-four: hypothyroid. Low T-S-H with high free T-four: hyperthyroid. And a patient on levothyroxine whose T-S-H is still high isn't getting enough, so you increase the dose. The T-S-H level, he said, will always give you an indication if they are getting enough.

For hypothyroidism, the drug of choice is levothyroxine. That's T-four. Why T-four, and not the active T-three? Because the body regulates how much T-four it converts, so the supply stays steady. What he wants you to know: take it first thing in the morning, on an empty stomach, because it binds almost anything. A long half-life, six to ten days, so steady state takes six to eight weeks. And a narrow therapeutic index, so you monitor T-S-H and adjust. Its side effects are simply the signs of hyperthyroidism.

Liothyronine is synthetic T-three: a short half-life, swinging levels, and more risk of overdose and heart toxicity. Desiccated thyroid, dried pig thyroid, carries too much T-three relative to us, so its potency is unpredictable. Neither beats levothyroxine.

Interactions. Calcium, aluminum, magnesium, and binders like cholestyramine lower its absorption. And thyroid hormone raises warfarin's effect, so the I-N-R and the bleeding risk go up.

The drug-induced one to remember: amiodarone. The I-O-D in amiodarone is iodine, and it can cause hypo or hyperthyroidism.

For hyperthyroidism, methimazole and propylthiouracil, P-T-U. Both block synthesis: they stop iodine getting onto thyroglobulin, and they stop coupling. Both can injure the liver, and both can cause agranulocytosis. Methimazole is first line for most patients: once a day, lower liver risk. But it's teratogenic, especially in the first trimester. P-T-U carries a boxed warning for the liver, but it's the drug in the first trimester of pregnancy, and often first in thyroid storm, because it also blocks T-four to T-three conversion in the tissues. In his words, there's a reason the question says this person is non-pregnant. Non-pregnant Graves patient: methimazole. Positive H-C-G, first trimester: P-T-U.

Radioactive iodine, iodine one thirty-one, is trapped by the thyroid and destroys it. Never in pregnancy, and watch for the patient swinging into hypothyroidism. Lugol's iodine blocks hormone release, before surgery and in thyroid storm, but it isn't first line. Beta-blockers, like propranolol, control the tremor and the racing heart, but don't treat the disease. And dexamethasone cuts T-four to T-three conversion in thyroid storm.

## 8. The endocrine pancreas

Doctor Madison's pancreas, with Doctor Herr's islets from histology two. Big picture, not every enzyme. Doctor Madison: big picture, it's important to know what insulin is doing in the body.

The islets of Langerhans. Doctor Herr's number, the thing to remember: they're only one to two percent of the pancreas's volume, mostly in the tail. Pale clusters with no ducts, wrapped in fenestrated capillaries. Beta cells are the most abundant, about sixty-five to seventy percent, making insulin. Alpha cells are second, about fifteen to twenty percent, making glucagon. Delta cells, five to ten percent, make somatostatin. Then a few P-P cells, with pancreatic polypeptide, and epsilon cells, with ghrelin. Just know those two exist.

Blood flows from the center of the islet outward. Beta cells sit in the center, alpha and delta cells at the edge, so each cell's product washes over its neighbors. Insulin inhibits its own release, autocrine, and glucagon's, paracrine. Somatostatin brakes both.

Insulin is made like a peptide: preproinsulin, then proinsulin in the E-R, then the C-peptide is cut out in the Golgi and the granules. Insulin and C-peptide come out one to one. But the liver clears about sixty percent of insulin on its first pass, and it leaves C-peptide alone. So C-peptide is the marker of how much insulin the beta cells are really making.

The sequence. Glucose enters the beta cell through GLUT two. Glycolysis raises A-T-P. A-T-P closes the A-T-P-sensitive potassium channels. Potassium stays in, so the cell depolarizes. Voltage-gated calcium channels open, calcium comes in, and the insulin vesicles fuse and release. Sulfonylureas shut those same potassium channels. Amino acids, glucagon, acetylcholine, and the incretins, G-L-P one and G-I-P, boost insulin too. Norepinephrine, fight or flight, holds it back.

The insulin receptor is a tyrosine kinase. It phosphorylates insulin receptor substrates, and within seconds, GLUT four moves to the membrane of muscle and fat. The liver doesn't use GLUT four.

Insulin's message: burn it, or store it. In the liver: make glycogen and fat, and stop gluconeogenesis and ketone production. In fat: take up glucose, build triglyceride, stop lipolysis. In muscle: take up glucose through GLUT four, store glycogen, and build protein. So the review's muscle-specific effect of insulin: glucose uptake through GLUT four, stored as glycogen. Breaking down muscle protein is cortisol. Gluconeogenesis belongs to the liver.

Glucagon is the opposite, for between meals. A peptide from alpha cells, through a G-S receptor, with a half-life of three to four minutes. It goes into the portal vein, so its main target is the liver. Muscle has no glucagon receptors. In the liver it drives glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis and ketogenesis. Falling glucose is its main trigger, and amino acids and epinephrine raise it too. The review had a twist: high insulin, in someone who responds to it, stimulates glucagon. Not directly. The insulin drops the glucose, and the falling glucose calls the glucagon.

Somatostatin, from delta cells, through a G-I receptor, is the paracrine brake on both insulin and glucagon. Rising glucose and fuel from a meal stimulate it, and in the gut it slows motility and absorption.

Diabetes. Type one: the beta cells are destroyed, usually by autoimmunity, and the patient needs insulin. Type two: tissue resistance, with beta cells overworking until they wear out. Glucose spills into the urine once it exceeds the kidney's transport maximum. Then polyuria from osmotic diuresis, polydipsia, and polyphagia. The complications are vascular: retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy and heart disease. For type two: metformin first, which suppresses the liver's glucose output. Then sulfonylureas, thiazolidinediones, S-G-L-T two inhibitors, G-L-P one agonists, and D-P-P four inhibitors.

## 9. The adrenal gland

The adrenal, from Doctor Madison's lecture and Doctor Herr's histology. It sits on the kidney like a little hat. Small, but with one of the biggest blood supplies per gram in the body. Three arteries feed it: the superior suprarenal, from the inferior phrenic; the middle, straight off the aorta; and the inferior, from the renal artery.

And now, in Doctor Herr's words: here's the question that's going to be on your exam. The medulla has a dual blood supply. Arterial blood, from medullary arterioles that run straight through the cortex. And venous blood, from the cortical sinusoids, which has already passed through the cortex and picked up cortisol. Blood flows from the cortex to the medulla. That one fact explains the medulla.

The cortex is about ninety percent of the gland, and it makes steroids from cholesterol, mostly taken up as L-D-L. Three zones, from the outside in.

The zona glomerulosa, about fifteen percent of the cortex, makes aldosterone. It's the only zone with aldosterone synthase.

The zona fasciculata, the biggest, sixty-five to eighty percent, makes cortisol. Its cells are full of lipid droplets, so they're called spongiocytes. Doctor Herr: the thing to remember for the exam, for the zona fasciculata, is that name.

The zona reticularis, about ten percent, makes weak androgens: D-H-E-A and androstenedione.

All three zones answer to A-C-T-H. The glomerulosa also answers to angiotensin two, its main control.

Doctor Madison's two lightning bolts. One: the first step for every steroid, cholesterol to pregnenolone, by cholesterol desmolase. A-C-T-H drives it, and it's the rate-limiting step for cortisol. Two: aldosterone synthase, the control point for aldosterone.

Aldosterone. Its main regulator is angiotensin two, through a G-Q receptor: low pressure, renin, angiotensin one, A-C-E in the lung, angiotensin two. High potassium drives it too, by depolarizing the glomerulosa cell. A-C-T-H only nudges it. In the kidney's principal cells, aldosterone adds sodium channels and sodium potassium pumps. Keep sodium, water follows, and pressure rises. Lose potassium into the urine. So too much aldosterone: hypertension, low potassium, alkalosis. Too little: salt craving, low sodium, high potassium, acidosis, low blood pressure. And the kidney uses eleven beta hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase to turn cortisol into cortisone, so cortisol can't act like aldosterone.

Cortisol. C-R-H, then A-C-T-H through a G-S receptor, then cortisol, which feeds back. A-C-T-H is cortisol's primary regulator. Not angiotensin, not renin. Cortisol peaks around seven to eight in the morning, right at waking. Its metabolic goal is to raise blood glucose, for the brain under stress: gluconeogenesis in the liver, less glucose uptake in muscle and fat, muscle protein broken down for amino acids, and lipolysis in the limbs. And it's a powerful immune suppressant. That's why steroids treat inflammation, and why they mask infection.

The medulla is a modified sympathetic ganglion. ## 11. Calcium and phosphate

Last lecture: Doctor Galin on calcium and phosphate. Six questions. His framing: the hormones all act in the same places. Your job is just to remember which is acting where, and what they're doing to calcium and phosphate.

Why the range is so narrow. Calcium sets how easily nerves fire. Low calcium makes them too excitable: tetany, seizures, and two bedside signs. Trousseau's: inflate a blood pressure cuff, and the hand spasms. Chvostek's: tap over the facial nerve, and the face twitches. High calcium does the opposite: lethargy and weakness.

About half the calcium in plasma is ionized, and that's the part the hormones control. The rest is bound to protein, or complexed. And the hormones act at three sites: the gut, the bone, and the kidney.

Now, he said, put a star by this. Which bone cell has which receptor. Osteoblasts, the builders, carry the receptors for P-T-H, vitamin D and estrogen. Osteoclasts, the breakers, carry the receptor for calcitonin. Yes, P-T-H breaks bone down, but its receptor is on the osteoblast. The osteoblast then signals the osteoclasts next door, with RANK ligand among other factors. Osteoclasts come from the blood cell lineage, like macrophages.

The chief cell watches blood calcium with the calcium-sensing receptor, a G-Q receptor. And here's the paradox. When blood calcium is high, the receptor fires, calcium rises inside the cell, and that rise blocks secretion. No P-T-H. When blood calcium is low, the receptor goes quiet, and P-T-H is released. What P-T-H does. In bone, through the osteoblasts, it breaks bone down. In the kidney, it keeps calcium, and dumps phosphate. And it turns on one alpha hydroxylase, so the gut absorbs more calcium. Net effect: calcium up, phosphate down.

That kidney step is the star. Everywhere else, calcium and phosphate move together. Under P-T-H, in the kidney, they split, because calcium and phosphate meeting in a tubule make a stone.
