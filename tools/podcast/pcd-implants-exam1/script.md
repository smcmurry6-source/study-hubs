## 2. The seven types of implants

Lecture one, Doctor Mueninghoff. He has been at the school about thirty-eight years, and he has treated patients with every implant in this lecture. He was very clear about what matters. In his words: There are seven. And those are the things that you've got to remember what they are. And out of this entire lecture, that's something that you really need to know.

And again: As far as any kind of exam is concerned, you have to recognize the types of implants. We don't worry about the other things.

So, the seven, and how to recognize each one on a radiograph or a photo. The easiest way to hold them is by where they sit.

On the bone. The subperiosteal implant. A metal lattice draped over the ridge, under the periosteum, with posts coming through the mucosa. Two surgeries, and when they failed, they destroyed a lot of bone. His words: when they went south, they really went south.

Through a tooth. The endodontic stabilizer. A long rod run through a root canal and out past the apex into the bone, to steady a weak tooth. On a film, it's a long pin poking out of a treated root. They failed because they were stainless steel, not titanium, and the seal where the rod left the root was poor.

Through the bone. The transosseous, or transosteal, implant. A plate under the chin, with posts coming up through the full thickness of the anterior mandible, joined by a bar: the transmandibular implant. Surgery through the chin, under general anesthesia, and only in the anterior mandible.

In the mucosa. The intramucosal insert. Little buttons on the tissue side of a denture, usually a palateless upper, that snap into holes in the mucosa. The catch: if the patient left the denture out for more than a few hours, the holes started to close.

And in the bone. Three of these. The blade, or plate form: a thin flat blade, often with holes, set into a slot in the bone, and usually tied to a natural tooth, with fibrous tissue between the metal and the bone. The mandibular ramus frame: one piece, running from the symphysis back into both rami, with a bar over the ridge. Big implants fail big. And the root form: the screw-shaped titanium implant. The standard today.

So: on the bone, subperiosteal. Through a tooth, endodontic stabilizer. Through the bone, transosteal. In the mucosa, intramucosal insert. In the bone, blade, ramus frame and root form.

Why the early metals failed: gold and platinum form very little surface oxide. Titanium is covered in titanium oxide, a ceramic, and bone integrates with that oxide, not with the metal.

And the old standard of success, before Brånemark: still in service at five years, in seventy-five percent of patients.

What you don't need. In his words: The advantages and disadvantages of the root form, don't worry about that. Recognition. The stories are context.

## 3. Osseointegration and titanium

Lecture two, Doctor Chavali, and the start of the hint list.

Osseointegration was found by accident, by an orthopedic surgeon. In nineteen fifty-two, Per-Ingvar Brånemark put titanium chambers into rabbit bone, and when he went to take them out, they wouldn't come. The bone had grown onto the titanium.

A rabbit. Of course it was a rabbit. Rabbits have teeth that never stop growing. I find that a little showy. Some of us got one set of roots and made it work.

His slide's definition of osseointegration: the apparent direct attachment or connection of osseous tissue to an inert, alloplastic material without intervening connective tissue.

Primary and secondary stability. The surgeon drills an osteotomy with drills that get bigger, one after another. The implant is a little wider than the last drill, and bone is slightly elastic, so the implant locks in mechanically, like a screw in a wall. That's primary stability. Then the bone remodels. Cells take away some of the bone that was there at surgery and lay down new bone against the implant. That's secondary stability, and that's osseointegration proper.

From about day seven, osteoclasts resorb the bone edges, so primary stability drops, while woven bone forms on the implant, and secondary stability rises. He won't ask you which cell is which. The drop in stability is what to know.

How long to wait. The mandible is dense, the maxilla less so. Solid oak versus styrofoam. The traditional wait in the clinic: about four months in the mandible, and six in the maxilla.

One stage or two. In his words: I'll ask you a question about single-stage surgery and two-stage surgery.

Two-stage is Brånemark's. The implant goes in, a cover screw goes on, and the flap is closed over it. The implant is submerged, cut off from the mouth, for three to six months. Then a second surgery uncovers it, and a healing abutment goes on.

One-stage is Schroeder's. The healing abutment goes on at placement, so the implant sticks out through the tissue from day one. Transmucosal healing, three to four months, and no second surgery.

Either way, the tissue needs at least three weeks to heal around the healing abutment before the final impression. Who gets which? Two-stage when healing is a worry, like a diabetic or a smoker: seal it in and let it heal in a clean environment. One-stage for a young, healthy patient with good hygiene, good primary stability, and good keratinized tissue.

Titanium's surface grabs oxygen and forms a tough, passive oxide layer, and that oxide is why it doesn't corrode and why bone likes it.

Another line he flagged. In his words: I'm going to ask you this question, if it's the grading of different titanium.

Commercially pure titanium comes in four grades, and strength rises with the grade. Modern implants are grade four. Stronger than all four grades is the alloy, titanium six aluminum four vanadium.

Which system uses which. Straumann and Nobel Biocare: grade four commercially pure titanium. BioHorizons: the alloy.

Shape and surface. A machined, threaded screw beats the old smooth cylinder: more primary stability, better integration, better survival. Then a surface treatment roughens it, so bone integrates faster.

Two families of treatment. Additive puts something on: titanium plasma spray, a hydroxyapatite coating, or bonded microspheres. Subtractive takes something away: acid etching, and blasting with particles.

And in his words: Every implant system has their own surface treatment. And I want you to remember this. So this will also be on my exam.

Nobel Biocare: Ti Unite. Straumann: S-L-A, sandblasted, large grit, acid etched. BioHorizons: R-B-T, resorbable blast texturing.

Here's a hook. BioHorizons is the odd one out, all through this course. The alloy, not grade four. R-B-T. A screw torque of thirty, not thirty-five. And the platform trap, coming up in Doctor Chen's lecture.

## 4. An implant is not a tooth

Still Doctor Chavali. An implant behaves like an ankylosed tooth, with no periodontium. On the hint list: periodontal attachment versus peri-implant attachment. Here's the comparison.

Around a tooth, the junctional epithelium attaches to enamel, with hemidesmosomes along its whole length. Around an implant, it attaches to titanium, with hemidesmosomes only in the apical third.

Around a tooth, the junctional epithelium comes from the reduced enamel epithelium. Around an implant, it grows in from the neighboring oral epithelium.

Around a tooth, twelve groups of fibers run perpendicular to the root, and Sharpey's fibers insert into cementum and bone. Around an implant, two groups, circular and parallel, and not one of them inserts into the implant.

Around a tooth, more fibroblasts and less collagen. Around an implant, more collagen and fewer fibroblasts. Scar-like.

The biologic width. A tooth: about two point zero four to two point nine one millimeters. An implant: three point zero eight millimeters on average, about a millimeter more.

And there's no periodontal ligament. So no proprioception: the patient can't feel how hard they're biting on an implant. That's why its crown gets lighter contact. Hold that for the biomechanics lecture.

Now the line he was most sure about. In his words: How do you diagnose a failing implant? And this will be definitely on your quiz or exam.

The signs on his slide: mobility, percussion pain, infection, a radiolucency around the implant, and bone loss. And mobility means the implant is doomed.

His health scale. Success: no pain, no mobility, less than two millimeters of bone lost since surgery, no history of exudate. Satisfactory survival: two to four millimeters of bone loss, still no pain or mobility. Compromised survival: more than four millimeters, but less than half the implant. And failure is any one of these: pain on function, mobility, bone loss past half the implant's length, uncontrolled exudate, or an implant that's no longer in the mouth.

A report from next door. Number thirty-one heard this part, and spent the whole evening tapping himself with a spoon, checking for percussion pain. Then he asked me whether he looked radiolucent. Thirty-one. You can't fail osseointegration. You were never osseointegrated. You have a ligament. You're supposed to move a little.

One practical rule for you, as the restoring dentist. Take a radiograph before you start the crown. Don't take on an implant that's already losing bone.

And the classification of implants by where they sit, from his slides. Endosteal, in the bone, crossing only one cortical plate: the root form, the blade and the ramus frame. Eposteal, on the bone: the subperiosteal frame. Transosteal, through both cortical plates: the transmandibular implant. His tricky question: a blade is endosseous. Not transosteal. Not subperiosteal.

## 5. Biomechanics

Lecture three, Doctor Chavali again, built on Misch's textbook.

Wolff's law: the internal architecture of bone remodels according to the loads on it. Too little load, and bone wastes away, which is why the ridge resorbs after a tooth is lost. Too much, and it breaks down. In between is the adaptive window.

And a definition he asked you to keep. In his words: I want you to remember the stress is force per unit area. Stress is force divided by area. Spread the same force over more area, and the stress falls. That's the whole case for splinting.

The two jaws. The maxilla has thin cortical plates and fine trabeculae. It's a force distributing unit, full of sinuses and cavities, like the crumple zone of a car. The mandible has thick cortical plates and coarse trabeculae. It's a force absorbing unit. That's why implants fail more in the maxilla.

Misch's bone types. In his words: I do not have this listed but I want you to remember if you can write it down. D-one bone, anterior mandible.

D-one: thick, dense cortical bone. Anterior mandible. D-two: thick cortex with coarse trabeculae. Posterior mandible. D-three: thin cortex, finer trabeculae. Anterior maxilla. D-four: fine trabeculae, very little cortex. Posterior maxilla. The hook: start at the front of the lower jaw and walk back, then do the same in the upper. The bone gets softer every step.

The three forces. In his words: the most favorable would be compressive forces, the least favorable would be shearing forces. Okay, let's say giveaway for the quiz. Bone is strongest in compression, then tension, and weakest in shear. Maxillary implants sit at an angle, so they tend to take more shear and tension.

Cantilevers. Horizontal or vertical, both are bad. A cantilever is a force magnifier, because moment equals force times distance. His example: a twenty-five pound bite on a cantilevered pontic became seventy-five pounds on the implant acting as the fulcrum, and fifty on the other one. The fix is another implant under the pontic, or two separate bridges. The one exception he allowed: an opposing denture.

A vertical cantilever is a tall crown. As bone and gum are lost, the prosthesis has to replace more, and gets taller, like a fence post that's easy to wiggle from the top. Fix it with vertical ridge augmentation, or more implants.

When the forces are high, he gave three moves. More implants. A smaller occlusal table. And splint the implants together.

Biting force, from most to least. An implant-supported fixed bridge. Then natural teeth. Then an implant-retained denture. Then a complete denture.

And then the line he said outright. In his words: Remember never connect an implant and natural teeth. This will be your exam question.

Here's why, and it's personal. A tooth sits in a periodontal ligament. When you bite, a tooth sinks about twenty-five to a hundred microns into it. An implant moves three to five microns, and only because bone is a little elastic. Join them with a bridge and bite down. The tooth sinks into its ligament, and the implant can't, so the implant takes the force. Bone loss, then failure.

I'd like to point out that I have a ligament. Twenty-five to a hundred microns of give. It's how I can feel a twenty-five micron hair between my teeth. An implant is a very impressive piece of titanium, and it feels nothing. So please, don't tie me to one.

Implant occlusion. The goal: lighter contact on the implant than on the teeth around it. Load along the long axis, with lower cusps, a smaller table, and no contacts in excursions. Check it with shimstock, a very thin metal foil. It should hold on the natural teeth and pull through on the implant. A single implant crown opposing a natural tooth: out of contact in maximum intercuspation, when you check with shimstock. Two implant crowns opposing each other: out of contact even in forceful biting, and the shimstock drags, but doesn't tear.

## 6. The vocabulary of implants

Lecture four, Doctor Chen, the course director. His promise, in his words: If you can totally understand what I have on the slide, you should have no problem. I don't ask you to study on the textbook.

Brands and product lines. Nobel Biocare, Straumann and BioHorizons are brands. Each has several product lines. The school uses Nobel's Conical Connection, Straumann's Bone Level, and BioHorizons' Tapered Internal.

The platform is where the implant meets the abutment. Within a brand, colors tell you which parts match. But Nobel's yellow is not BioHorizons' yellow.

Now the BioHorizons trap. In his words: Look at the stickers, very important. Stickers, stickers. The surgeon's note says a four point six millimeter BioHorizons Tapered Internal Plus. You grab the four point five, the green one, because the numbers are close. Wrong. That implant has the three point five platform, the yellow one. The surgeon cares about the body's diameter. You care about the platform. The sticker in the chart tells you the platform. For Straumann, look for N-C or R-C, narrow or regular connection, not the body size.

Platform switching: the abutment is narrower than the implant. That moves the microgap inward, away from the crestal bone, which preserves bone, and it leaves more room for soft tissue.

External versus internal connection. External: the implant has a hex sticking up. More force goes to the abutment and screw, so more screw loosening, but it tolerates implants that diverge when they're splinted. Internal: the female part is inside the implant. More force on the implant itself, and less microgap. Everything at school is internal.

Conical versus non-conical. A conical connection is an internal connection where the abutment is tapered where it meets the implant. Compared with non-conical: the same implant survival, but less microgap, less marginal bone loss, and fewer prosthetic complications. A Morse taper is a conical connection with a taper that cold welds, by friction.

Bone level versus tissue level. A tissue-level implant has a polished collar up in the soft tissue, which moves the microgap away from the bone. A bone-level implant sits at the bone, and gives you more room to shape the emergence profile, so potentially better esthetics.

Parallel walls versus tapered. Parallel walls give more surface area. Tapered gives more primary stability, and less chance of hitting a root or a nerve.

The stability dip. Primary plus secondary equals total stability. Primary falls as the bone remodels, secondary rises, and the total dips a few weeks after placement. That's when you leave the implant alone.

Torque. It's rotational friction: the implant against bone going in, or a screw against its threads, and you set it with a torque wrench. In his words: So those are the common number you will need to memorize. Thirty-five, thirty, when do we use thirty-five, when we use thirty.

Nobel Biocare: thirty-five newton centimeters, except its three millimeter implant, at fifteen. Straumann: thirty-five. BioHorizons: thirty. The odd one out again.

Cover screw versus healing abutment. A cover screw is short, and hidden under the tissue at the first stage of a two-stage surgery. A healing abutment is taller and sticks through the tissue: at one-stage surgery, or at the second stage.

## 7. Where it goes, and what goes on it

Still Doctor Chen. First, the numbers around the implant. In his words: So these numbers I want to remember, okay? There's not too many signs inside, just remember, that's a rule of thumb.

Depth: the platform three to four millimeters below the ideal gingival margin. That's room for the biologic width, and for the crown to grow out of the tissue.

One and a half millimeters from a natural tooth. Three millimeters between two implants. Closer than three, and the bone between them resorbs, the papilla goes, and you get black triangles.

Two millimeters of bone on the facial, for anterior and immediate implants, because the facial bundle bone always resorbs after an extraction. And at least one millimeter on the lingual.

So: three to four deep, one and a half to a tooth, three to an implant, two facial, one lingual.

Anterior implants go a little palatal. Posterior implants go in the middle of the ridge.

Engaging versus non-engaging. An engaging abutment locks onto the implant's hex or grooves, and can't spin. A non-engaging one spins freely. His rule for a single implant crown: always engaging, whether it's screwed or cemented. Otherwise the crown spins.

For a cement-retained bridge: custom abutments, engaging. For a screw-retained bridge: non-engaging, on a Ti base. Two implants are almost never perfectly parallel, and the longer engaging connections would bind on the way in. Custom abutments are basically always engaging: a contoured abutment that could spin would never sit the same way twice.

Screw-retained versus cement-retained. Screw-retained: the abutment and crown are one piece, held by a screw through the crown. It's retrievable, and there's no cement to leave behind. Cement-retained: the abutment is screwed in, and the crown cemented on top. More passive, more esthetic, and a stronger crown with no hole in it.

If the implant comes out too far facial, a screw channel would come through the facial or the incisal edge, so you cement, or choose an angled screw channel.

Prefabricated versus custom abutments, also on the hint list. Prefabricated, or stock: a straight contour, a uniform margin, and you can only adjust by cutting away. Custom: shaped to the patient, more retention, and it traces the soft tissue, so the margin isn't buried.

Interocclusal space for a cement-retained molar, by Doctor Chen's count from the bone up. Three millimeters of biologic width, minus one for a margin one millimeter under the tissue, plus four for the abutment, plus one for the crown. Seven millimeters, from the bone to the opposing tooth. He called that a very minimal requirement. Hold that number. Doctor Chavali counts differently, and we'll square them in the next segment.

Crown-to-implant ratio. More forgiving than a tooth's one to one. But around two to one, say a fifteen millimeter crown, brings screw loosening, fractures and bone loss. Consider splinting.

The emergence profile, and the E, B and C zones, counting up from the platform. The C zone: the first one to one and a half millimeters, kept straight, set by the abutment. The B zone: the middle, the running room, concave preferred. The E zone: the last millimeter under the gingival margin, which decides how it looks. The rule: make it concave, and the margin creeps down, coronally. Make it convex, and you push the margin up, apically.

The placement and loading timeline, from the twenty eighteen I-T-I consensus. Placement. Immediate: the same day as the extraction. Early: four to eight weeks, after the soft tissue heals, or twelve to sixteen weeks, after partial bone healing. Late: more than six months. Loading. Immediate: within a week of placement. Early: a week to two months. Conventional: more than two months.

For immediate loading, the criteria include intact socket walls, a facial wall at least a millimeter thick, thick soft tissue, no acute infection, and an insertion torque of twenty-five to forty newton centimeters, or an I-S-Q above seventy. And putting a provisional on doesn't mean you have to load it.

## 9. Imaging for implants

Lecture six, Doctor Givan. His hint: be able to answer the details on his study guide. So here's the study guide.

The theme first. Implants are placed for a purpose, not, as he put it, because the patient is suffering from titanium insufficiency. In his words: That's why we speak of implant planning overall as being a prosthetically driven but biologically balanced process. Plan the teeth first. Then make sure there's bone to hold them.

Imaging in the three phases. Phase one, before surgery: the quality, quantity and angulation of the bone, where the critical structures are, and whether there's disease. Phase two, at surgery: the position and orientation of the implants, then healing and integration, and the abutment and prosthesis. Phase three, after the prosthesis, for as long as the implant lasts: the crestal bone. The crest is the early warning sign.

The three most common modalities for implants: periapical, panoramic, and cone beam C-T. And the guiding principle: ALARA. As low as reasonably achievable.

The periapical. Very high resolution. But no bone width, no measure of bone quality, and distortion if the angle is off. In his words: PAs are by far the number one go-to that we would want to have. A baseline film, and routine follow-up of the crest.

The panoramic. A great overview and a screening tool. But no bone width, no measure of bone quality, variable magnification, and positioning is crucial.

Medical C-T versus cone beam C-T. Medical C-T uses a fan beam, rotates very fast, scans the patient lying down, gives a higher dose, and has better soft tissue contrast. Cone beam uses a cone-shaped beam onto a flat panel, rotates slowly once, scans the patient sitting or standing, gives five to ten times less dose, and has more Compton scatter, so less soft tissue contrast. A cone beam is roughly three pans, and about a tenth of a medical C-T.

The Hounsfield scale is a measure of radiodensity. In the next lecture, a slightly different definition: a voxel's attenuation relative to air and water. His study guide says, know both.

What C-T gives you: accurate measurements in all three directions, no geometric distortion, models and drill guides, and the possibility of flapless surgery. Its problems: metal, which scatters the beam, and a patient who moves.

The artifacts. Motion: the patient or the template moves. Starburst: sharp changes in density, like bone next to metal, from limits in sampling, not from scatter. And beam hardening: metal filters the low-energy x-rays out of the beam, so parts of the image go black. Titanium makes little artifact. Gold makes a lot.

And the scan template. A temporary prosthesis the patient wears during the scan, replacing the missing teeth, so the planned teeth show up in three dimensions against the real bone. It holds the arches steady, opens the bite slightly, and shows the planned restoration, often with gutta percha markers. That's prosthetically driven planning, made visible.

## 10. Cone beam basic science

Lecture seven, Doctor Givan's physics. He picked the takeaway himself. In his words: I want to kind of make sure we pull out two important topics and good takeaways from this of saying why do I have trouble imaging, particularly around a lot of metal. The two answers: Compton scatter, and beam hardening.

How a cone beam works, in four sentences. The x-ray source and detector sit on a rotating gantry, with the patient in the axis of rotation. A cone-shaped beam passes through the region of interest. In a single rotation, a hundred eighty or three hundred sixty degrees, several hundred flat images are captured. Software reconstructs them into a volume you can view in the axial, sagittal and coronal planes.

The source. A heated cathode filament boils off electrons. That's thermionic emission. The voltage, the K-V-P, flings them into a tungsten focal spot on the anode. About ninety-nine percent of their energy becomes heat. About one percent becomes x-rays. Higher K-V-P, harder x-rays, and harder ones penetrate more.

Two ways an x-ray is made. Characteristic: the electron knocks out an inner shell electron, an outer one drops in to fill the hole, and a photon of one very specific energy comes out. Bremsstrahlung, braking radiation: the electron swings past a nucleus, slows, and the lost energy leaves as an x-ray of any energy. So the beam is polyenergetic. A spread of energies.

An aluminum or copper filter strips out the low-energy photons, which would only add dose.

Attenuation is the beam losing intensity as it passes through matter, by absorption and scatter. Thicker material, more attenuation. Higher atomic number or density, more attenuation. Gold is atomic number seventy-nine and scatters a lot. Titanium is twenty-two, and scatters little.

The four interactions. One: none at all, the photon passes straight through. Two: photoelectric absorption. The photon gives up all its energy and ejects an inner electron. That's what makes x-rays ionizing. It rises with atomic number, and falls as the photon's energy rises. Three: Rayleigh scattering. The photon changes direction without losing energy. Minor. Four: Compton scattering. The photon bounces off an electron, loses energy, and heads off in a new direction. Detected, it's an artifact, and it's the main difference between cone beam and medical C-T. The two that matter most: photoelectric absorption and Compton.

Receptors. Phosphor plates are for periapicals and bitewings, not cone beam. Cone beam mostly uses an indirect flat panel detector: a scintillator turns x-rays into light, and photodiodes turn the light into a signal. Each one is a pixel. Smaller pixels mean more resolution, but a worse signal-to-noise ratio.

Dynamic range is how finely a pixel can tell light from dark. It's measured in bit depth, usually fourteen or sixteen bits. Higher bit depth means finer gray scale, and it's one of the things that drives up the cost of a machine.

The region of interest is what you need to see. The field of view is the volume the machine captures, and ALARA says take only the field you need. Each cell of the volume is a voxel, and in cone beam it's a cube. The files go out in a standard format called DICOM.

Beam hardening shows as cupping, or white streaks and dark bands between two dense objects. Patient motion gives double contours.

And the line from his study guide: In C-B-C-T the H-U scale is unreliable, but still helpful, for treatment decisions, such as for immediate loading. In medical C-T, gray values are calibrated to Hounsfield units, air at minus a thousand, water at zero. In cone beam, scatter makes those numbers inconsistent. Treat them with caution.

## 11. Implant surgery

Lecture eight, Doctor Geisinger. Her title was Don't have a screw loose.

Osseointegration, two definitions on the hint list. Brånemark, nineteen sixty-nine: an intimate bone-to-implant contact at the light microscope level. Albrektsson and Sennerby, nineteen ninety-one: a direct structural and functional connection between living bone and the surface of a load-carrying implant. Living matters. A graft particle touching an implant isn't alive, so it isn't holding it.

The reality: only about sixty percent of the implant's surface has bone on it, even when it's healthy. The contact stops about point three millimeters from the implant-abutment junction. And integration takes six weeks to three months.

Early failures, on the hint list. Surgical: too much force, too much speed, not enough irrigation, contamination. Occlusal overload before secondary stability. Impaired wound healing. And poor bone metabolism. Late failures: peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis, overload, and prosthetic problems.

On heat, in her words: If bone temperature gets above forty-seven degrees Celsius, we get thermal necrosis that can be catastrophic. That's why the drills go small to large, one after another, with lots of irrigation.

Peri-implant disease. In her words: About seventy-five percent of peri-implantitis cases that we attempt to treat are either not treated or recur within a five-year period. So we're terrible at it. So the treatment is prevention.

The case definitions. Peri-implant health: no inflammation, no profuse bleeding, depths that don't increase over time, and no bone loss after initial healing. Peri-implant mucositis: inflammation and bleeding, but no bone loss beyond the early remodeling. Peri-implantitis: inflammation and bleeding, plus progressive bone loss, compared with the film one year after the prosthesis went in. And with no baseline records: bone three millimeters or more below the top of the implant, and or probing depths of six or more, with profuse bleeding.

The risk factors come in five groups: the patient, like hygiene and tobacco; the implant's design and surface; the site; the prosthesis, like residual cement; and the surgery, like a malpositioned implant.

Implant length, in her words: Implants over thirteen and a half millimeters actually have a higher failure rate than implants between eight and thirteen millimeters. Longer and wider is not always better.

Stability at placement, in her words: Thirty-five is a threshold to put something transgingival on. An implant that has a stability of less than fifteen is considered unstable. So, fifteen to thirty-five newton centimeters, and thirty-five or more before a healing abutment or provisional goes through the tissue.

Esthetic risk factors. The high-risk column: a reduced immune system, a heavy smoker, ten or more a day, high expectations, a high lip line, thin, high-scalloped tissue, triangular crowns, an acute infection at the site, bone seven millimeters or more from the contact point, restored neighbors, a gap of two or more teeth, a soft tissue defect, and a vertical bone deficiency.

And the space each prosthesis needs, from her table. A crown or bridge: seven to eight millimeters cement-retained, seven and a half screw-retained. A non-splinted overdenture: ten to twelve. A bar overdenture: eleven to fourteen. A hybrid: fifteen or more.

## 12. Guided surgery

Lecture nine, Doctor Chen again, the last one.

Surgically driven versus prosthetically driven. Surgically driven puts the implant where the bone is. Prosthetically driven puts it where the restoration needs it. In his words: an implant can be placed almost everywhere, except on a root, a nerve, or air. But you need to be able to restore it.

The best position. Posterior: right in the middle of the tooth, both ways, along the long axis. Anterior: along the zenith and long axis, and bucco-lingually at the middle of the lingual surface, above the cingulum.

Four ways to get there.

Freehand. No guide, the surgeon's experience, and it tends to follow the bone, so it's surgically driven. Cheap, but a higher risk of errors in depth, angle and position. It still needs a cone beam.

Semi-guided, or pilot-guided. A template guides only the first drill. The bone underneath isn't known when it's made, so after the pilot, the surgeon adjusts freehand. In his words: This is what we do most of the time in U-A-B student clinic.

Fully guided. The plan is made in software, from the cone beam and a digital wax-up, and the printed guide controls every drill. Accurate if it seats correctly, and faster surgery. But it costs more, takes longer to prepare, and there's the risk of blind surgery: trusting the guide too much.

Dynamic guided. No guide at all. Computer navigation, in real time, on a screen.

A fully guided plan needs two files. An S-T-L file: a surface scan of the teeth and soft tissue. And a DICOM file: the cone beam, with the bone, nerve and sinus. The surface scan is there because a cone beam shows little soft tissue, and restorations blur the teeth.

How a guide is supported. Tooth-supported is the most accurate. Tissue-supported can move, because tissue is soft. Bone-supported is built from the cone beam, so it can carry its distortion.

And his summary, in his words: C-B-C-T is the current standard of care for most implant cases.
