## 2. Insulin, and how diabetes is diagnosed

Lecture eleven, Doctor Fasinu. He opened by telling you how much this lecture matters. In his words: If you need to know anything at all in endocrinology, diabetes is the one that you want to know. In the exams, diabetes takes a very big chunk of assessment.

And he told you the two questions he asks about every drug. In his words: Which one will not cause hypoglycemia? Which one will not cause weight gain? I've said it, like, a thousand times now. So for every class, I'll give you both answers.

Diagnosis first. There are four tests, and in his words: We have four parameters here, and you need two. An A-one-C of six point five percent or higher. A fasting glucose of one twenty-six or higher. A two-hour glucose tolerance test of two hundred or higher. Or a random glucose above two hundred. Prediabetes is an A-one-C of five point seven to six point four, or a fasting glucose of one hundred to one twenty-five.

The complications, split by the size of the vessel. In his words: Micro is the one that involves neuropathy, nephropathy, and retinopathy. Those three are very important to know. Macrovascular is coronary artery disease, peripheral vascular disease and stroke.

GLUT four is the insulin-dependent glucose transporter, in muscle and fat. Insulin's tyrosine kinase receptor moves it up into the membrane.

C-peptide. Beta cells cut proinsulin into insulin and C-peptide, one to one. Injected insulin carries no C-peptide. In his words: C-peptide level will always tell you if they are making insulin at all, or if they are not making. So a patient on insulin for years with no C-peptide makes no insulin of their own.

Now the insulins. In his words: It's common to have questions that ask you, someone is planning to eat something, and what type of insulin they need.

Rapid-acting: lispro, aspart and glulisine. Onset five to fifteen minutes, gone in about four hours. That's mealtime, the bolus. Regular, the short-acting human insulin, peaks at about three hours and is gone by about eight. N-P-H, the intermediate one, carries protamine to slow it down, and its onset is two to four hours. Too slow for a meal you're about to eat. Glargine and detemir are basal: a slow, steady release with no real peak, and glargine lasts more than twenty-four hours. Degludec is ultra-long, more than forty-two.

In his own practice question, the patient was about to eat, and the choices were regular, N-P-H and glargine. His answer was regular, the fastest of those three.

Which brings me back to the pie. A patient about to eat that pie wants a rapid one, or regular. Not N-P-H. Not glargine. I'm not a patient. I'm a tooth. The pie is decorative.

Only two insulins are sold without a prescription: regular and N-P-H. In his words: Regular and intermediate have been around for a long time, so they are available over the counter, so you should know that.

Insulin's adverse effects: hypoglycemia first, weight gain, a brief worsening of retinopathy when it's started, and lipoatrophy or lipohypertrophy at the injection site. And he said you won't need dose arithmetic.

## 3. The diabetes drugs that aren't insulin

The secretagogues first. In his words: Any drug that works by stimulating insulin release will have this same side effect of hypoglycemia.

The sulfonylureas, glyburide, glipizide and glimepiride, bind the sulfonylurea receptor on the beta cell's A-T-P sensitive potassium channel and shut it. Potassium stays in, the cell depolarizes, calcium comes in, and insulin goes out. Whatever the glucose is. So: hypoglycemia, and weight gain.

They need working beta cells. In his words: This drug will only work in people who have a functional pancreas, who are still able to make insulin. No C-peptide, no point. They also wear out: after a year or two, the dose climbs until they stop working. Their warnings: G-six-P-D deficiency, liver and kidney disease, and a disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol.

The meglitinides, repaglinide and nateglinide, hit the same channel, fast, with meals. And yes, they cause hypoglycemia too. Less than the sulfonylureas, but they do. The class keeps missing that one.

Metformin. In his words: Metformin is very important, because so far it is still the first-line drug for anyone who is newly diagnosed with diabetes. Its main effect is to cut the liver's glucose production. And the key line, in his words: If it's normal, metformin will not bring it lower. So its risk of hypoglycemia is very low, and it's weight neutral, or a little weight comes off.

Metformin's adverse effects: stomach upset that fades in a week or two, low vitamin B twelve, and lactic acidosis. Lactic acidosis is metformin's. The disulfiram reaction belongs to the sulfonylureas. The class mixes those two up, so don't. And the absolute contraindication, in his words: When the G-F-R is less than thirty, we do not give them metformin.

The thiazolidinediones, pioglitazone and rosiglitazone. PPAR gamma agonists, a nuclear receptor. Little hypoglycemia, but weight gain, and edema, and so heart failure. In his words: This drug is only recommended now for people who don't have any cardiovascular issues, because of the tendency to cause edema. And macular edema. He said he skipped it in class, but it's right there on the slide. The class linked to macular edema is this one.

The G-L-P one agonists. G-L-P one is a gut hormone that raises insulin only when glucose is up, lowers glucagon, slows the stomach and cuts appetite. The first drug came from exendin four, in Gila monster saliva, which the enzyme D-P-P four can't break down. Semaglutide is weekly, and Rybelsus is the one tablet. On their own, no hypoglycemia, and weight comes off. In his words: If somebody has a weight problem, obviously everybody here should know that G-L-P will be the first-line drug.

Their boxed warning is thyroid C-cell tumors. In his words: People who have a history of thyroid cancer have been advised not to try the drug.

The D-P-P four inhibitors, the gliptins: sitagliptin, saxagliptin, linagliptin and alogliptin. Daily, by mouth, no hypoglycemia, weight neutral, and modest. Avoid them in heart failure, except sitagliptin. And linagliptin needs no renal adjustment.

The S-G-L-T two inhibitors, the gliflozins. They block glucose reabsorption in the kidney, so the patient pees out the sugar. Hypoglycemia is rare. The side effects come from sugary urine: genital yeast infections, urinary tract infections, a lot of urine, and low blood pressure. The boxed warning is amputation. And because they work like diuretics, they help heart failure. Dapagliflozin, Farxiga, is approved to treat it. In his words: Somebody has a cardiovascular problem and you give them diuretics to help them pee, and now they have newly diagnosed diabetes. Why won't you consider something like Farxiga?

So, the situations, because that's how he asks. New and otherwise healthy: metformin. A weight problem: a G-L-P one agonist, like semaglutide. Heart failure on a diuretic: an S-G-L-T two inhibitor, like dapagliflozin, and never a thiazolidinedione. Kidney function below thirty: no metformin, and linagliptin needs no adjustment. A history of thyroid cancer: no G-L-P one agonist.

And hypoglycemia in your chair. Awake and able to swallow: juice, regular soda or glucose tablets. Unresponsive: in his words, In emergency, the drug of choice is glucagon. By injection, or as a nasal spray.

## 4. Primary or secondary, and the pituitary

Lecture twelve, Doctor Dababneh, a head and neck pathologist, over two hours.

The rule he gave you is the most useful thing in either hour. Primary means the problem is in the gland that makes the hormone. Secondary means that gland is being driven, or let down, from somewhere else, usually the pituitary. In his words: T-three, T-four high, T-S-H low, that's primary. Both high, that's secondary. And this applies to any other organ where there's communication with the pituitary gland.

So: opposite directions, the gland is the cause, and it's primary. Same direction, the pituitary is the cause, and it's secondary. T-four high and T-S-H low is primary hyperthyroidism, like Graves. T-four high and T-S-H high is a pituitary adenoma making T-S-H. T-four low and T-S-H high is primary hypothyroidism, like Hashimoto. Everything low means the pituitary has failed, like Sheehan syndrome.

Keep that rule handy. It comes back for cortisol in two segments, and that's where the class has been reading the labs backwards.

The anterior pituitary is a true gland. The posterior is nerve fibers and pituicytes, storing A-D-H and oxytocin from the hypothalamus. Five anterior cell types make seven hormones, and the name tells you the hormone. Corticotrophs make A-C-T-H and M-S-H. Gonadotrophs make F-S-H and L-H. T-R-H releases two hormones, T-S-H and prolactin. Dopamine stops prolactin, and somatostatin stops growth hormone.

Adenomas. A microadenoma is under ten millimeters, a macroadenoma over ten. The big ones show up by mass effect: an enlarged sella, headache, nausea and vomiting, and bitemporal hemianopsia. The tumor pushes up on the optic chiasm and hits the fibers that cross in the middle, so the outer half of each visual field goes.

The functioning ones. Prolactinoma is the most common: amenorrhea and galactorrhea, usually a microadenoma in a premenopausal woman. Somatotroph adenomas are the largest: gigantism in a child, acromegaly in an adult. Corticotroph adenomas cause Cushing disease. Gonadotroph adenomas cause no syndrome, and turn up by their mass.

Hypopituitarism needs about seventy-five percent of the gland gone. The causes: a null cell adenoma, which makes nothing and crowds out the rest. Sheehan syndrome, the gland dying after blood pressure crashes around delivery. And empty sella, where he said you don't need the details.

Diabetes insipidus and S-I-A-D-H. His trick is to let the name carry you. In his words: If it's an antidiuretic thing and you have a deficiency of it, you'll have a lot of diuresis. Central diabetes insipidus: no A-D-H, a lot of dilute urine, and a high sodium. Nephrogenic: the A-D-H is normal, and the kidneys ignore it. S-I-A-D-H is the opposite: water is kept, and sodium falls. In his words: The most common cause of an ectopic production of A-D-H is small cell carcinoma of the lung. These patients will come with hyponatremia.

## 5. The thyroid: Graves, Hashimoto and the tumors

The thyroid starts at the back of the tongue and travels down. So a lingual thyroid, at the base of the tongue, and a thyroglossal duct cyst, in the midline of the neck, are leftovers of that trip. A dentist can find both.

Graves disease is the most common cause of endogenous hyperthyroidism. An I-G-G antibody stimulates the T-S-H receptor. Diffuse hyperplasia, a goiter, the eye disease, and about five women for every man. Hashimoto thyroiditis, properly chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis, is the most common hypothyroidism in the United States. As taught in this lecture, its antibodies block the T-S-H receptor. In his words: Unlike the autoantibodies in Graves that stimulate T-S-H, these ones block T-S-H. Same target, opposite effect.

Adult hypothyroidism is myxedema: sluggish, cold, weight gain, a coarse face, puffy hands, and an enlarged tongue. In a child it's cretinism: short stature, slow development, a large head and a large tongue. The tongue is the one you'll see first, from the chair.

Now the tumors, and the genes he said are real practice even though they're not on the slides. Papillary carcinoma is B-raf. Follicular carcinoma is Ras. Medullary carcinoma is Ret.

Papillary carcinoma. Sixty to eighty percent of thyroid cancers, usually under forty, linked to radiation, slow growing. It spreads by the lymphatics, to the neck nodes. And it can be multifocal. In his words: If we diagnose F-T-C, a hemithyroidectomy is enough. If you have P-T-C, you have to take the entire thyroid gland, because the other lobe might have another focus.

Under the microscope: papillae on a fibrovascular core, and the nuclei, the Orphan Annie eye nuclei. Enlarged, crowded, with grooves and pseudoinclusions. And pale. In his words: Most importantly, they have hypochromasia. Not hyper, hypo. So a question that calls them hyperchromatic is the trap. And psammoma bodies, the layered calcifications of dead papillae, in about half.

Follicular carcinoma. The capsule and the vessels are the whole story. A follicular adenoma has an intact capsule. A carcinoma invades the capsule, mushrooming through it, or invades a vessel. It spreads by blood, to the lung and bone, not by the lymphatics. That's another one the class keeps missing: follicular carcinoma reaches the lung through the blood. And taking out one lobe is enough.

Medullary carcinoma comes from the C cells, which make calcitonin. The Ret mutation. Eighty percent are sporadic, solitary, around forty-five. The familial ones are younger, bilateral, with C-cell hyperplasia, part of multiple endocrine neoplasia types two-A and two-B. The tumor holds amyloid, and it stains for calcitonin, chromogranin and C-E-A.

Anaplastic carcinoma: older patients, a neck mass that grows within a couple of months and kills. He called it one of his emergencies: straight to molecular testing, hoping for a target, like B-raf.

## 6. The parathyroid

The second hour. Parathyroid hormone first, and his shorthand: P-T-H takes back calcium and dumps phosphate.

Where the glands are. In his words: They sit behind the thyroid gland, not anterior to it. Behind. And that's why a thyroidectomy can take them out with it.

His rule for a normal gland: the percent that's cells is about a hundred minus the age. In his words: If you are in your seventies, seventy percent of the parathyroid should be fat, and thirty percent should be your neuroendocrine cells.

Primary hyperparathyroidism: calcium high, P-T-H high, phosphate low. Stones, bones and groans, though most patients today are found on a routine blood panel with no symptoms. Three causes. An adenoma, eighty to ninety percent: one gland, more often a lower one. Hyperplasia, ten to fifteen percent: all four glands. And carcinoma, very rare, with a calcium over thirteen, invasion and necrosis.

The point he made carefully. In his words: Adenomas and hyperplasias cannot be distinguished only on lab testing. It's purely based on whether it's a single nodule or multiple. So count the glands. One enlarged gland is an adenoma: take it out. Several is hyperplasia: take three and a half.

On a sestamibi scan, only the overactive gland is still lit at two hours. And during surgery, in his words: If the surgery is effective, the amount of parathyroid hormone will drop more than fifty percent. It works because active P-T-H lasts only minutes.

Secondary hyperparathyroidism. The glands are doing their job, against chronic low calcium, most often from renal failure. Usually every gland turns hyperplastic. So a dialysis patient with four big glands has secondary hyperparathyroidism, not primary hyperplasia. The class has been picking primary there. And the fix, in his words: The most important is to solve the primary issue.

Hypoparathyroidism: P-T-H low, calcium low, phosphate high. After a thyroidectomy, in DiGeorge syndrome, or autoimmune. Two signs of the low calcium. Chvostek's sign: tap the cheek, and the face twitches. Trousseau's sign: inflate a cuff on the arm, and the hand spasms. And one for you. In his words: Dental root hypoplasia, important, no calcium.

Pseudohypoparathyroidism. In his words: Pseudo means false. The parathyroid hormones are produced just fine, but the organ is not responding. So it looks like hypoparathyroidism, low calcium and high phosphate, but P-T-H is high. Add short stature and short metacarpals.

## 7. The adrenal

The cortex, from the outside in. In his words: It gets sweeter as you go from outside to inside. Salt, sugar, sex. The glomerulosa makes aldosterone. The fasciculata makes cortisol. The reticularis makes androgens. And the medulla makes catecholamines.

Cushing syndrome. Read cortisol with A-C-T-H, exactly like the thyroid. In his words: Primary Cushing, cortisol high, and the A-C-T-H low. Secondary, both will be high.

This is where the class has been going wrong, so slowly. An adrenal tumor makes cortisol on its own, and the cortisol shuts the pituitary off. Cortisol high, A-C-T-H low. A pituitary tumor making A-C-T-H, which is Cushing disease, drives both adrenals. Cortisol high, A-C-T-H high. An ectopic tumor, like a lung cancer, does the same: both high. So if A-C-T-H is low, it is not the pituitary. And if A-C-T-H is sky high while cortisol is low, that's Addison disease: the adrenal failing, and the pituitary shouting at it. Not a pituitary tumor.

Aldosterone works the same way. Conn syndrome, an adrenal adenoma: aldosterone high, renin low. Secondary, from heart failure or renal artery stenosis: aldosterone high, renin high. In his words: The renin would be low, aldosterone would be high. The same concept repeats itself, primary versus secondary. And most cortical adenomas secrete nothing. The ones that do mostly make aldosterone.

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia. Autosomal recessive, a missing enzyme on the way to cortisol. Of his whole steroid map, he taught two blocks. Twenty-one hydroxylase, the most common: the pathway backs up into androgens. Androgen excess, and about a third also waste sodium. Seventeen hydroxylase: androgen deficiency, plus precursors that act like aldosterone, so sodium is kept and blood pressure rises. The treatment, in his words: The treatment for all congenital adrenal hyperplasia is glucocorticoids, because they utilize the same concept of negative feedback. The glucocorticoid turns A-C-T-H down, and the whole pathway quiets.

Addison disease: everything low but A-C-T-H. Low sodium, high potassium, low glucose, low blood pressure, and hyperpigmentation. Autoimmune in sixty to seventy percent.

The medulla. Pheochromocytoma: chromaffin cells making catecholamines, so sweating, palpitations and attacks of hypertension. Under the microscope, the cells sit in nests. In his words: The first person who described it was German, so he described the pattern as Zellballen, which means the balls. The rule of tens: ten percent familial, ten percent outside the adrenal, ten percent bilateral, ten percent malignant. He warned the numbers are old, but they're what the slide teaches.

And neuroblastoma, the childhood tumor of the medulla. Very aggressive, eighty percent under age five, most often in the abdomen. In his words: Whenever you hear the word blast, it means it's the precursor, or the immature form of the cells.

The penis. Two corpora cavernosa fill with blood. The urethra runs in the corpus spongiosum, which fills far less, so the urethra stays open. In his words: If we had a corpus cavernosum around the urethra, there wouldn't be many human beings. Erection is smooth muscle relaxing, not contracting. And his old teacher's mnemonic, in his words: It's always point and shoot. So it's parasympathetic for pointing and sympathetic for shooting.

## 9. Male reproductive physiology

Doctor Madison, the same organ from the hormone side. He told you exactly where to study. In his words: This is where I would study. Being able to compare and contrast Sertoli cells and Leydig cells is really important for this lecture.

So here's the two-column list. Sertoli: in the tubule wall, run by F-S-H. Nourishment, the blood-testis barrier, androgen-binding protein to keep testosterone high in the tubule, inhibin, spermiation, aromatase, and in the fetus, A-M-H. Leydig: in the interstitium, run by L-H. Testosterone.

His correction. His slide says the Leydig cells hold the aromatase in adults. In class he told you, in his words: You can cross this out and put it in red. The Sertoli cells make most of the aromatase, from fetal life on, and the Leydig cells some. So if a question asks where aromatase is, say Sertoli.

Feedback, and where it lands. In his words: So testosterone turns off luteinizing hormone at the anterior pituitary, inhibin turns off F-S-H. Testosterone works at two levels: G-N-R-H at the hypothalamus, and L-H at the pituitary. Inhibin works at the pituitary only. In his words: Inhibin specifically doesn't feed back to the hypothalamus.

Reading the patterns. Failing testes: testosterone low, L-H and F-S-H high. A failing pituitary: everything low. Testosterone injections: blood testosterone high, L-H and F-S-H low, and the sperm count falls, because the testis loses its own high testosterone.

Building a male. A-M-H from the Sertoli cells makes the Mullerian ducts regress. In his words: What you need to know is that in development, when A-M-H is not around, you're going to develop these Mullerian ducts. Testosterone from the Leydig cells builds the Wolffian structures: the epididymis, the vas deferens and the seminal vesicles. And D-H-T, made by five alpha reductase, builds the penis, the scrotum and the prostate.

Two disorders, and one question settles them: can testosterone act? In androgen insensitivity, the receptor is broken. X-Y, with testes inside the body, but no Wolffian structures, female external genitalia with a blind-ended vagina, and a female puberty. In five alpha reductase deficiency, testosterone works, so the Wolffian structures are there, but there's no D-H-T, so the external genitalia are female. These children are often raised as girls, and virilize at puberty. And in both, A-M-H works, so neither one has a uterus.

Making testosterone: L-H turns up cholesterol desmolase, the rate-limiting step, the same step A-C-T-H controls in the adrenal.

Temperature: he said about thirty-four degrees Celsius, and Doctor Herr's slide said thirty-five. Go with each lecture's own number.

His numbers for the glands: seminal vesicles, sixty to seventy percent, with fibrinogen that clots the semen. Prostate, twenty to thirty percent, with P-S-A, which breaks that clot down.

And the erection itself. The parasympathetic nerves release nitric oxide, which dilates the cavernosal arteries. Nitric oxide turns on guanylate cyclase, which makes cyclic G-M-P, and the smooth muscle relaxes. P-D-E five breaks cyclic G-M-P down. Sildenafil blocks P-D-E five, so cyclic G-M-P lasts longer. It needs nitric oxide to work. No stimulation, no effect.

## 10. Female reproductive histology

Now the female side, Doctor Herr's histology first. His focus list here: the antral and Graafian follicles, the uterine tube's cells, the uterine wall, and the endometrium in menstruation. This is the lecture the class has practiced least, by a lot. If you have an hour left, give some of it to this one.

The oocyte's two arrests, from a slide he marked with three asterisks. In his words: All follicles within the ovary contain a primary oocyte arrested in prophase one. The L-H surge drives this process. The secondary oocyte enters meiosis two and arrests at metaphase two until fertilization.

The numbers fall the whole way. Five to seven million oocytes by the fifth fetal month, one to two million at birth, about four hundred thousand at menarche, and about four hundred fifty ovulated in a lifetime.

The five follicle stages. Primordial: one layer of flat, squamous cells. Early primary: one layer of cuboidal granulosa cells, and the zona pellucida starts. Late primary: several layers, and the theca forms outside. Secondary: fluid, the liquor folliculi, and an antrum. And the Graafian follicle: about twenty millimeters, bulging from the ovary.

The Graafian follicle's wall, from the antrum out. In his words: There's a granulosa layer, a very small basement membrane, a theca interna, and a theca externa. The theca interna has L-H receptors and makes androgens. And granulosa cells have gap junctions but no tight junctions, so there's no blood-follicle barrier, unlike the testis.

The time scale. In his words: The process of going from this primordial follicle to a mature follicle takes many months. Not one cycle. And in his words: So F-S-H surge induces growth, but we need L-H to kind of trigger maturation and ovulation of the dominant oocyte. F-S-H grows ten to twelve antral follicles. L-H ovulates the dominant one.

Speaking of follicles. I came from one too. Every tooth grows inside a dental follicle, a little sac of tissue around the developing tooth. Mine made my cementum, my periodontal ligament, and the bone around my roots. You know how I feel about my ligament. Turns out it runs in the family.

Ovulation. Estrogen from the Graafian follicle sets off the L-H surge, which finishes meiosis one. The follicle bursts through the ovary's capsule into the peritoneal cavity, and the tube's fimbriae catch the oocyte. What's left becomes the corpus luteum, making progesterone. Without a pregnancy, it becomes the corpus albicans, a white scar.

His cycle graph. Ovulation on days thirteen to fifteen. Estrogen peaks around day twelve or thirteen, just before the L-H surge. F-S-H peaks with L-H just before ovulation, a smaller peak. Progesterone peaks around day twenty-two, from the corpus luteum.

The uterine tube. In his words: The wave of cilia is directed towards the uterus. These non-ciliated peg cells will secrete fluid that provides nutrient to the ovum. Folds in every region, a poorly defined muscularis, and fertilization, usually in the ampulla.

The uterine wall. The endometrium: simple columnar epithelium, simple branched glands, a functionalis and a basalis. The myometrium: inner longitudinal, middle circular with the big vessels, the stratum vasculare, and outer longitudinal. Then the thin perimetrium.

Menstruation. Spiral arteries feed the functionalis. Straight arteries feed the basalis. In his words: There's periodic contractions of the spiral artery that are triggered by a reduction in progesterone. The functionalis goes hypoxic and sheds. The basalis has its own straight arteries, so it survives, and regrows the lining.

Quickly: the cervix makes a thick mucus plug in pregnancy, and switches to stratified squamous at the transformation zone. Prolactin makes milk, oxytocin ejects it, and colostrum has more protein and I-G-A.

## 11. Female reproductive physiology

The last lecture on the exam, Doctor Madison again. He flagged two things.

The first. In his words: High yield for exam, right? Compare and contrast luteal versus follicular.

So here's the table. The follicular phase: days zero to fourteen, also called proliferative. The growing follicle makes estradiol. The lining grows and its arteries lengthen. The cervical mucus is watery and stretchy, and helps the sperm along. It's working toward fertilization.

The luteal phase: days fourteen to twenty-eight, also called secretory. The corpus luteum makes progesterone. Proliferation slows. Secretions, blood vessels and glycogen go up. The mucus turns thick. Body temperature rises a little, from progesterone on the hypothalamus. It's working toward implantation.

The second thing. In his words: Very important slide. I'd know this slide pretty well, because this is showing you who's responding to what. Two cells, two gonadotropins. L-H acts on the theca cells and turns up cholesterol desmolase, the rate-limiting step, cholesterol to pregnenolone. The theca cells make testosterone, but they have no aromatase, so the testosterone moves over to the granulosa cells. F-S-H acts on the granulosa cells and turns up aromatase: testosterone to seventeen beta estradiol. Theca is like Leydig. Granulosa is like Sertoli. And of every enzyme on his map, in his words: I don't care that you know most of those enzymes unless we're talking about aromatase or cholesterol desmolase.

Feedback. Progesterone is negative, only. Estradiol is negative most of the time. Then, once it passes about two hundred picograms per milliliter, it flips to positive, and you get the L-H surge. That positive feedback is why the female axis cycles and the male axis doesn't. And F-S-H barely rises with it. In his words: And inhibin, remember, is going to preferentially inhibit F-S-H. So that's why we don't call it an L-H F-S-H surge, we call it an L-H surge. F-S-H still rises at midcycle, just far less than L-H. Doctor Herr's graph and Doctor Madison agree on that.

Ovulation. The follicle about to ovulate is loaded with L-H receptors. The surge does three things to it. Collagenase weakens its wall. Prostaglandins raise its blood flow. And oocyte maturation inhibitor falls, so meiosis one can finish. Ovulation comes fourteen days before menses. The corpus luteum lasts about fourteen days, then regresses, estrogen and progesterone drop, and menses begins.

The follicle, his way. Granulosa cells carry the F-S-H receptors from the primary stage, and theca cells, with their L-H receptors, appear at the secondary stage. Several follicles grow each cycle. Of the names in between, in his words: Just know that one of them is going to become the dominant follicle.

Three estrogens, three stages of life. Estradiol in the reproductive years, from the ovary. Estriol in pregnancy. Estrone in menopause, made from the adrenal's D-H-E-A, and much weaker.

Menopause. The follicles are gone, so there's no estradiol and no inhibin. Without negative feedback, G-N-R-H, L-H and F-S-H all go up. Hot flashes have been linked to the G-N-R-H peak, though he said the research is still going on.

The default pathway. In his words: The default pathway is female reproductive development. No A-M-H, so the Mullerian ducts form the tubes and the uterus. No testosterone, so the Wolffian ducts regress. No D-H-T, so the external genitalia are female.

Amenorrhea. Primary means periods never start: usually genetic, and his example was Turner syndrome. Secondary means periods stop: pregnancy, lactation, menopause, and too much prolactin. In his words: Prolactin is a negative signal to G-N-R-H.

What you don't need: the slide on the whole-body actions of estrogen and progesterone. In his words: I'm not gonna test you on this.
