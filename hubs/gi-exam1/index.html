Introduction to the GI Tract

Dr. Fuller · sets up the whole course

The tract as a processing plant

Dr. Fuller frames the whole GI system as a chemical processing line: a mechanical stage to break raw material into manageable pieces, a reaction vessel (the stomach) that dissolves and sterilizes with acid, inputs of enzymes and detergents (pancreas, liver), a long absorptive tube, a compaction stage for waste, and an emission device. The tube itself runs mouth → pharynx → esophagus → stomach → small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum) → large intestine (cecum, appendix, colon) → rectum/anus, supported by the accessory organs: salivary glands, liver, gallbladder, pancreas.

Wall organization, inside out

Mucosa — epithelium (single layer, absorbs and secretes) + lamina propria (carries an artery, vein, and lymphatic vessel up into each villus) + muscularis mucosae (a thin smooth muscle sheet that moves the villi themselves). Submucosa — connective tissue, larger vessels, glands in some regions, and Meissner's (submucosal) plexus. Muscularis externa — inner circular layer + outer longitudinal layer, with Auerbach's (myenteric) plexus sandwiched between them. Serosa — outermost coat, continuous with the mesentery.

Amplifying surface area

Absorption is the point of the whole system, so the small intestine stacks three levels of folding: plicae circulares (macroscopic folds of the tube), villi (finger-like outward projections of the mucosa — absorption happens at the tip), and microvilli (a brush border on each individual epithelial cell). Stacked together this is roughly a 600-fold amplification over a plain cylinder. New epithelial cells are born from stem cells in the crypts at the base of each villus — all secretion happens down in the crypt — then migrate up the villus over about three days, switching their gene expression from secretory to absorptive as they climb, before being shed at the tip. The colon is nearly the mirror image: almost all crypt, no villi, built for secretion and water absorption rather than nutrient uptake.

Four functions of the GI system

Per the course's own framing, the four functions are secretion, absorption, motility, and protection (not "digestion" — that's a common textbook substitution you may see elsewhere, but this course names protection as the fourth). About 8–9 liters of fluid are dumped into the gut per day (saliva, gastric juice, pancreatic and biliary secretions, plus what you drink) — nearly all of it is reabsorbed, and only ~100–200 mL leaves as fecal water. Motility is built on peristalsis: distension triggers contraction above and relaxation below a bolus, entirely managed within the gut wall — no brain input required. Protection layers tight junctions between epithelial cells (the physical barrier), mucus (lubricates and traps pathogens, and shields the stomach from its own acid), pH maintenance (Brunner's glands and pancreatic bicarbonate buffer the acid load hitting the duodenum), and GALT (gut-associated lymphoid tissue) — an estimated 25–75% of the body's immune tissue lives in the gut.

The microbiome

Over 2,000 bacterial species live in a healthy gut. Eubiosis is a balanced, mutualistic, anti-inflammatory population — some of these bacteria ferment fiber we can't digest (like inulin) into short-chain fatty acids (butyrate, propionate, acetate) that nourish the gut itself. Dysbiosis is reduced diversity with pathogenic species gaining ground — a pro-inflammatory state that can degrade tight-junction integrity into leaky gut, letting bacteria and toxins cross into the bloodstream (the liver's Kupffer cells act as the next line of defense).

The enteric nervous system

The gut has roughly 500 million neurons — second only to the brain — organized mainly into the myenteric (motility) and submucosal (secretion/blood flow) plexuses, dense enough that an isolated segment of intestine will still peristalse in a dish with no blood or nerve supply. The autonomic nervous system modulates this system rather than replacing it. Many "brain" neurotransmitters double as gut signals (acetylcholine, dopamine, norepinephrine, serotonin — about 95% of the body's serotonin is made in the gut — plus CCK, opioids, VIP, substance P), which is part of why the gut-brain axis is an active research area. The gut also senses macronutrient composition and adjusts pancreatic enzyme gene expression accordingly (more amylase on a high-carb diet, more protease on a high-protein diet).

Three modes of chemical signaling

Endocrine — released into blood, acts on a distant target. Neurocrine — released from a nerve varicosity, acts like a neurotransmitter. Paracrine — released and diffuses to a neighboring cell. The same molecule (CCK is the textbook example) can act in more than one mode — the mode is a property of how and where it's released, not of the molecule itself.

Four phases of digestion

Cephalic/oral — pure anticipation (smell, sight, sound, and the taste/pressure of food reaching the mouth); 100% neural via the vagus, no hormones involved yet. Drives salivary, gastric, and pancreatic secretion, gallbladder contraction, and sphincter of Oddi relaxation. Pavlov's dogs are the textbook conditioned-reflex example. Gastric — mechanical distension plus chemical stimulants drive acid, pepsinogen, and mucus release and stronger contractions. Intestinal — chyme and its osmolarity trigger pancreatic/biliary secretion and nutrient absorption while inhibiting further gastric secretion. Colonic — the ileocecal valve relaxes, water is absorbed, and mass movements drive material toward defecation.

Think of your gut as one very long tube running from your mouth to the other end, built like a factory line: mash the food up, dissolve it with acid, soak the good stuff into your blood, and pack the leftovers into waste. The tube's wall is built like a jacket with four layers — an inner lining that touches food and does the absorbing, a layer packed with blood vessels and nerves, muscle layers that squeeze food along, and an outer wrapper.

To fit more absorbing surface into a small space, the lining is folded, then covered in tiny finger-like bumps (villi), and each of those bumps is covered in even tinier hairs (microvilli) — like turning a smooth drinking straw into shag carpet. That trick roughly multiplies the usable surface by 600 times. New lining cells are born in little pits at the base of each "finger," ride up it like a slow escalator over about three days, and get worn off the tip once they arrive — replaced constantly.

Your gut basically runs itself. It has almost as many nerve cells as a small brain, wired specifically to squeeze food along on autopilot — cut a piece out and put it in a dish, and it'll still contract in the right pattern with no help from your actual brain. Trillions of bacteria live in there too; a healthy, balanced community helps you, but an unbalanced one can make the gut lining "leaky" and cause inflammation.

The gut does four jobs: makes fluids (secretion), pulls nutrients into the blood (absorption), pushes material along (motility), and keeps out things that shouldn't get in (protection — mucus, a tight cell-to-cell seal, and a small army of immune cells). And digestion doesn't wait for food to arrive — just smelling dinner cooking gets your stomach revving, the same trick Pavlov used to make his dogs drool at a bell before any food showed up.

Anatomy of the Foregut

Foregut · Celiac trunk
Dr. Herr

What counts as foregut

The foregut runs from the abdominal esophagus (once it crosses the diaphragm) through the stomach to the second part of the duodenum, plus the spleen, liver, gallbladder, and pancreas. Everything in it is supplied by one unpaired branch of the abdominal aorta: the celiac trunk.

Peritoneum and mesentery

Visceral peritoneum covers organs and carries visceral afferents — poorly localized, referred pain. Parietal peritoneum lines the body wall and carries somatic afferents — sharp, well-localized pain. A mesentery is a reflection of peritoneum from parietal to visceral that suspends an organ and carries its neurovascular supply; naming follows the target — meso- for a dorsal wall attachment (mesoappendix), ligament for two organs tied together or a ventral wall attachment (hepatoduodenal ligament), omenta for anything attached to the stomach. Retroperitoneal organs (peritoneum on one side only) are remembered with SAD PUCKER: Suprarenal glands, Aorta/IVC, Duodenum (parts 2–4), Pancreas (mostly), Ureters, Colon (ascending/descending), Kidneys, Esophagus, Rectum.

Stomach

Abdominal esophagus enters at the cardia; the domed top is the fundus; the body secretes most of the acid; the pylorus is a thick sphincter controlling emptying into the duodenal bulb — the classic site for duodenal ulcers, dangerous because the gastroduodenal artery and common bile duct sit directly behind that wall. Lesser curvature attaches toward the liver; greater curvature attaches toward the transverse colon and spleen. The Z-line marks the abrupt esophageal-to-gastric mucosal transition. Rugae are the folds seen when the stomach is empty. The pylorus sits roughly at the transpyloric plane (L1, halfway between the jugular notch and pubic symphysis).

Duodenum

C-shaped, four parts: superior (1st, ulcer-prone), descending (2nd — the major duodenal papilla here marks the foregut→midgut boundary and the switch from celiac to superior mesenteric supply), horizontal (3rd, crosses at L3), ascending (4th). The major duodenal papilla receives the hepatopancreatic ampulla (common bile duct + main pancreatic duct), gated by the sphincter of Oddi.

Liver, gallbladder, biliary tree

The liver sits right-sided; the falciform ligament divides right and left lobes and carries the ligamentum teres (obliterated fetal umbilical vein) to the umbilicus. The porta hepatis is its hilum, carrying the portal triad (common bile duct, proper hepatic artery, portal vein). The bare area (no mesentery, against the diaphragm) is a direct route for disease to spread between lung and liver. Bile flows liver → right/left hepatic ducts → common hepatic duct → (joined by the cystic duct from the gallbladder) → common bile duct → hepatopancreatic ampulla → major duodenal papilla. Gallstone logic is a favorite exam question: a stone in the cystic duct still allows new bile through to the duodenum, just not stored bile; a stone in the common hepatic duct blocks both storage and direct delivery; a stone in the common bile duct blocks all bile delivery even though the liver and gallbladder are both fine; a stone at the hepatopancreatic ampulla blocks bile and pancreatic enzymes together.

Pancreas and spleen

Pancreas: head, neck, uncinate process, body, tail — all oriented around the superior mesenteric vessels; the main pancreatic duct (of Wirsung) joins the common bile duct at the ampulla, while the accessory duct (of Santorini) opens at the minor duodenal papilla. Islets of Langerhans are endocrine (insulin, glucagon); the acinar tissue is exocrine (digestive enzymes). Spleen: left side, against ribs 9–11, held by the gastrosplenic and splenorenal ligaments; red pulp recycles red blood cells, white pulp makes lymphocytes.

Celiac trunk, branch by branch

Three branches. Splenic artery → pancreatic branches, short gastric arteries (fundus), left gastro-omental (gastroepiploic) artery (greater curvature). Left gastric artery → esophageal arteries (abdominal esophagus), lesser curvature. Common hepatic artery → splits into the proper hepatic artery (→ right/left hepatic arteries, right gastric artery, cystic artery) and the gastroduodenal artery (→ superior pancreaticoduodenal arteries, right gastro-omental artery, which anastomoses with the left gastro-omental artery from the splenic artery). Rule of thumb: gastro-omental/epiploic vessels feed the greater curvature; plain gastric vessels feed the lesser curvature.

The foregut is the mouth-side half of your "core" digestive organs: the belly-portion of your esophagus, your stomach, the first bend of small intestine, and the liver, gallbladder, pancreas, and spleen hanging off nearby. All of them share one blood supply — a single artery called the celiac trunk that splits into three branches like a tree fork.

Picture peritoneum as plastic wrap around your organs. The wrap touching the organ itself gives you vague, hard-to-point-to pain; the wrap lining the inside of your belly wall gives you sharp, exact pain. A "mesentery" is a fold of that wrap acting like a sling — it holds an organ in place and carries a bundle of blood vessels and nerves to it, like a cable running through a strap. A handful of organs (SAD PUCKER is the memory trick) are stuck to the back wall with wrap on only one side.

Your stomach is J-shaped: a domed top, a big middle where most of the acid is made, and a tight, muscular bottom valve that only lets food through once it's ground down enough — which is exactly why that first stretch of small intestine right past the valve is where ulcers most often form. The liver makes bile, the gallbladder just stores and concentrates it, and both drain into the same short stretch of pipe that the pancreas also drains into, right before it dumps everything into the small intestine. So a gallstone's exact location tells you exactly what backs up — just stored bile, all bile, or bile and pancreas juice together.

Anatomy of the Midgut & Hindgut

Midgut · SMA Hindgut · IMA
Dr. Herr

Boundaries

Midgut: duodenum (parts 2–4) → jejunum → ileum → cecum/appendix → ascending colon → roughly two-thirds of the transverse colon, all via the superior mesenteric artery (SMA). Hindgut: the last third of the transverse colon → descending colon → sigmoid colon → superior rectum, via the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA). The ligament of Treitz (suspensory ligament of the duodenum) marks the duodenojejunal flexure and is the clinical dividing line for GI bleeds: proximal to it, blood typically presents as hematemesis (bloody vomit); distal to it, as hematochezia (bloody stool).

Small intestine and cecum

Jejunum and ileum are intraperitoneal, suspended by "the mesentery proper," which carries their vessels, nerves, and fat and gives the small bowel its loose, central arrangement. The ileum empties into the cecum at the ileocecal valve. The appendix (vermiform — worm-shaped) hangs off the cecum with its own mesoappendix and appendicular artery (an ileocolic/SMA branch).

Appendicitis

A fecalith blocking the appendiceal lumen causes swelling and inflammation. Because the appendix is intraperitoneal, its pain starts as visceral afferent signaling — poorly localized, referred to the periumbilical region (T10, via the lesser splanchnic nerves) — then localizes sharply to McBurney's point (two-thirds of the way from the umbilicus to the right anterior superior iliac spine) once inflammation reaches the parietal peritoneum.

Colon landmarks

Ascending colon (retroperitoneal) → right/hepatic flexure → transverse colon (intraperitoneal, its own mesocolon; midgut→hindgut switch occurs about two-thirds of the way across) → left/splenic flexure → descending colon (retroperitoneal) → sigmoid colon → rectum. Three features distinguish large from small intestine on sight: taeniae coli (three longitudinal smooth muscle bands whose contraction shortens and puckers the colon into haustra, sacculations that reform in fixed positions) and epiploic appendages (fat tags). Intussusception — a segment telescoping into the adjacent one — cuts off blood supply and risks necrosis. Diverticulosis is normal-range outpouching of the colon wall.

Rectum and the pectinate line

The rectum is the most posterior pelvic organ, beginning at S3, with a dilated rectal ampulla and internal (smooth, involuntary) and external (skeletal, voluntary) anal sphincters; the puborectalis maintains the perineal flexure (this is what a "squatty potty" straightens out). The pectinate line, formed by the anal sinuses, is a hard boundary for exam purposes: above it — visceral motor and autonomic sensory innervation (poorly localized pain), arterial supply from the IMA (a hindgut artery), venous return to the portal system, lymphatics to internal iliac nodes; below it — somatic motor and sensory (well-localized pain), arterial supply from the internal iliac artery, venous return to the caval system, lymphatics to superficial inguinal nodes. Clinically, a rectal pro-drug placed below the line never reaches the portal circulation, so it's never hepatically activated.

Arterial supply

SMA branches: inferior pancreaticoduodenal arteries (anastomose with the celiac trunk's superior pancreaticoduodenal arteries around the pancreatic head — the foregut/midgut collateral), intestinal branches (jejunum/ileum), ileocolic artery (→ appendicular artery), right colic artery (ascending colon), middle colic artery (transverse colon). IMA branches: left colic artery (distal transverse and descending colon), sigmoid arteries, superior rectal artery. The marginal artery of Drummond is the midgut–hindgut anastomosis running along the colic arteries; the territory near the splenic flexure is a relative watershed and the first to suffer if perfusion drops.

SMA syndrome vs. Nutcracker syndrome

The SMA crosses anterior to both the third part of the duodenum and the left renal vein — two compression points against the aorta. SMA syndrome: compression of the duodenum → obstruction, needs urgent management. Nutcracker syndrome: compression of the left renal vein → backs up the left gonadal vein (which drains into the left renal vein, unlike the right gonadal vein, which drains straight into the IVC) → left flank pain, hematuria, or varicocele.

Portal vs. caval drainage and the three anastomoses

Every unpaired abdominal organ drains venous blood to the hepatic portal system → liver (detoxification) → hepatic veins → IVC. Every paired organ (kidneys, adrenals, gonads, abdominal wall) drains directly to the caval system. Where a portal-draining vein and a caval-draining vein meet, that's a porta-caval anastomosis — normally low-flow, but the pressure relief valve when the liver can't accept portal blood (portal hypertension). Three to know: esophageal veins (portal) ↔ azygos vein (caval) → esophageal varices; paraumbilical vein (portal, via the ligamentum teres) ↔ superficial abdominal veins (caval) → caput medusae; superior rectal vein (portal) ↔ inferior/middle rectal veins (caval) → internal hemorrhoids.

The midgut is the middle stretch of the tube — most of the small intestine plus the first big chunk of the large intestine — all fed by one artery (the SMA). The hindgut is the last stretch of the large intestine, fed by a different artery (the IMA). A sling called the ligament of Treitz marks the handoff point; bleeding above it usually comes up as vomit, bleeding below it usually comes out the other end.

Your appendix is a dead-end pouch off the start of the large intestine. When it gets blocked and inflamed, the pain starts out vague, around the belly button — because that's just how "hard to locate" gut pain gets wired — and only turns into a sharp, exact pain once the inflammation reaches the body-wall lining, at one specific spot (McBurney's point). You can always spot large intestine versus small intestine by three fat cords running down its length, the little pouches those cords bunch it into, and small fat tags stuck to the outside.

Near the very end of the tube there's an important dividing line inside the anal canal. Above it, everything works and feels like the rest of your gut — vague pain, drains toward the liver first. Below it, it works and feels like skin — sharp pain, drains straight into normal circulation. That's why a suppository placed too low never gets processed by the liver.

Finally: every "single" digestive organ (stomach, spleen, intestines, etc.) sends its blood through a special detour into the liver before rejoining regular circulation. When the liver is too damaged to let that blood through, it backs up and forces its way through three backup routes instead — which is why liver disease can show up as swollen veins around the belly button, fragile veins in the esophagus, or hemorrhoids.

Anterolateral & Posterior Abdominal Wall

Dr. Herr

Surface landmarks and regions

Midline landmarks: linea alba (avascular midline seam — a favored surgical entry point), umbilicus, tendinous intersections of rectus abdominis, linea semilunaris (rectus's lateral border), ASIS, pubic symphysis/tubercle. The abdomen divides into 9 regions (mid-clavicular lines + subcostal plane + intertubercular plane) or simply 4 quadrants (median sagittal + transumbilical planes, the latter at the L3/4 disc). Visceral pain is referred by embryologic origin: foregut → epigastric region, midgut → umbilical region, hindgut → pubic/suprapubic region. The transpyloric plane (halfway between jugular notch and pubic symphysis, ≈ L1) is a useful ultrasound landmark — a visibly undulating pylorus there means the patient has eaten recently.

Layers, centrally vs. laterally

Centrally (over rectus abdominis): skin → subcutaneous tissue → anterior rectus sheath → rectus abdominis → posterior rectus sheath (only above the arcuate line) → transversalis fascia → extraperitoneal fascia → parietal peritoneum. Laterally (over the flank): skin → subcutaneous tissue → external oblique (fibers down-and-in) → internal oblique (fibers up-and-in) → transversus abdominis (fibers horizontal) → transversalis fascia → extraperitoneal fascia → parietal peritoneum. At the semilunar line, all three oblique/transversus layers become aponeurotic and split to envelop the rectus — front and back — above the arcuate line; below it, all three aponeuroses pass anterior to the rectus only, the posterior wall there is just transversalis fascia, and the inferior epigastric vessels run behind the rectus. (Low transverse incisions, including cesarean sections, exploit this — one fewer tough layer to cut.)

Dermatomes and blood supply

Key levels: T2 (sternal angle), T4 (nipple line), T6 (xiphoid process), T10 (umbilicus — "belly button-TEN"), T11–T12 toward the pubic region. The abdominal wall is segmentally innervated. Arterial supply comes from two directions that anastomose behind the rectus abdominis: the internal thoracic artery (→ superior epigastric artery) from above, and the external iliac artery (→ inferior epigastric artery) from below — together forming a collateral pathway that can bypass a diseased or occluded abdominal aorta.

Umbilical folds and hernias

Five peritoneal folds converge on the umbilicus: one median umbilical fold (covers the obliterated urachus — a patent urachus can leak urine, or present as a polyp/cyst, at the umbilicus), two medial umbilical folds (cover the obliterated umbilical arteries), and two lateral umbilical folds (cover the inferior epigastric vessels). The inguinal canal (both sexes) carries the vas deferens (or round ligament of the uterus), plus nerve, blood, and lymph. Inguinal hernias are direct or indirect relative to the inferior epigastric vessels — mnemonic "MDs don't LIe": Medial to the vessels = Direct; Lateral to the vessels = Indirect. (Per this course's own materials, a direct hernia passes through the inguinal canal, medial to the vessels — worth noting since some general references phrase the direct/indirect distinction slightly differently.)

Diaphragm and posterior wall

The diaphragm pulls up on ribs and down on its central tendon as it contracts. Three openings, each at a different vertebral level — mnemonic "I ate 10 eggs at 12": caval opening (T8), esophageal hiatus (T10 — the vagal trunks travel alongside the esophagus here and can be compressed in a hiatal hernia), aortic hiatus (T12). Posterior wall muscles: quadratus lumborum (anchors the floating 12th rib), psoas major/minor and iliacus (hip flexors; together, iliopsoas). Posterior wall vasculature includes the three unpaired midline arteries already covered (celiac, SMA, IMA) plus paired arteries (inferior phrenic, middle suprarenal, renal, lumbar, gonadal, common iliac) and nerves (subcostal T12, iliohypogastric, ilioinguinal, lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh, genitofemoral).

Unlike your chest, your belly has no bone cage — just stacked layers of muscle and fibrous sheet. A tough, nearly bloodless seam runs down the middle (handy for surgeons), and pain from your stomach, intestines, or colon each show up in a different predictable spot on that map (upper-middle, around the belly button, or low-middle, respectively).

Off to the sides, three sheets of muscle are stacked like plywood, each running a different direction. Toward the middle they turn into flat tendon and wrap around your "six-pack" muscles — front and back above the belly button, but only in front below it (that dividing line is why doctors cut low for C-sections — one less tough layer). A body-wide "map," lining up skin patches to spinal nerve levels, lets you localize nerve problems just by where a patch of skin is numb or painful — belly button is a reliable landmark on that map.

A backup blood highway runs top-to-bottom just behind your six-pack, connecting the artery under your collarbone to the artery in your groin — useful if the main aorta between them gets blocked. Around the belly button, five little ridges cover fetal leftovers (an old bladder duct, two old arteries to the placenta) that normally seal shut after birth — when one doesn't, it can leak or form a cyst. Down in the groin, a short tunnel lets reproductive-tract tubing pass through the belly wall; when intestine bulges through weak spots nearby, that's a hernia, and exactly where it bulges relative to one specific pair of vessels tells you which type. Finally, the diaphragm — the big breathing muscle — has three holes punched through it at three different heights for the three things that must cross from chest to belly: a vein, the esophagus, and the main artery.

Histology of the Oral Cavity & Salivary Glands

Dr. Herr

Oral mucosa, three types

Upper GI tract (mouth, esophagus, stomach) handles swallowing and digestion; lower GI tract (small and large intestine) handles absorption and elimination. Within the mouth, three mucosa types by location and job: masticatory mucosa (gingiva, hard palate — keratinized/parakeratinized stratified squamous, built for compression), lining mucosa (lips, cheeks, floor of mouth, ventral tongue, soft palate — thicker, nonkeratinized, contains keratinocytes, Langerhans cells, melanocytes, like skin), and specialized mucosa (dorsal tongue only — papillae and taste buds). A cross-section of the lip shows orbicularis oris muscle centrally, skin with hair follicles on the outer face, and glandular oral mucosa on the inner face, with the vermilion zone marking the visible color transition between them.

Lingual papillae — the one with no taste buds is the trap

Filiform — V-shaped rows across the tongue, highly keratinized, function purely in rasping; no taste buds. Fungiform — mushroom-shaped, scattered over the tongue, parakeratinized, have taste buds. Circumvallate (vallate) — large, form the V marking the anterior two-thirds/posterior one-third division of the tongue, parakeratinized, taste buds plus adjacent serous glands of von Ebner. Foliate — ridges on the lateral tongue, parakeratinized, taste buds. Each taste bud has gustatory cells (wired to sensory nerve — CN VII for the anterior two-thirds, CN IX posterior one-third, CN X at the epiglottis), supporting cells, and basal cells that regenerate the gustatory cells.

Tooth layers

Enamel — outermost, highly mineralized and acellular, the primary mechanical/chemical barrier. Dentin — makes up the bulk of the tooth, both root and interior crown. Cementum — anchors the root into the periodontal ligament/gum.

Gland classification

Developmentally, glandular epithelium buds downward from the surface; exocrine glands keep their duct, endocrine glands lose the duct and gain a rich capillary bed instead. Salivary glands are classified three ways: structurally (tubular vs. acinar vs. tubuloacinar, each simple or compound); by secretion mechanism (merocrine = exocytosis, the salivary-gland mechanism; holocrine = whole-cell disintegration, e.g. sebaceous glands; apocrine = apical pinch-off, e.g. mammary glands); and by product (serous — protein-rich, stains dark, round nuclei; mucous — mucin-rich, stains pale, flattened basal nuclei; mixed, often mucous acini capped by serous demilunes).

Duct hierarchy — mnemonic ISE

Acinus (secretion made here) → Intercalated duct (simple squamous/cuboidal, still isotonic) → Striated duct (cuboidal-to-columnar, where NaCl is reabsorbed and K⁺/HCO₃⁻ secreted — this is where saliva becomes hypotonic) → Excretory ducts (intralobular → interlobular → lobar, progressively larger). Acinus + intercalated duct + striated duct together are called the salivon, the functional secretory unit, wrapped by contractile myoepithelial cells.

Saliva: composition and function

~1.2–1.5 L/day. Moistens and lubricates for swallowing, dissolves food so it can be tasted, buffers oral pH (~8 — protects teeth from acid, including from frequent vomiting), begins digestion (amylase for starch, minor lipase for fat), and defends the mucosa (lysozyme, lactoferrin, and secretory IgA — a dimer held together by a J chain, made by plasma cells, the primary antibody isotype in secretions). Primary saliva from the acinus is isotonic; the ductal system makes final/secondary saliva hypotonic.

Your mouth uses three different linings depending on the job: tough, scuff-resistant lining over your gums and the roof of your mouth; softer, stretchier lining over your cheeks, lips, and under your tongue; and a specialized surface only on top of your tongue, covered in bumps that carry your taste buds. Those bumps come in four shapes — tiny pointy ones for gripping/scraping that can't taste anything at all, mushroom-shaped ones that do taste, a ring of large ones near the back that also taste, and ridged ones along the sides that taste too. Each tooth is a rock-hard mineral shell (enamel) over a bony core (dentin), anchored into the gum by a third layer (cementum).

Your three sets of spit glands specialize differently: the one by your ear makes thin, watery, protein-rich spit; the one under your jaw makes the biggest daily volume, mostly watery with a little thick mucus mixed in; and the one under your tongue makes mostly thick, mucus-y spit. Inside each gland, spit is made in tiny clusters, then travels down a plumbing system that actively adjusts its saltiness — it starts out exactly as salty as your blood, but salt gets pulled back out along the way, so by the time it reaches your mouth it's less salty than blood, though still great at buffering acid. Spit moistens food for swallowing, starts breaking down starch, buffers acid so it doesn't wear down your teeth, and fights off bacteria riding in on your food.

Histology of the Esophagus & Stomach

Dr. Herr

The four layers, and the esophagus mnemonic

Same four layers everywhere in the tract — mucosa, submucosa, muscularis externa, serosa/adventitia (adventitia where the tube is fixed to a wall, e.g. much of the esophagus; serosa where it's free in the peritoneal cavity). For the esophagus specifically, the mucosa itself has three sub-layers, remembered as ELPMM: Epithelium, Lamina Propria, Muscularis Mucosa. Deep to that: submucosa (with Meissner's/submucosal plexus and, in the esophagus, glands) then muscularis externa (inner circular + Auerbach's/myenteric plexus + outer longitudinal).

Landmark: no villi means stomach

A single labeled cross-section from esophagus to colon is worth memorizing cold: esophagus has submucosal glands; the stomach has deep gastric pits/glands and never has villi (a "V" on a stomach slide usually marks a vessel, not a villus — a favorite trap); the duodenum has both villi and Brunner's glands in the submucosa; jejunum and ileum have villi (the ileum specifically adds Peyer's patches); the colon has no villi at all — just deep, straight crypts, described as "test tubes in a rack."

Esophagus specifics

Nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelium, a prominent muscularis mucosae, ~25 cm long. Two submucosal gland types: esophageal glands proper (along the length) and esophageal cardiac glands (mucus-secreting, lamina propria only, right at the terminal end). Muscularis externa is regional: upper third skeletal muscle, middle third mixed, lower third smooth muscle — so the esophagus carries both somatic and autonomic innervation depending on level.

The Z-line and the LES

The esophageal-cardiac junction (Z-line) is an abrupt, unmistakable switch from protective nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelium to secretory, mucus-producing simple columnar epithelium — visible grossly, endoscopically, and histologically. The lower esophageal sphincter combines the esophagus's own smooth muscle with a contribution from the diaphragm at the esophageal hiatus, maintaining high resting pressure to block reflux.

Stomach glands, region by region

Grossly, rugae are the mucosal/submucosal folds seen when the stomach is empty. Microscopically, the mucosa is dimpled with gastric pits leading to simple, branched, tubular gastric glands. Glandular composition shifts by region — cardiac glands (narrow zone near the esophageal orifice, mucous, protect against reflux), the classic gastric/fundic glands of the body (rich in parietal and chief cells), and pyloric glands (deeper, more mucous-cell-dominated, with scattered gastrin-secreting G cells and nearby lymphoid tissue).

Every stop on your digestive tube is built from the same four-layer "jacket," but the details shift to match the job. The esophagus is a lined chute — tough, scuff-resistant lining because its only job is getting food down, with muscle that shifts from voluntary at the top to involuntary at the bottom. The stomach's lining is dimpled with deep pits leading into glands, and here's the detail worth locking in: the stomach never has the little finger-like villi the small intestine has — if something fingerlike shows up in a stomach photo, it's either a blood vessel or you're actually looking at the duodenum. The instant the stomach hands off to the duodenum, another sharp switch happens — the pits vanish and true villi (plus a gland found nowhere else) appear immediately.

GI Hormones

Dr. Latimer · full reference tables live in the

Three ways a signal can travel

Every receptor discussed in this lecture is a GPCR. Endocrine — released from a GI endocrine cell into blood, acts on a target elsewhere (still "local" in practice, since the target is usually still within the GI system). Paracrine — released and diffuses to a neighboring cell. Neurocrine — released from a firing neuron onto its target. The same molecule (CCK is the standing example) can act in more than one mode; the classification depends on the release context, not a fixed property of the molecule.

Six endocrine hormones — headlines

Gastrin (G cells) drives acid secretion and mucosal growth. CCK (I cells) contracts the gallbladder, drives pancreatic enzyme release, and slows gastric emptying so fat has time to digest. Secretin (S cells) drives pancreatic/biliary bicarbonate and inhibits acid. GIP and GLP-1 (K and L cells — the "incretins") both potentiate insulin release after a meal; GLP-1 also slows gastric emptying and suppresses appetite. These two are the physiological basis of the GLP-1/GIP-agonist weight-loss drugs (semaglutide/Ozempic-Wegovy, tirzepatide/Mounjaro, liraglutide/Saxenda-Victoza) — GLP-1 mimetics trace back to a 1990s discovery in Gila monster venom. Motilin (M cells) triggers the migrating myoelectric complex during fasting — its receptor also binds erythromycin, the basis for using erythromycin as a prokinetic. Ghrelin (P cells, gastric fundus) is the "hunger hormone" — high when fasting or sleep-deprived, low when fed, stressed, or depressed.

Two paracrines and the neurocrines

Histamine (ECL cells) potentiates acid secretion via H2 receptors — the target of H2-blocker drugs. Somatostatin (D cells) is the main "off switch," released once antral pH drops below 3, shutting down neighboring G cells. Among the neurocrines: substance P (excitatory — contracts smooth muscle, boosts blood flow, stimulates secretion), VIP and nitric oxide (inhibitory, co-released — relax smooth muscle and sphincters), enkephalins (decrease motility, the physiologic basis of opioid-induced constipation), and GRP (the vagal signal that tells G cells to release gastrin). The peristaltic reflex is these working together: substance P contracts the muscle behind a bolus while VIP/NO relax the muscle ahead of it, in one coordinated reflex.

Worth rememberingAcetylcholine and norepinephrine behave "backwards" from their whole-body reputations here: ACh ("rest and digest") speeds the gut up; norepinephrine ("fight or flight") slows it down — consistent with not digesting while running from a threat.

Your gut sends chemical messages three ways: shout it into the blood so faraway cells hear it, whisper it to the cell next door, or fire it straight across a nerve junction — and the same molecule can do any of these depending on where it's released. Six hormones are worth knowing by their headline job: one ramps up stomach acid, one squeezes the gallbladder and stalls the stomach so fat has time to digest, one tells the pancreas to send antacid, two boost insulin after a meal (exactly what drugs like Ozempic and Mounjaro copy), one sweeps your gut clean between meals, and one is your hunger signal, rising when you're starving and falling when you're full or stressed. Two "whisper to the neighbor" chemicals matter most: one that revs acid-making cells up (blocked by heartburn medicine) and one that's the main "off switch" for acid. And a few "fire across the nerve" messengers coordinate the actual squeezing motion — one tells the muscle behind a bit of food to squeeze while two others simultaneously tell the muscle ahead to relax, pushing food in one direction instead of just squishing it. Quirky detail: the "calm down" nerve chemical actually speeds your gut up, and the "fight or flight" one slows it down — you don't want to digest lunch while running from a bear.

Salivary & Gastric Secretion

Dr. Fuller

Making primary saliva

At the acinar cell's basolateral membrane, the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase sets up ionic gradients that drive a Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ cotransporter, loading the cell with chloride (K⁺ recycles out through a basolateral channel; Na⁺ is pumped back out) — chloride accumulates well above what the Nernst equation alone would predict. On stimulation (ACh via a muscarinic receptor → rising intracellular Ca²⁺), an apical Cl⁻ channel opens; Cl⁻ exits into the lumen, drags Na⁺ with it paracellularly, and water follows through acinar-cell aquaporins (AQP5) — producing an isotonic primary saliva.

Modifying it in the duct

The ductal epithelium has no water permeability at all. As primary saliva flows down the striated duct, the duct cells reabsorb Na⁺ and Cl⁻ and secrete K⁺ and HCO₃⁻ — removing ions without removing water, so final saliva is hypotonic and bicarbonate-rich (~pH 8). At very high flow rates, the ductal Cl⁻ transporters hit their transport maximum, so some Cl⁻ escapes reabsorption and creeps back up in the final saliva — but total ion content stays below plasma regardless of flow rate.

Regulation is 100% neural — and both branches agree

Unlike almost every other tissue, salivary glands are stimulated by both parasympathetic (ACh, substance P → Ca²⁺/PLC pathway) and sympathetic (norepinephrine/epinephrine → cAMP/PKA pathway) input — no opposition, no hormonal input at all. Parasympathetic loss atrophies the gland; sympathetic loss has a milder effect, so parasympathetic input is functionally dominant. Controlled by a salivary center in the medulla; the classic conditioned reflex (Pavlov's dogs) runs through this same circuit. Sjögren's syndrome is an autoimmune lymphocytic infiltration of the salivary and lacrimal glands — xerostomia, dry eyes, poor dentition, gland swelling, difficulty swallowing/speaking/tasting; chronic and progressive, with only symptomatic treatment.

Parietal cell remodeling and acid output

At rest, parietal cells store tubulovesicles and abundant mitochondria; on stimulation they remodel over ~10 minutes into an intracellular canalicular system with microvilli, massively expanding the apical proton-pump surface. Gastric HCl runs 100–200 mM, pH 0.8–1. At rest, gastric secretion is mostly NaCl (surface epithelial cells); once stimulated it becomes nearly pure HCl — not a modification of one secretion, but two separate secretions whose mixture shifts as the acid-rich one dominates.

How the parietal cell makes HCl

The apical H⁺/K⁺-ATPase exchanges one H⁺ out for one K⁺ in (electroneutral; K⁺ recycles back out through an apical channel). Intracellularly, carbonic anhydrase converts CO₂ + H₂O → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻; the H⁺ feeds the pump, while the HCO₃⁻ leaves the basolateral membrane via a Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchanger (importing Cl⁻); that Cl⁻ then exits an apical Cl⁻ channel to pair with H⁺ as HCl. The HCO₃⁻ dumped into venous blood during active secretion causes a small, measurable rise in blood pH leaving the stomach — the alkaline tide.

Three stimulators, two inhibitors, one synergy

Gastrin (endocrine, CCK-B receptor), acetylcholine (neurocrine, muscarinic) and histamine (paracrine, H2 receptor) all converge on the parietal cell — gastrin and ACh via Ca²⁺/PLC, histamine via cAMP/PKA — and act synergistically, more potent together than the sum of each alone (which is why blocking just histamine with an H2 blocker still meaningfully reduces acid). Somatostatin and prostaglandins inhibit via Gi, lowering cAMP. Gastrin and ACh also stimulate ECL cells to release histamine — knockout studies suggest this histamine relay may be gastrin's dominant mechanism of action.

Turning acid off

Vagal ACh → an excitatory interneuron releasing GRP → G cells release gastrin, while a separate inhibitory vagal branch keeps antral D cells suppressed. As the stomach empties and antral pH falls below 3, that acidity directly triggers D cells to release somatostatin, which shuts down the neighboring G cells — gastrin falls, acid secretion falls. Right after a meal, food buffers keep luminal [H⁺] low even as the secretion rate ramps up; hours later, once buffers are gone, [H⁺] rises — which is exactly the signal that shuts secretion back down via somatostatin.

Mucus/bicarbonate barrier, pepsinogen, intrinsic factor

Surface and neck mucous cells secrete a mucus gel that traps a bicarbonate layer against the epithelium, holding surface pH near 7 even with a luminal pH of 1–2 — stimulated by the vagus and prostaglandins (how acid itself gets safely through this layer to the lumen isn't fully settled). Pepsinogen, from chief cells, is an inactive precursor cleaved to active pepsin by existing acid/pepsin once in the lumen; secretion is stimulated by vagal input and by secretin. Intrinsic factor, from parietal cells, is the only route for vitamin B12 absorption: dietary B12 binds salivary R-proteins in the stomach, is handed off to intrinsic factor once R-proteins are digested away in the duodenum, and the IF–B12 complex is taken up by receptor-mediated endocytosis in the terminal ileum. B12 stores in the liver for ~5 years, which is why intrinsic factor loss doesn't cause symptoms (pernicious anemia) until years later.

Spit and stomach acid are both made the same clever way: a "rough draft" fluid first, refined as it flows through a pipe. Spit's rough draft is just salt water at blood strength; as it flows down the duct, salt gets pulled back out (without any water following) and swapped for bicarbonate, so what reaches your mouth is weaker in salt than blood but great at buffering acid. Oddly, both your "rev up" and "calm down" nerve branches tell your spit glands to make more spit — no hormones involved at all, purely nerve-controlled. An autoimmune disease called Sjögren's attacks these same glands (and the nearly identical tear glands), causing permanent dryness.

Stomach acid works in reverse: an energy-hungry pump trades one potassium for one hydrogen at a time, dumping a leftover (bicarbonate) into your blood — which is why blood leaving a digesting stomach is briefly, measurably less acidic. Three separate signals — a hormone in the blood, a nerve signal, and a neighboring cell's chemical — all push acid-making cells to work harder, and are much stronger together than any one alone. There's a built-in off switch too: once your stomach empties and there's nothing left to soak up the acid, the rising acidity itself triggers a stop signal. Your stomach lining survives its own acid because a gel layer of mucus traps a nearly neutral cushion right against the cells, even while the open stomach nearby is more acidic than battery acid. And one particular stomach protein (intrinsic factor) is the only way your body can absorb vitamin B12 — losing it takes years to notice, since your liver has years of B12 already stored away.

GI Motility

Dr. Fuller

Chewing and swallowing

Chewing mixes food with saliva (initiating amylase digestion) and aids taste; the only real requirement is reducing particle size enough to swallow safely. Swallowing starts voluntarily (tongue rolls the bolus against the hard palate, propels it toward the pharynx) then becomes an involuntary reflex once pharyngeal pressure receptors fire, coordinated by the medullary swallowing center: soft palate elevates to seal the nasopharynx, epiglottis folds to protect the airway, the upper esophageal sphincter relaxes, and pharyngeal muscles contract to drive the bolus into the esophagus. An extremely robust reflex — works upside down, works in zero gravity.

Esophageal peristalsis

Transit takes ~10 seconds. Primary peristalsis is the main swallow-triggered wave (distension → contraction above/relaxation below), occasionally consciously perceptible. Secondary peristalsis is a local, stretch-triggered cleanup wave for anything primary peristalsis missed — never consciously felt. The LES stays contracted at rest and relaxes via enteric neurons releasing VIP and nitric oxide (plus some vagal input) to admit each bolus. Achalasia — myenteric plexus loss prevents LES relaxation, food backs up, the esophagus dilates massively (classic "bird-beak" on barium swallow). GERD is not a primary motility disorder — it's excess transient LES relaxations letting reflux occur; untreated, it risks Barrett's esophagus and adenocarcinoma.

Slow waves — the gut's basic electrical rhythm

Rhythmic oscillations in smooth-muscle resting membrane potential, generated by the interstitial cells of Cajal (pacemaker cells sitting between the myenteric plexus and the muscle, gap-junction coupled to it). Frequency is a fixed, tissue-intrinsic property (~3/min stomach, ~12–18/min small intestine, ~6/min colon — demonstrated by transplant experiments preserving the donor tissue's native rate) and runs continuously regardless of feeding state. A slow wave alone doesn't guarantee a contraction — only if depolarization reaches threshold do true action potentials ("spike potentials") fire on top of it, admitting more Ca²⁺ and producing an actual contraction; bigger depolarization → more spikes → stronger contraction. Stretch, ACh, and parasympathetic/hormonal input raise the resting baseline (more excitable); norepinephrine/sympathetic input lowers it.

Gastric emptying

Receptive relaxation lets the fundus accommodate incoming food with no pressure rise until a large volume is reached (a vagovagal reflex, lost after vagotomy). Antral grinding reduces chyme to ≲2 mm particles; retropulsion — a contraction wave overtaking chyme and closing the pylorus first — throws unground material back for another pass, so material exits in small jets. Slows emptying: fat/CCK, high GIP, hypo/hypertonicity, duodenal acid or pressure, large particles, some amino acids. Speeds emptying: large volume, liquids, duodenal relaxation, a wider pyloric opening. The ileal brake: L cells in the distal ileum detect unabsorbed nutrients (especially fat, which should normally finish absorbing by the end of the jejunum) and signal back to slow gastric emptying further.

Small intestine: MMCs, segmentation, peristalsis

Migrating myoelectric complexes (MMCs) — cyclical (~every 90 min) bursts of strong contraction seen only during fasting, sweeping from stomach to ileocecal valve as a "housekeeping" wave, driven by motilin (essentially its only known job). Immediately abolished by eating — IV feeding does not suppress them, only food in the lumen does. The motilin receptor also binds erythromycin, explaining both its GI-cramping side effect and its clinical use as a prokinetic in gastroparesis. Segmentation mixes chyme locally without net forward movement (myenteric-nerve controlled); peristalsis moves content forward — too fast causes diarrhea, and reverse peristalsis drives vomiting.

Large intestine and defecation

Total transit ~30–40 hours; final major site of fluid reabsorption (~500 mL/day delivered, ~400 mL reabsorbed). The ileocecal valve opens via a short-range ENS reflex when the ileum is stretched, and stays shut if the cecum itself is already stretched (the coloileal reflex). Haustra (segmentation-like sacculations that reform in fixed locations) and taeniae coli (three external longitudinal muscle bands) are unique to the colon. A few times daily, a mass movement sweeps 10–20 cm of colon at once, triggered by gastric distension — the gastrocolic reflex, a long-range extrinsic reflex (too far for local ENS alone; needs vagal input, likely with gastrin). The related gastroileal reflex relaxes the ileocecal valve on gastric filling, clearing room downstream. The defecation (rectosphincteric) reflex: rectal filling to ~25% triggers involuntary internal anal sphincter relaxation paired with voluntary external sphincter contraction that can override the urge. Hirschsprung's disease — congenital absence of myenteric plexus in a colon segment (associated with trisomy 21) — is the colonic analog of achalasia: that segment can't relax, causing massive proximal dilation and newborn obstruction.

Chewing's only real job is breaking food small enough to swallow safely — no magic chew-count required. Swallowing starts as a choice, then a reflex takes over completely the instant food hits the back of your throat: your nose seals off, a flap folds down to protect your windpipe, and muscles force the food down — forcefully enough to work upside down or in zero gravity. The esophagus then pushes it down with a traveling squeeze (about 10 seconds, like squeezing toothpaste down a tube); a backup wave you never feel cleans up anything left behind. A ring of muscle at the bottom stays clamped shut to block acid reflux and only opens for each swallow — when it won't open at all, food backs up (achalasia); when it's too loose, acid leaks upward (reflux).

Your gut muscle runs on a built-in, always-on electrical rhythm — like a heartbeat for squeezing — at its own fixed speed depending on location, generated by dedicated pacemaker cells. That rhythm hums constantly but only becomes an actual squeeze once something pushes the muscle excitable enough to cross a threshold, which is why your gut squeezes hard after a big meal and barely at all while you sleep.

Your stomach relaxes first to make room for incoming food, then grinds it into gritty slurry before letting tiny jets through to the small intestine — anything too chunky gets bounced back for more grinding, and fatty meals empty slowest because fat takes longest to digest. Between meals, a totally different "housekeeping" wave sweeps through every 90 minutes to clear out debris — it switches off the instant you eat, and only real food (not an IV drip) can trigger that switch. Inside the small intestine, muscle either mixes food in place (like kneading dough, to maximize absorption) or pushes it forward — too much forward push too fast is diarrhea, reverse is vomiting. Your large intestine moves much slower, mainly squeezing water back out as it packs waste from mush into solid stool over about a day and a half. Eating a meal itself triggers your colon to make a big shove of its own, which is why many people feel the urge to go soon after eating. And the final "go" reflex is a neat two-part trick: filling the rectum automatically opens the inner gate, but a separate, voluntary outer gate lets you hold it until it's a good time.

Salivary gland architecture

The full parotid / submandibular / sublingual comparison lives in the Lecture 5 summary — this is the duct order and classification logic underneath it.

The secretory unit: the salivon

  1. Acinus — where secretion is actually made
  2. Intercalated duct — simple squamous/cuboidal; saliva is still isotonic here
  3. Striated duct — cuboidal-to-columnar; reabsorbs Na⁺/Cl⁻, secretes K⁺/HCO₃⁻ — this is where saliva turns hypotonic
  4. Excretory ducts — intralobular → interlobular → lobar, progressively larger, no further ion transport

Acinus + intercalated duct + striated duct together make up the salivon — the full functional unit, wrapped in contractile myoepithelial cells that squeeze secretion forward.

By structureTubular, acinar, or tubuloacinar — each simple or compound

By productSerous (dark-staining, round nuclei) · mucous (pale, flattened nuclei) · mixed (mucous acini capped by serous demilunes)

Commonly confused pairs

Achalasia vs. GERDAchalasia: LES won't relax (myenteric plexus loss) — food backs up, "bird-beak" esophagus. GERD: LES relaxes too often — opposite mechanism, same neighborhood of symptoms.
Segmentation vs. peristalsisSegmentation mixes chyme in place, no net forward movement. Peristalsis pushes content forward — too fast causes diarrhea, reversed causes vomiting.
MMC vs. mass movementMMC: fasting-only, motilin-driven, sweeps stomach→ileocecal valve every ~90 min. Mass movement: a few times daily, gastrocolic-reflex-triggered, sweeps 10–20 cm of colon at once.
Primary vs. secondary peristalsisPrimary: the main swallow-triggered wave, sometimes consciously felt. Secondary: a local stretch-triggered cleanup wave for whatever primary missed — never consciously felt.
R-proteins vs. intrinsic factorSalivary R-proteins bind B12 first, in the stomach. Intrinsic factor (parietal cells) only takes over once R-proteins are digested away in the duodenum — the "who's carrying B12" answer changes mid-trip.
SMA syndrome vs. Nutcracker syndromeSame artery, two different structures pinned against the aorta: the duodenum (SMA syndrome → obstruction) vs. the left renal vein (Nutcracker → flank pain/hematuria/varicocele). Full table in the Anatomy tab.
Direct vs. indirect inguinal hernia"MDs don't LIe" — medial to the inferior epigastric vessels = direct, lateral = indirect. This course's materials describe direct hernias as passing through the inguinal canal; flagged as worth confirming (see Quiz tab, question S22).
Rugae vs. plicae vs. villi vs. haustraFour "folds," four different organs — rugae (empty stomach), plicae circulares (small intestine, macroscopic), villi (small intestine, microscopic), haustra (colon, from taeniae coli tone).
Endocrine vs. paracrine vs. neurocrineSame three questions every time: where's it released, how far does it travel, what carries it there. Full breakdown with worked examples in the Hormones tab.

Get the wording exact
The four functions are Secretion, Absorption, Motility, and Protection
Not "Digestion" — that's the common textbook framing, but it's not this course's fourth function. Protection covers the mucosal barrier, mucus, luminal pH maintenance, and GALT. Crypts secrete, villus tips absorb — and the gene expression flips as the cell migrates
