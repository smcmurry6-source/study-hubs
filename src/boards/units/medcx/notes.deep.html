  2. For adult dental care, use the ADA guide's repeat-and-refer steps when a reading is above 160/100 mm Hg.

The medical history and ASA status

A useful medical history is an interview about what changes the patient's risk today. Confirm the diagnosis in the patient's own terms, the current medicines and schedules, recent changes, allergies and the care team's instructions. Ask what limits normal activity and whether symptoms have changed; controlled and poorly controlled disease can fall into different ASA classes.

The ASA Physical Status classification is a pre-anesthesia description of systemic health. It does not encode the dental procedure or predict an individual's outcome. ASA I means a normal healthy patient. ASA II covers mild systemic illness without substantive functional limitation, such as controlled hypertension or diabetes, mild lung disease, pregnancy, obesity with BMI over 30 and under 40, current smoking or social alcohol use.

ASA III means severe systemic disease with a substantive functional limitation. Examples include poorly controlled diabetes or hypertension, COPD, BMI of at least 40, active hepatitis, regularly scheduled dialysis, an implanted pacemaker, moderately reduced ejection fraction, or a history of MI, stroke, TIA or coronary stents more than three months ago. The examples help orient the class; the whole history still matters.

ASA IV means severe disease that is a constant threat to life. Examples include MI, stroke, TIA or coronary stent within the past three months, ongoing ischemia, severe valve dysfunction, severely reduced ejection fraction, sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, acute respiratory distress or ESRD without regular dialysis. ASA V describes a moribund patient not expected to survive without the operation, and ASA VI a declared brain-dead organ donor.

Append E to classes I through V for an emergency in which delay would significantly increase the threat to life or a body part. The emergency modifier records urgency; it does not make the underlying physical-status class less severe.

Periodontitis, cardiovascular disease and diabetes often coexist, but their association does not establish that one directly causes the other. A history can surface modifiable risks such as smoking and diet, and periodontal treatment includes risk-factor management such as diabetes control. Keep this association distinct from an individual patient's diagnosis.

Vital signs and blood pressure

Record the blood pressure as systolic over diastolic and interpret the reading in context. The 2017 ACC/AHA categories are normal below 120 and below 80, elevated at systolic 120–129 with diastolic below 80, stage 1 at 130–139 or 80–89, stage 2 at 140 or higher or 90 or higher, and crisis above 180 and/or above 120 mm Hg. Use the more severe category when the two components differ.

One office reading does not diagnose chronic hypertension. Diagnosis needs an average of at least two elevated readings on at least two occasions. The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline kept the 2017 definition of hypertension (130/80) and the stage 1 range; the crisis numbers in these notes are the 2017 ACC/AHA ones.

The ADA Practical Guide is a separate dental-care framework. Below 160/100 mm Hg, it recommends no modification for elective or emergency care. Above 160/100, repeat the reading; if it falls or fits written physician guidance, proceed, while a confirmed elevation means no elective care and referral to the physician. For a pain-driven emergency at confirmed 160–180 systolic and/or 100–109 diastolic, the guide describes treatment with anxiety reduction and blood-pressure checks every 10–15 minutes. At more than 180 systolic and/or 109 diastolic, consult the physician before proceeding.

The ADA table has an internal mismatch: the body uses more than 109 diastolic for consultation before proceeding, while its footnote uses more than 100 for referral. Treat that as a source limitation and avoid 101–109 diastolic as the only fact distinguishing the pathways.

The 2017 ACC/AHA recommendation about considering deferral at 180 systolic or 110 diastolic is for elective major surgery. It is not the same threshold as the ADA dental guide, and a 2020 JADA review notes there is no evidence-based single blood-pressure cutoff for deferring dental care. So 180/110 is the ACC/AHA surgical number, and 160/100 is the ADA dental one.

Cardiovascular disease and endocarditis prophylaxis

The AHA 2021 statement kept the limited approach from 2007: prophylaxis is reserved for four groups at highest risk of a poor outcome from viridans streptococcal infective endocarditis. These are prosthetic cardiac valves or listed prosthetic material; previous infective endocarditis; specified congenital heart disease; and cardiac transplant recipients who develop valvulopathy.

The congenital-heart group is specific. It includes unrepaired cyanotic disease, repaired disease with residual defects adjacent to prosthetic material, and the first six months after complete repair with prosthetic material or a device. It also includes a surgical or transcatheter pulmonary artery valve or conduit. A pacemaker, ICD, coronary stent, peripheral vascular graft, native valve disease, or a septal closure device after complete closure is not included solely because it is implanted or cardiac.

For a patient in one of the high-risk groups, prophylaxis is recommended for dental procedures that manipulate gingiva, manipulate the periapical region of a tooth or perforate oral mucosa. Radiographs, injections through noninfected tissue, removable appliances, orthodontic adjustments, bracket placement, primary-tooth shedding and bleeding from accidental lip trauma are not indications.

The AHA regimen is one dose 30–60 minutes before treatment. For an adult able to take oral medication, that is amoxicillin 2 g. The doses here come from the AHA statement's table; the ADA's summary lists the same drug choices.

When penicillin allergy is reported, select an alternative according to the reaction and oral access. The AHA table includes cephalexin, azithromycin or clarithromycin, and doxycycline for oral use; ampicillin, cefazolin or ceftriaxone are parenteral alternatives when oral medication cannot be taken. Do not use a cephalosporin after a penicillin or ampicillin reaction of anaphylaxis, angioedema or urticaria. Clindamycin was removed from the recommended regimens because of more frequent and severe adverse reactions.

If an eligible patient forgot the preprocedure dose, it may still be given up to two hours after the procedure. A patient already taking a short antibiotic course should receive a different class when prophylaxis is needed. The AHA statement also suggests, when possible, waiting about 10 days after a course ends before elective care.

For patients with significant cardiovascular disease, a common recommendation is an epinephrine ceiling of 0.04 mg. The ADA hypertension page equates that amount to one 1:50,000 cartridge, two 1:100,000 cartridges or four 1:200,000 cartridges. A separate review described up to four 1:100,000 lidocaine cartridges as relatively safe in a cardiovascular-compromised group; that review is not the common 0.04 mg teaching limit, so the statements should not be collapsed into one threshold.

Product labels warn that epinephrine with nonselective beta-blockers, tricyclic antidepressants or monoamine oxidase inhibitors can produce severe or prolonged blood-pressure effects. With implanted cardiac electronic devices, electrosurgery carries the greatest interference risk, while piezoelectric scalers may be safer than magnetostrictive devices.

Prosthetic joints

The presence of a prosthetic hip, knee or other joint does not automatically justify antibiotic prophylaxis before dental treatment. The ADA's 2015 guideline, based on a systematic review, found that dental procedures were not linked to infections of prosthetic joints, and it recommends against routine prophylaxis.

The exception is a patient with prior complications from joint-replacement surgery who needs a procedure involving gingival manipulation or mucosal incision. In that situation, discuss the risks with the patient and orthopedic surgeon. If prophylaxis is considered necessary, the orthopedic surgeon should recommend the regimen and write the prescription when reasonable.

The ADA's 2026 position leaves the timing of care, and the rare decision to premedicate, to the dentist, the orthopedic team and the patient together. A routine waiting period after joint replacement and automatic premedication are not supported by this position. Keep the joint-prosthesis question separate from infective-endocarditis prophylaxis: a prosthetic cardiac valve is an IE high-risk condition, but a prosthetic joint alone is not.

Bleeding risk and anticoagulation

First establish the medicine, indication, dose schedule, procedure and consequences of interruption. Anticoagulants prevent thromboembolism; a local oral bleed is usually visible and can be managed, while stroke, myocardial infarction or stent thrombosis can be life-threatening. Do not tell a patient to stop a prescribed antithrombotic drug without coordination with its prescriber.

For a simple extraction, the usual teaching rule is to continue regular warfarin when INR is 3.5 or less and consult the physician above that. The usual therapeutic INR is 2–3. Use a recent INR, checked as the patient's physician directs; no fixed time window is given here.

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have fixed dosing and shorter half-lives than warfarin, and routine INR testing does not measure their effect. For most dental care, leave the regimen unchanged. If a procedure has higher bleeding risk, the physician may choose to delay the dose until after treatment, schedule late after the last dose, or interrupt treatment briefly. Interruption, when chosen, is usually 24–48 hours, and it is the physician's decision, not the dentist's.

Do not stop aspirin or a P2Y12 antiplatelet drug for routine dental care solely to reduce bleeding. This includes dual therapy. When a coronary-stent patient is taking dual antiplatelet therapy, contact the cardiologist before any change because early stopping can lead to stent thrombosis, MI and death.

Local hemostasis can include firm pressure, absorbable gelatin sponge, oxidized cellulose, sutures and antifibrinolytic treatment such as tranexamic acid. Explain post-operative instructions and arrange follow-up when the patient's disease or procedure raises risk. The WFH guidelines note that tranexamic acid is cleared by the kidneys, so a systemic dose is reduced in renal impairment; the medical team sets it.

Hemophilia A is factor VIII deficiency and hemophilia B is factor IX deficiency. Severity categories are based on circulating factor: severe is below 1% of normal, moderate 1–5%, and mild 5 to below 40%. The category describes bleeding tendency; it does not supply a universal dental extraction target. Plan invasive care with the hematologist, who selects any factor replacement or other systemic support.

Injections with vasoconstrictor, slow delivery and a fine needle are usually low risk in hemophilia. Inferior alveolar blocks, superior alveolar blocks, and injections into the floor of mouth or vascular lingual tissue carry more risk of a deep hematoma; systemic hemostatic measures must be coordinated before these injections. Buccal infiltration or a single-tooth intraligamentary injection can be alternatives to a mandibular block.

Von Willebrand disease affects platelet adhesion and factor VIII carriage. For minor mucosal procedures, the ASH/ISTH/NHF/WFH guideline conditionally suggests bringing VWF activity up to 0.50 IU/mL or higher (by desmopressin or a factor concentrate), combined with tranexamic acid. In a patient with type 1 disease, baseline VWF above 0.30 IU/mL and a mild bleeding phenotype, tranexamic acid alone may be used. The recommendations have very low-certainty evidence; coordinate with the hematologist, and do not use desmopressin for type 2B or type 3 disease.

Diabetes

Diabetes is diagnosed by laboratory criteria. A1C under 5.7% is normal, 5.7–6.4% is the prediabetes range and 6.5% or higher meets the diabetes threshold. Fasting plasma glucose is normal at 99 mg/dL or less, in the prediabetes range at 100–125, and in the diabetes range at 126 or higher. A 2-hour glucose tolerance result of 200 mg/dL or higher meets the diabetes threshold. A random result of 200 mg/dL or higher qualifies when classic symptoms are present.

A1C reflects average glucose over roughly three months. Many patients have a goal below 7%, but the goal should be individualized; a less stringent goal can be safer for someone with severe hypoglycemia or limited life expectancy. Ask about the most recent A1C rather than assuming that all patients share one target.

Hypoglycemia means glucose below 70 mg/dL. Early signs may include sweating, shakiness, hunger, palpitations, dizziness, tingling around the lips, blurred vision or confusion. A severe episode can leave the patient unable to eat or drink, unconscious or seizing.

For a conscious patient who can swallow, give 15–20 g of fast carbohydrate, wait 15 minutes, recheck glucose and repeat until it is above 70 mg/dL. Examples include four glucose tablets, one tube of glucose gel, four ounces of juice or regular soda, or a tablespoon of sugar or honey. For kidney disease, use apple, grape or cranberry juice rather than orange juice because of potassium.

If the patient is unconscious or cannot swallow, give nothing by mouth, call EMS and use glucagon if available. The injection label supports 1 mg for adults and children at least 20 kg, or 0.5 mg below 20 kg; a second equal dose may be given after 15 minutes if there is no response while waiting for EMS. Turn the patient on the side, and offer oral carbohydrate only once swallowing is safe.

Periodontitis and type 2 diabetes are associated in both directions. Periodontitis is linked with higher A1C, while scaling and root planing lowers A1C at three months, with a greater reduction reported at six months. This supports asking about the patient's current diabetes control; it does not establish that periodontal treatment replaces diabetes care.

Asthma and COPD

For asthma, ask what triggers wheezing and which medicines the patient uses. A need for a short-acting beta-2 agonist more than two days per week generally indicates asthma is not well controlled. Triggers include stress, cold air, irritants, sulfites, aspirin and other NSAIDs, and beta-blockers. The medication-count and rescue-frequency rule is a clinical guide, not a substitute for the patient's asthma action plan.

Ask specifically whether aspirin or another NSAID has caused bronchoconstriction. A patient who reacted to one can react severely to other NSAIDs because of cross-reactivity. In known aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, acetaminophen is an alternative that usually does not trigger acute bronchoconstriction, although cross-sensitivity is reported in a minority. Do not generalize the avoidance rule to every patient with asthma.

Have the patient bring a rescue inhaler to each appointment and keep it accessible. Defer treatment for active wheezing or poorly controlled asthma. An inhaled corticosteroid such as fluticasone prevents inflammation but is not the rescue drug for an acute attack; rinsing and spitting after use reduces oral candidiasis, and a spacer also reduces deposition in the mouth.

For acute bronchospasm, position the conscious patient upright as comfortable, give supplemental oxygen, and administer inhaled albuterol, a short-acting beta-2 agonist. Dental reviews describe 2–4 inhalations, while the label gives two inhalations every 4–6 hours; UK dental guidance permits up to ten puffs through a spacer. Those doses come from different sources, so an exact puff count depends on which source you follow. Aminophylline and corticosteroids are not used for the initial rescue: corticosteroids take hours to work.

If the patient is too distressed to use an inhaler during a severe attack, dental CE reviews support IM epinephrine 0.3 mg for an adult and 0.15 mg for a child. If airway swelling accompanies bronchospasm, treat the event as anaphylaxis. A severe attack that worsens or does not improve quickly and lastingly with albuterol needs emergency medical care.

In COPD, position a conscious patient upright because a flat position can reduce oxygenation. Give oxygen when needed, but titrate it rather than withholding it or using uncontrolled high flow in a patient at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure. The British Thoracic Society target for this group is 88–92% pending blood gases; a US dental CE review uses above 90%. The saturation targets differ, so know which number belongs to which source.

Use opioids, benzodiazepines and other respiratory depressants cautiously in significant COPD or compromised respiratory reserve. Nitrous oxide is not contraindicated in asthma by the AAPD guidance, while severe obstructive disease or emphysema warrants specialist consultation before nitrous oxide/oxygen. A stable patient with asthma and a patient with acute wheeze therefore need different decisions.

Kidney, liver, thyroid and adrenal disease

Chronic kidney disease is kidney structure or function abnormality lasting at least three months, with implications for health. GFR categories run from G1 at 90 mL/min/1.73 m² or higher through G5 below 15; G1 or G2 alone does not establish CKD without evidence of kidney damage. In dental prescribing, use GFR to review renal drug clearance and weigh benefit against harm.

NSAIDs can reduce kidney blood flow and lower GFR, increasing acute kidney injury risk in patients with CKD, diabetes or hypertension. Avoid routine over-the-counter NSAID use in CKD and consider acetaminophen as a non-nephrotoxic option. This is not an absolute ban: KDIGO notes judicious NSAID use under nephrology supervision can be preferable to opioids in some cases.

Medication adjustment depends on the specific drug and renal function. The amoxicillin label, for example, says not to use the 875 mg dose below GFR 30 mL/min and gives longer intervals at lower GFR; those are infection-treatment doses and do not apply to the single IE prophylaxis dose. Doxycycline and lidocaine are little affected by renal impairment, though lidocaine metabolites may accumulate.

Patients receiving hemodialysis are anticoagulated during treatment. Dental reviews commonly schedule invasive care on a non-dialysis day, preferably the day after dialysis, but high-quality evidence for the precise timing is limited. Coordinate with the nephrologist. The ADA/AHA IE list does not make dialysis or an arteriovenous graft an independent prophylaxis indication.

Liver disease can reduce clotting factor production and alter metabolism of local anesthetics and other drugs. The ADA dental guidance supports physician consultation and coagulation review before invasive procedures. Cirrhosis is a special case: its hemostasis is rebalanced but fragile, and INR and aPTT do not accurately predict bleeding. Do not use a cirrhosis INR cutoff to decide whether extraction is safe.

A platelet count of 50,000/µL has traditionally been recommended before procedures that may bleed, but current evidence does not make a lower count an automatic contraindication. Assess the whole patient and procedure. During cancer therapy, sources use other thresholds: the archived NIDCR guide 75,000/mm³ and the pediatric AAPD guidance 60,000/mm³.

For cirrhosis, narrative reviews favor acetaminophen at a reduced daily amount, about 2 g or less; the reviews differ, and the lower ceiling is the safer shared statement. Avoid routine NSAIDs because of renal, hepatorenal and gastrointestinal bleeding risk. The over-the-counter label's 4,000 mg liver-damage warning is not this product's directions maximum, and liver disease warrants clinician advice.

Amide local anesthetics are metabolized by the liver. Severe liver disease can prolong lidocaine half-life twofold or more and raise toxic plasma concentrations, but neither the lidocaine nor the articaine label gives a reduced maximum cartridge dose. Use caution.

Thyrotoxicosis can make vasoconstrictor-containing local anesthetics more hazardous. A dental review supports normal concentrations in well-managed disease and avoiding epinephrine in untreated or poorly managed thyrotoxicosis; no thyroid-specific numeric limit is set. Elective surgery should wait until uncontrolled hyperthyroidism is treated, while an acute infection still needs care.

Hypothyroidism can increase sensitivity to central nervous system depressants. More important in an emergency: the Endocrine Society guideline warns that current or recent glucocorticoid use can cause adrenal insufficiency, and adrenal crisis can follow infection, surgery or dental treatment under local anesthesia.

The 2024 ESE/Endocrine Society guideline treats a procedure under local anesthesia as minor stress for patients with or at risk of glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency. For a patient not taking a daily steroid, or taking less than hydrocortisone 40 mg/day, the described plan is oral hydrocortisone totaling 40 mg that day, with 20 mg one hour before and two later doses of 10 mg. Equivalent increases are prednisone to 10 mg or dexamethasone to 1 mg for patients taking lower daily doses. The guideline recognizes practice variation and limited evidence; coordinate the plan with the prescriber.

Cancer therapy, MRONJ and radiation

Before myelosuppressive chemotherapy, finish invasive dental procedures while the patient has time to heal. The NIDCR guide recommends invasive care 7–10 days before treatment and postponing elective oral surgery until cancer treatment is complete. AAPD's pediatric guidance gives a different range; when a number is used, state the source.

During chemotherapy, contact oncology before procedures and review blood work obtained within 24 hours before invasive care, following the archived NIDCR guide. That guide advises postponing below 75,000 platelets/mm³ or ANC 1,000/mm³. Pediatric AAPD guidance says to defer elective care below 60,000 platelets/mm³ and uses ANC bands: above 2,000/mm³, no antibiotic prophylaxis; 1,000–2,000, clinical judgment; below 1,000, defer elective care and discuss emergency treatment with oncology.

These are not interchangeable cutoffs. Many teaching sources use 50,000 platelets/mm³ before extraction, while the archived NIDCR guide uses 75,000; the AAPD gives 60,000 for deferring elective treatment. There is no single evidence-based threshold for every patient on cancer therapy, so discuss the current counts, regimen and procedure with the oncology team.

Oral care during cancer treatment remains important. Use a soft nylon toothbrush and fluoride toothpaste twice daily; foam swabs do not routinely replace brushing. Bland saline or sodium bicarbonate rinses can soothe the mouth, while alcohol rinses and daily hydrogen peroxide are discouraged. Chlorhexidine may be prescribed for neutropenia but is not recommended to prevent mucositis, so distinguish the indication.

Medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw requires all three parts of the definition: current or previous antiresorptive therapy, exposed bone or bone reached through a fistula for more than eight weeks, and no history of jaw radiation or jaw metastasis. It can occur with antiresorptives used alone or with immune modulators or antiangiogenic drugs. If the patient has jaw radiation, consider osteoradionecrosis rather than MRONJ.

MRONJ ranges from an at-risk state with no exposed bone to stage 0 radiographic or nonspecific findings, stage 1 exposed or probeable bone without infection, stage 2 with infection and symptoms, and stage 3 with extension beyond alveolar bone or complications such as fracture or extraoral fistula. Cancer-dose treatment carries a substantially higher risk than osteoporosis-dose treatment; learn the contrast (under 5% in cancer, under 0.05% in osteoporosis) rather than one per-drug percentage.

Drug holidays remain controversial; the working group could not reach consensus. Decisions depend on indication, dose schedule, duration, comorbidities and planned surgery. Denosumab interruption in osteoporosis can lead to rebound bone loss and multiple vertebral fractures; if held, surgery is done 3–4 months after the last dose and restart 6–8 weeks after surgery. CTX is not validated for deciding whether treatment is safe.

Before cancer-dose antiresorptives, examine the mouth, obtain indicated radiographs, and complete treatment of poor-prognosis teeth when systemic therapy can safely wait. During cancer-dose therapy, avoid dentoalveolar surgery when possible; consider root retention, and do not place implants.

Osteoradionecrosis is bone death caused by radiation injury and reduced blood supply; it may be diagnosed from clinical or radiographic findings, even when the mucosa is intact. The classic three-month exposed-bone definition is older teaching; the newer consensus does not require a minimum exposure period.

Before head and neck radiation, arrange a dental assessment about four weeks ahead when possible, and finish invasive procedures at least 14 days before radiation. After radiation, avoid elective surgery in the irradiated field; refer when an extraction or bone surgery is necessary so the dose at the specific site can be reviewed. Evidence does not support routine hyperbaric oxygen before extraction. The 2024 ISOO-MASCC-ASCO guideline gives only a weak recommendation for pentoxifylline with tocopherol in selected high-risk patients.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Pregnancy does not require a patient to defer routine preventive, diagnostic or restorative care. ADA and ACOG agree that radiographs and local anesthesia are safe at any point and that emergency care, including extraction, root canal treatment or restoration, can be completed. Delaying a painful or infected problem can make it worse.

Do not turn the familiar second-trimester preference into a requirement. That preference is a teaching convention; the ADA and ACOG point is that needed care may be given at any stage of pregnancy.

The ADA list names local anesthetics with or without epinephrine, penicillin, amoxicillin, cephalosporins, clindamycin and metronidazole as options considered safe in pregnancy. For pain medicines, the ADA advises consulting the obstetrician because the FDA found the evidence too limited for a general recommendation.

For NSAIDs, fetal risk changes with gestational age. At about 20 weeks or later, NSAIDs can cause fetal renal dysfunction and oligohydramnios; if use is necessary in the 20–30 week period, use the lowest dose for the shortest time and consider ultrasound monitoring if treatment exceeds 48 hours. At about 30 weeks and later, avoid NSAIDs because of the risk of premature ductus arteriosus closure.

Patients with infectious disease

Standard precautions apply to every patient, regardless of known or suspected infection. Hepatitis B can be prevented by vaccination. There is no hepatitis C vaccine, though current treatment cures more than 90% of cases. Hepatitis B and C infection alone does not call for withholding dental care.

Hepatic dysfunction may show in the mouth as mucosal jaundice, petechiae, easy bruising, gingival bleeding, fetor hepaticus, cheilitis or a smooth atrophic tongue. Those findings suggest medical context; they do not by themselves identify the cause.

AIDS is HIV infection with CD4 count below 200 cells/mm³ or one or more opportunistic infections. The ADA lists a platelet count below 60,000 and a neutrophil count below 500 (per µL; the ADA page prints the unit as cells/mL) as values that may change dental care. Otherwise, antibiotics are used judiciously rather than routinely.

After occupational exposure, antiretroviral post-exposure prophylaxis should begin within the first 72 hours, and sooner is better. Follow the exposure protocol and arrange urgent clinical evaluation.

Active tuberculosis can spread by airborne transmission, so standard precautions alone are insufficient. Defer nonurgent care. If treatment is urgent, refer promptly to a medical setting equipped to evaluate infectiousness and provide airborne isolation.

Sickle cell disease, transplant and immunosuppression

Stress, infection, dehydration and extreme temperature changes can trigger sickle cell complications. Before dental care, confirm stability with the physician, keep appointments short and low stress, and reduce risks of dehydration and acidosis. During an active crisis, treat only acute infection or trauma and defer elective care until the crisis resolves. These clinical recommendations are expert guidance rather than trial-based rules.

Dental infection deserves timely attention because it may trigger or worsen a crisis. Patients with sickle cell disease have higher infection risk because sickling damages the spleen early in life; a fever above 101.3°F (38.5°C) needs prompt medical attention. Chest pain, cough, fever and shortness of breath may indicate acute chest syndrome and require hospital treatment.

AAPD lists sickle cell anemia among conditions that increase methemoglobinemia risk; prilocaine is relatively contraindicated in that setting. The available evidence does not support treating epinephrine as universally contraindicated in sickle cell disease. Nitrous oxide may be considered after specialist consultation for a significant condition; it is not automatically prohibited.

Antibiotic prophylaxis in sickle cell disease is unsettled. AAPD lists SCD and asplenia among situations where prophylaxis may be considered for invasive dental procedures, with physician consultation; it does not support extensive use. The AHA endocarditis indications do not add sickle cell disease as a cardiac indication.

Before solid-organ transplantation or hematopoietic cell transplantation, complete needed dental care before immunosuppression whenever possible. AAPD pediatric guidance says a child receiving a solid-organ transplant tolerates dental care best from three months after the transplant. For hematopoietic cell transplant, elective care waits for immune recovery, at least 100 days, and longer if chronic graft-versus-host disease or other complications are present.

During immunosuppression, defer elective care and coordinate emergencies with the transplant physician. The conditioning and neutropenic phase after hematopoietic cell transplant is particularly restrictive. Transplant-related antibiotic prophylaxis is not universal: AAPD says to consider it for selected high-risk patients after consultation, while ADA gives no general prophylaxis recommendation for adult solid-organ transplant recipients.

Medication interactions can have serious consequences. Clarithromycin and azole antifungals can raise tacrolimus levels, increasing nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity and QT-prolongation risk; erythromycin and fluconazole also raise levels. Cyclosporine levels rise with several macrolides and antifungals. Coordinate an alternative and monitoring with the transplant prescriber rather than adjusting immunosuppression in dental care.

Cyclosporine can cause nephrotoxicity and hypertension, and NSAIDs can worsen renal function, particularly with dehydration. Check the transplant team's medication plan before recommending an analgesic.

Preventing and preparing for emergencies: the kit and BLS

Prevention starts before treatment: review the health history, identify conditions that can destabilize, keep prescribed rescue medicines accessible and know how to summon EMS. The ADA recommends that the team review emergency skills at least once a year, preferably more often.

Use P-C-A-B-D as a sequence for organizing the response: positioning, circulation, airway, breathing and definitive care. It is a memory structure for action, not a diagnosis. Altered consciousness in the dental office commonly follows syncope, hypoglycemia or seizure, so check responsiveness, pulse, breathing and glucose when indicated while arranging help.

Adult CPR uses compressions at 100–120 per minute and at least two inches deep, allowing full recoil. Use cycles of 30 compressions and two breaths, keep interruptions under 10 seconds, and attach an AED as soon as one is available. These numbers follow the American Red Cross adult CPR steps.

For suspected anaphylaxis, the auto-injector is dosed by weight: 0.15 mg at 15–30 kg and 0.3 mg at 30 kg or more, into the anterolateral thigh. Each device provides one dose, so EMS activation remains essential. For angina, a prescribed sublingual nitroglycerin tablet may be repeated every five minutes to a maximum of three in 15 minutes; persistent or unusual pain requires prompt medical attention.

For suspected opioid overdose, give 4 mg naloxone nasal spray and call 911 right away; repeat every two to three minutes if the person does not wake. For anaphylaxis, injectable diphenhydramine is only an adjunct after the acute symptoms are controlled, not a substitute for epinephrine.

Emergency by emergency

Syncope. Vasovagal syncope is a brief loss of consciousness from transient reduction of blood flow to the brain. Pain, fear and orthostatic stress can trigger it, often with sweating, pallor or nausea first. At the first sign, stop treatment and lay the patient supine with legs level with or slightly above the head; check pulse and breathing and open the airway. Full Trendelenburg positioning is controversial.

If the patient is unresponsive and not breathing normally or has no pulse, activate EMS, begin CPR and use the AED. Otherwise give supportive care and compare vital signs with baseline. Most faints resolve with positioning; routine drugs are not needed. Do not restart dentistry during the same visit because recurrence is more likely. A delayed return of consciousness or an event without a clear trigger calls for assessment of another cause.

Hypoglycemia. Glucose below 70 mg/dL with sweating, tremor, hunger, dizziness or confusion is treated as hypoglycemia. If the patient can swallow, give 15–20 g fast carbohydrate, wait 15 minutes and recheck. Repeat until above 70. If the patient cannot swallow or is unconscious, give nothing by mouth, call EMS and give glucagon if available; turn the patient on the side and give oral carbohydrate only after swallowing is safe.

Asthma. A patient with acute bronchospasm should sit upright as comfortable, receive supplemental oxygen and use an albuterol inhaler, preferably with a spacer if coordination is poor. Dental reviews give two to four inhalations, but the label says two; use the named source for exact dosing. If a severe attack prevents inhaler use, dental reviews describe 0.3 mg IM epinephrine for an adult. Escalate to EMS if the attack is severe, worsening or not responding promptly.

Anaphylaxis. Sudden allergic airway or circulatory symptoms need immediate IM epinephrine in the anterolateral thigh and EMS activation. Use a 0.3 mg auto-injector at 30 kg or more, or 0.15 mg at 15–30 kg. A patient with both laryngeal edema and bronchospasm is having anaphylaxis, not an isolated asthma attack. Diphenhydramine is an adjunct after acute symptoms are controlled.

Chest pain. Stop treatment and assess whether the pain matches the patient's usual angina. If prescribed nitroglycerin is available, let one tablet dissolve under the tongue and repeat every five minutes as needed, up to three tablets in 15 minutes. Persistent pain after three tablets, or pain different from usual, needs prompt medical attention. Suspected heart attack is an EMS emergency; aspirin is given to stop more clots from forming.

Seizure. Protect the patient from injury, keep the airway clear, and time the seizure. Call EMS when it lasts longer than five minutes or another seizure follows soon afterward.

Stroke. Sudden imbalance, vision change, facial or limb weakness, confusion or speech difficulty is a stroke warning. Use B.E. F.A.S.T. as a screen and call 911 right away; do not wait for symptoms to resolve.

Some emergencies imitate one another: a faint can include brief twitching, and delayed recovery should trigger a broader assessment; bronchospasm with laryngeal swelling changes the working emergency from asthma to anaphylaxis. Use the patient history, timing, airway and vital signs to guide immediate response while arranging definitive care.
