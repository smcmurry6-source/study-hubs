High-yield

  1. ASA class describes systemic disease and urgency; it does not calculate one patient's operative risk.
  2. For adult dental care, use the ADA guide's repeat-and-refer steps when a reading is above 160/100 mm Hg.
  3. IE prophylaxis is reserved for four cardiac risk groups and procedures that manipulate gingiva, periapical tissue, or perforate oral mucosa.
  4. Prosthetic joints alone do not call for routine dental antibiotic prophylaxis.
  5. Do not stop warfarin, a DOAC, or antiplatelet therapy for routine dental care without coordinating with the prescriber.
  6. Glucose below 70 mg/dL is hypoglycemia; use fast carbohydrate when the patient can swallow.
  7. A wheezing patient needs the rescue inhaler available; postpone treatment when asthma is poorly controlled.
  8. For cancer-treatment count thresholds, identify the source: NIDCR and AAPD recommendations differ.
  9. Pregnancy alone is not a reason to delay needed dental care, local anesthesia, or radiographs.
  10. Start an office emergency with P-C-A-B-D, activate EMS when indicated, and use an AED as soon as available if arrest is suspected.

The medical history and ASA status

Ask about diagnoses, current medicines and doses, recent hospital care, allergies and the patient's usual ability to function. Ask what changes symptoms and what treatment the medical team has advised; a medicine list can reveal conditions the patient did not name.

The ASA Physical Status classes describe the patient's systemic disease before anesthesia. ASA I is a healthy patient; ASA II is mild systemic disease without major functional limitation, such as controlled diabetes or hypertension; ASA III is severe disease with substantive functional limitation, such as poorly controlled diabetes, COPD or dialysis-dependent kidney failure.

ASA IV means severe disease that is a constant threat to life. ASA V describes a moribund patient not expected to survive without the operation; ASA VI is a declared brain-dead organ donor. Add E to classes I through V when delay would significantly increase the threat to life or a body part.

Use the class to describe health status, then make the dental plan from the current condition, procedure, medicines and medical advice. The class by itself is a poor predictor of an individual's operative risk.

Vital signs and blood pressure

Blood pressure is a measurement, not a diagnosis by itself. The 2017 ACC/AHA categories use whichever component is more severe; diagnosis requires at least two elevated readings on two occasions.

For adult dentistry, the ADA Practical Guide says below 160/100 mm Hg needs no modification. Above that, repeat; if confirmed, defer elective care and refer. A pain-driven emergency at 160–180 systolic and/or 100–109 diastolic may be treated with anxiety reduction and checks every 10–15 minutes. Its table conflicts on diastolic referral wording, so avoid 101–109 alone as the deciding value.

The 2017 ACC/AHA threshold of 180 systolic or 110 diastolic applies to possible deferral of elective major surgery, not the ADA dental pathway. No single evidence-based dental cutoff is established.

Cardiovascular disease and endocarditis prophylaxis

Infective endocarditis prophylaxis is limited to patients at highest risk of a poor outcome: those with a prosthetic cardiac valve or listed prosthetic valve material, prior endocarditis, specified high-risk congenital heart disease, or a cardiac transplant with valvulopathy. A pacemaker, coronary stent, most native valve disease or a completed septal closure device is not by itself an indication.

For an eligible patient, prophylaxis applies to dental procedures that manipulate gingiva, work in the periapical region, or perforate oral mucosa. It is not recommended for radiographs, injections through noninfected tissue, routine appliance placement or adjustment, or natural shedding of primary teeth.

The standard adult oral regimen is amoxicillin 2 g once, 30–60 minutes before treatment. Choose alternatives by allergy and oral access; clindamycin is no longer recommended. Avoid cephalosporins after anaphylaxis, angioedema or urticaria to penicillin.

A pacemaker or defibrillator calls for attention to device interference: electrosurgery has the highest reported risk, and piezoelectric scaling may be safer than magnetostrictive scaling.

Prosthetic joints

A prosthetic hip or knee does not routinely require antibiotics before dental treatment. The ADA guideline found no link between dental procedures and prosthetic joint infection and recommends against routine prophylaxis.

For a patient with prior joint-surgery complications who needs gingival manipulation or mucosal incision, discuss care with the patient and orthopedic surgeon. The ADA's 2026 position leaves the timing of care, and the rare decision to premedicate, to the dentist, the orthopedic team and the patient together; when prophylaxis is used, the surgeon recommends the regimen.

Bleeding risk and anticoagulation

For a simple extraction, continue warfarin when INR is 3.5 or less; consult the prescriber above that level. The typical therapeutic range is 2–3. Ask the patient's physician how recently the INR should be checked; no fixed time window is given here.

Most dental procedures do not require changing a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC). If bleeding risk is higher, options such as scheduling late after the last dose or briefly delaying or interrupting a dose require coordination with the physician. Antiplatelet drugs, alone or in combination, are usually continued; never stop dual therapy after a coronary stent without the cardiologist because premature interruption can cause stent thrombosis, myocardial infarction or death.

Control local bleeding with pressure, sutures, absorbable hemostatic material or tranexamic acid. The thromboembolic risk of stopping antithrombotic medicine often outweighs prolonged local bleeding.

Hemophilia A is factor VIII deficiency; hemophilia B is factor IX deficiency. Plan invasive care with the hematologist, especially before an inferior alveolar block or injection into vascular tissue. Slow, fine-gauge infiltration is lower risk.

Diabetes

Diabetes diagnosis uses laboratory criteria. A1C reflects roughly three months of glucose exposure, and the patient's target is individualized.

Hypoglycemia is glucose below 70 mg/dL. If the patient is awake and can swallow, give 15–20 g fast carbohydrate, wait 15 minutes, recheck, and repeat until glucose is above 70. A patient who cannot swallow gets nothing by mouth: call EMS and give glucagon if available.

Periodontitis and type 2 diabetes are linked in both directions: periodontal disease is associated with higher A1C, and periodontal treatment can lower it. Ask about the most recent A1C as part of the history.

Asthma and COPD

Ask about triggers, controller medicines and how often the patient needs a rescue inhaler. Rescue use for symptoms more than two days per week generally signals asthma that is not well controlled. Ask specifically about reactions to aspirin or other NSAIDs; a patient with a prior reaction can have severe cross-reactions.

Have every patient with asthma bring the rescue inhaler and keep it accessible. Postpone treatment when the patient is wheezing or asthma is poorly controlled. In an attack, sit the patient upright as comfortable, provide oxygen, and use inhaled albuterol; dental reviews give 2–4 inhalations, while the product label says 2, so an exact puff count depends on which source you follow. A steroid-only controller does not relieve acute bronchospasm.

In COPD, position the patient upright and titrate oxygen to the patient's target rather than withholding it or giving uncontrolled high flow. Targets differ: the British Thoracic Society recommends 88–92% for people at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure, while a US dental CE review uses above 90%. Know which number belongs to which source.

Kidney, liver, thyroid and adrenal disease

In chronic kidney disease, review the glomerular filtration rate when choosing or dosing medicines cleared by the kidneys. Avoid routine NSAID use because it can worsen kidney function; acetaminophen is a potential alternative, although a nephrologist may sometimes prefer carefully supervised NSAID use to opioids.

For a patient on hemodialysis, coordinate invasive care with the nephrologist; dental reviews commonly schedule it on a non-dialysis day, preferably the day after dialysis, but high-quality evidence for the timing is limited. Dialysis and a dialysis access graft are not, by themselves, a reason for antibiotic prophylaxis.

Liver disease can alter drug metabolism and lower clotting factor levels, so consult the physician before invasive care. In cirrhosis, INR and aPTT do not reliably predict procedural bleeding, and there is no validated dental extraction INR cutoff. Use case-by-case planning and local hemostasis.

In cirrhosis, acetaminophen is usually preferred over NSAIDs but at a reduced dose; reviews support about 2 g per day or less, and the patient should ask the treating clinician. Severe liver disease can also slow local-anesthetic metabolism, but the labels give no reduced cartridge maximum.

Use vasoconstrictors with extreme caution in thyrotoxicosis; elective surgery is postponed until uncontrolled hyperthyroidism is treated. No numeric epinephrine limit is set for thyroid disease. For a patient with current or recent glucocorticoid use or adrenal insufficiency, the Endocrine Society treats local-anesthetic procedures as minor stress and describes an oral stress-dose plan; coordinate it with the prescriber.

Cancer therapy, MRONJ and radiation

Before myelosuppressive cancer therapy, complete needed invasive dental care when time allows. The NIDCR guide recommends procedures 7–10 days before chemotherapy; AAPD gives different pediatric timing, so name the source. During therapy, contact the oncologist and review recent blood counts before invasive treatment.

There is no single evidence-based blood-count cutoff for all patients receiving cancer therapy. The archived NIDCR guide advises postponing invasive care below 75,000 platelets/mm³ or ANC 1,000/mm³; pediatric AAPD uses a 60,000 platelet threshold and different ANC bands. Confirm the source and coordinate with oncology rather than blending the numbers.

Medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) requires current or past antiresorptive therapy, exposed or probeable maxillofacial bone persisting more than eight weeks, and no jaw radiation or jaw metastasis. Cancer-dose antiresorptives carry much greater risk than osteoporosis-dose therapy. Drug holidays remain controversial; stopping denosumab can cause rebound bone loss and vertebral fractures, and CTX is not a validated decision test.

For head and neck radiation, plan dental assessment before therapy and avoid elective bone surgery in irradiated sites afterward. Newer evidence does not support routine hyperbaric oxygen before extraction; refer complex surgery or suspected osteoradionecrosis to a hospital team that can review the radiation dose to the site.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Preventive, diagnostic and restorative dentistry can be provided throughout pregnancy. Radiographs and local anesthesia are considered safe at any point, and needed emergency care should not be delayed solely because the patient is pregnant.

For medication decisions, check the current label and coordinate with the obstetric clinician when indicated. The FDA no longer uses pregnancy letter categories A, B, C, D and X for prescription labels; consult the narrative Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule sections instead.

Avoid NSAIDs at about 30 weeks and later; at about 20 weeks or later, use only when needed at the lowest dose for the shortest time. The ADA advises pregnant patients and staff to avoid nitrous oxide exposure.

Patients with infectious disease

Use standard precautions with every patient, including patients with hepatitis B, hepatitis C or HIV. Hepatitis B can be prevented by vaccination; there is no hepatitis C vaccine, and current treatment cures more than 90% of cases.

HIV infection alone does not call for routine antibiotics. AIDS means a CD4 count below 200 cells/mm³ or an opportunistic infection. The ADA lists two lab values that may change dental care: platelets below 60,000 (bleeding) and neutrophils below 500 (possible antibiotic prophylaxis), per µL; the ADA page itself prints the unit as cells/mL.

For an occupational exposure, start antiretroviral post-exposure prophylaxis within 72 hours, as soon as possible. Active tuberculosis needs airborne precautions: defer nonurgent care, and refer urgent care to a medical setting able to evaluate infectiousness.

Sources: HEP-1, HIV-1, HIV-2, HIV-3, TB-1

Sickle cell disease, transplant and immunosuppression

For sickle cell disease, confirm stability with the physician, keep visits short and low stress, prevent dehydration, and treat oral infections promptly. During a crisis, limit care to acute infection or trauma and defer elective procedures until it resolves.

The AAPD lists sickle cell anemia among the conditions that raise methemoglobinemia risk, in which prilocaine is relatively contraindicated. Nitrous oxide is not automatically contraindicated in sickle cell disease, but AAPD advises consultation with the medical specialist for significant conditions. Prophylactic antibiotics are not settled: AAPD says to consider them with physician input, not to prescribe them automatically.

Before solid-organ or hematopoietic transplant, complete needed dental treatment before immunosuppression when possible. AAPD says elective care after solid-organ transplant is best tolerated at least three months later; after hematopoietic cell transplant, wait at least 100 days and longer for complications. Antibiotic prophylaxis is a physician-coordinated consideration for selected immunocompromised patients, not a universal transplant rule.

Check interactions before prescribing: clarithromycin and azole antifungals can raise tacrolimus concentrations, increasing toxicity risk. Cyclosporine can worsen kidney function, and NSAIDs may add to that risk, especially with dehydration.

Sources: SCD-1, SCD-3, SCD-4, SCD-5, SCD-6, TX-1, TX-2, TX-4, TX-5, TX-6, PJ-4

Preventing and preparing for emergencies: the kit and BLS

Review emergency skills at least annually, preferably more often. Use P-C-A-B-D: positioning, circulation, airway, breathing, then definitive care. Syncope, hypoglycemia and seizure are common causes of altered consciousness in a dental setting.

Keep emergency drugs and equipment ready for the conditions the office treats, and make sure the team can call EMS, provide oxygen and use the AED. In adult CPR, compress at 100–120 per minute, at least 2 inches deep, with full recoil; use 30 compressions to 2 breaths and keep pauses under 10 seconds. Use the AED as soon as it is available.

Emergency by emergency

Syncope. Pain, fear or standing can trigger vasovagal syncope. At the first sign, place the patient supine with legs level with or slightly above the head, check pulse and breathing, and open the airway. If the patient is not breathing normally or has no pulse, begin CPR, use the AED and activate EMS. After a faint, do not resume dental treatment during that visit; delayed recovery needs evaluation for another cause.

Hypoglycemia. Below 70 mg/dL, treat an alert patient who can swallow with 15–20 g of fast carbohydrate and recheck in 15 minutes. If the patient cannot swallow, give nothing by mouth, call EMS and give glucagon; turn the patient on the side and offer carbohydrate once swallowing is safe.

Asthma. Sit the patient upright, give oxygen, and use inhaled albuterol. If the patient cannot use the inhaler during a severe attack, dental emergency reviews support adult epinephrine 0.3 mg IM; if symptoms do not improve quickly and lastingly or worsen, activate EMS. Laryngeal edema with bronchospasm suggests anaphylaxis, for which epinephrine is first-line.

Anaphylaxis. Give intramuscular epinephrine in the anterolateral thigh. The auto-injector dose is 0.3 mg at 30 kg or more and 0.15 mg at 15–30 kg; activate EMS. Antihistamines are adjuncts after acute symptoms are controlled and do not replace epinephrine.

Chest pain. Stop treatment and assess the patient. If the pain is consistent with angina and nitroglycerin is prescribed, allow one sublingual tablet to dissolve and repeat every five minutes to a maximum of three in 15 minutes. Persistent or unusual pain needs prompt medical attention; call EMS for suspected heart attack.

Seizure. Protect the patient from injury, keep the airway clear, and time the event. Call EMS if a seizure lasts longer than five minutes or another seizure follows soon after.

Stroke. Use B.E. F.A.S.T. for sudden balance, eye, face, arm or speech changes, and call 911 right away.
