Drug interactions and CYP3A

A drug's half-life is the time its serum concentration takes to fall to half its previous level and helps explain dosing intervals. For medicines following first-order elimination, after roughly five half-life periods around 97% has been eliminated, so the drug is generally treated as cleared.

Bioavailability is the portion of an active dose that reaches systemic circulation unchanged. Intravenous doses are fully bioavailable and enter circulation directly; an oral dose absorbed from the small intestine travels through the portal system to the liver, where first-pass metabolism can reduce how much reaches the circulation. How much reaches the blood depends partly on the drug and its first-pass metabolism.

Only unbound drug can act at its target or move into other compartments. Reduced protein binding can increase the active fraction, while lipid solubility, rather than low protein binding alone, helps a drug cross the blood-brain barrier. The liver handles most drug metabolism, and the kidneys remove many drugs; dysfunction in either organ may allow some medicines to accumulate. After an IV bolus, lidocaine's elimination half-life is about 1.5 to 2 hours; liver dysfunction can make it at least twice as long. Renal dysfunction does not change lidocaine kinetics, though its metabolites may accumulate; these facts do not call for a dental lidocaine dose reduction in liver disease.

The FDA table distinguishes strong CYP3A inhibitors, including clarithromycin and itraconazole, from moderate inhibitors, including erythromycin, fluconazole and grapefruit juice. Simvastatin and midazolam are sensitive CYP3A substrates; when one of the inhibitors is added, their levels can rise. The strength category matters because named inhibitors can produce different degrees of interaction.

The named CYP3A inducers have an opposite effect: rifampin and carbamazepine lower substrate levels. Starting or stopping an interacting medicine can change the effect; codeine and tramadol labels describe risks when CYP3A inhibitors or inducers are added or withdrawn. Grapefruit juice is also listed as a moderate CYP3A inhibitor, so include it in a focused medication review.

The named drug pairs are more useful than memorizing a generic warning. Metronidazole potentiates warfarin, so prothrombin time and INR monitoring matter; with alcohol or propylene glycol, it can produce a disulfiram-like reaction, and it can raise lithium levels. The alcohol restriction lasts during treatment and at least 3 days after the final dose, and disulfiram is avoided within 2 weeks.

Fluconazole has several distinct interaction targets. It can raise warfarin effect and bleeding events, cause clinically significant hypoglycemia with glipizide or glyburide, and increase myopathy or rhabdomyolysis risk with susceptible statins. It can also increase QT-prolongation risk; avoid combining it with erythromycin. Clarithromycin is contraindicated with lovastatin and simvastatin because of rhabdomyolysis, and with several medicines that can cause QT-related arrhythmias.

For codeine, CYP2D6 converts the parent drug to morphine. An ultra-rapid metabolizer can produce unexpectedly high morphine levels and develop dangerous respiratory depression even while following a labeled dose; codeine should not be used by known ultra-rapid metabolizers. Tramadol also forms a more potent active metabolite through CYP2D6, with the same concern for respiratory depression in ultra-rapid metabolizers. Both medicines are contraindicated in children younger than 12 and for postoperative pain in patients younger than 18 after tonsillectomy or adenoidectomy. They are not recommended for adolescents ages 12-18 with obesity, obstructive sleep apnea or severe lung disease, and neither is recommended while breastfeeding.

Codeine's CYP3A interactions can change the amount of morphine made from it. A CYP3A inhibitor, or stopping a CYP3A inducer such as rifampin or carbamazepine, can raise codeine and morphine exposure; CYP2D6 inhibitors can instead reduce morphine formation and pain relief. Tramadol has similarly complex warnings when a CYP3A inducer or inhibitor or a CYP2D6 inhibitor is added or stopped.

Methadone labels add a separate interaction concern: medicines that inhibit CYP3A4, CYP2B6, CYP2C19, CYP2C9 or CYP2D6 can raise methadone and increase the risk of fatal respiratory depression. The label examples include macrolide antibiotics and azole antifungals; QT prolongation and torsades de pointes are also warning concerns. Dose changes belong with the methadone prescriber or opioid treatment program.

Analgesics and the ADA approach to acute dental pain

The ADA guideline covers adolescents 12 and older, adults and older adults after extractions and for temporary toothache relief before definitive treatment. It starts with nonopioid care because an NSAID alone or paired with acetaminophen is the preferred initial approach in the guideline. When an NSAID is contraindicated, acetaminophen alone is an option; it is not necessary to add an opioid simply because pain is dental.

The suggested starting amounts are 400 mg ibuprofen or 440 mg naproxen sodium, alone or together with 500 mg acetaminophen. If an NSAID cannot be used, the ADA example is 1,000 mg acetaminophen. The guideline's daily ceilings are 2,400 mg for ibuprofen, 1,100 mg for naproxen sodium and 4,000 mg for acetaminophen. Those are guideline values for dental pain; the ibuprofen label's 3,200 mg arthritis ceiling should not replace the ADA value in a dental-pain plan.

This is a routine-choice warning rather than an absolute ban: supervised NSAID treatment may sometimes be preferred to an opioid, and advanced renal disease requires close attention to the ibuprofen label.

Asthma history needs a more specific question than simply asking whether the patient has asthma. Avoid aspirin and other NSAIDs when the patient has had asthma, hives or another allergic-type reaction after aspirin or an NSAID; the label describes cross-reactivity. In other patients with preexisting asthma, ibuprofen is used cautiously. Acetaminophen is an alternative for aspirin-sensitive patients, although a small cross-sensitivity risk is reported.

NSAIDs also carry warnings for gastrointestinal bleeding and cardiovascular thrombotic events, and are contraindicated around coronary artery bypass surgery. Ibuprofen can reduce low-dose aspirin's antiplatelet effect. Together, kidney disease, cirrhosis, aspirin sensitivity, cardiovascular history, pregnancy timing and concurrent medicines can change the first choice, even when a standard dose table is familiar.

For simple extractions, the ADA panel recommends against opioids. For surgical extractions or toothache, the guideline reserves an acetaminophen-plus-opioid combination for rare cases where NSAID-based therapy has failed or cannot be used; use the lowest opioid amount, the fewest tablets and a brief course. Acetaminophen already inside the combination still counts toward the daily total.

Opioids: prescribing, safety and opioid use disorder

The ADA acute dental-pain guideline and the CDC opioid guideline point in the same direction for routine dental pain: begin with nonopioid care when it can reasonably work. The CDC recommendation applies to outpatients at least 18 years old and names dental pain and pain after a simple extraction among its examples; for surgical dental pain, opioid relief probably falls short of NSAID relief, according to the CDC evidence review. The CDC does not cover cancer pain, sickle cell pain, palliative care or end-of-life care, so do not extend its scope to those settings.

When an opioid is needed, the CDC recommends immediate-release medicine rather than extended-release or long-acting products for acute pain. Start from product labeling and use the lowest effective dose; the CDC's often-equivalent figures of about 5-10 MME per dose or 20-30 MME per day are guides, not targets. For many opioid-naive outpatients, dosage increases are not needed for a course of a few days or less.

The CDC says to prescribe no greater quantity than the expected period of opioid-level pain and to avoid extra tablets for a possible future flare. Its common acute-pain examples often need a few days or less. The ADA 2024 dental-pain guideline says opioid duration rarely exceeds 3 days. The ADA's 7-day figure is a policy position supporting legal limits, not a desired clinical duration. Keep those statements attached to their sources because they answer different questions.

Before the first opioid prescription, ask about alcohol and other drug use, past overdose, current medicines and respiratory risks. The CDC recommends reviewing the state prescription drug monitoring program when practicable, ideally before each prescription. It also recommends offering naloxone when prescribing to higher-risk patients, including people with a prior overdose or substance use disorder, sleep-disordered breathing, benzodiazepine use or a high opioid dosage; FDA advice cited in the ADA guideline says to discuss naloxone with everyone prescribed an opioid.

The ADA's opioid statement calls for a medical and dental history that includes current medicines, interaction risk and substance-use history. Patients should hear about dependence, misuse, respiratory depression and driving, and the conversation should cover safe storage and disposal. When treating chronic orofacial pain, coordinate with the patient's other clinicians.

Opioid and benzodiazepine combinations need particular caution because opioid labels warn of profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma and death when they are combined with benzodiazepines, alcohol or other central nervous system depressants. For patients who take benzodiazepines, the CDC advises continuing medication treatment for opioid use disorder, including buprenorphine or methadone. For a patient with acute or severe asthma outside a monitored setting, opioid labels list a contraindication; significant COPD, cor pulmonale, reduced respiratory reserve, hypoxia or hypercapnia also raises concern for a dangerous reduction in breathing.

Dental pain does not justify stopping a patient's buprenorphine or methadone maintenance. The ADA advises continuing medication-assisted treatment during dental pain, while the CDC recommends nonopioid treatment as appropriate. The buprenorphine prescriber can consider more frequent dosing for acute pain; adding a short-acting full agonist is described only for severe acute pain in a supervised setting. Methadone maintenance does not reliably treat new acute pain. If an opioid is needed, somewhat higher or more frequent doses are often needed because methadone creates tolerance; coordinate additional treatment with the opioid treatment program.

Naltrexone blocks opioid medicines and can blunt opioid analgesia; for dental pain, use nonopioid approaches or regional care when appropriate. Do not try to overcome the blockade with a large opioid dose in a dental office. The blockade can be overcome with enough opioid, and tolerance is lower after naltrexone stops or an injection wears off, raising overdose risk. Naltrexone can precipitate withdrawal in someone physically dependent on opioids, including a patient taking buprenorphine or methadone.

Suspect overdose when a person cannot be awakened, has slow or shallow breathing, gurgling or snoring sounds, discolored lips or nails, or pinpoint pupils; hypoxia can instead cause dilated pupils. If the situation is unclear, the CDC advises treating it as an overdose. Protect the airway, support ventilation as needed, administer naloxone and call 911. After the first nasal dose, provide another at 2-3 minute intervals if there is no response; continue while awaiting recovery or emergency responders. Reversal may wear off before the opioid, so observe the patient and repeat as needed. See Medically complex patients for the emergency response framework.

Antibiotics for dental infections and stewardship

The ADA guideline separates pulpal diagnoses from spreading infection. It recommends against antibiotics for symptomatic irreversible pulpitis, whether or not definitive care is immediately available. For pulp necrosis with symptomatic apical periodontitis, it advises against antibiotics when definitive conservative treatment is not immediately available and recommends referral for that treatment. Delaying the visit alone does not convert these presentations into antibiotic indications.

The ADA guideline uses definitive conservative dental treatment to mean pulpotomy, pulpectomy, root-canal debridement, nonsurgical root-canal treatment or incision and drainage. It does not include extraction in the scope of that guideline. These procedures address the source; antibiotics are not a substitute for treatment when treatment is indicated.

For a localized acute apical abscess without fever, malaise or spread into a fascial space, the ADA guideline conditionally allows a delayed or current prescription when definitive treatment is not immediately available. Listed regimens are amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily or penicillin VK 500 mg four times daily, each for 3-7 days. When definitive treatment is performed, the guideline recommends against adding an antibiotic routinely.

Systemic involvement changes the plan. The ADA guideline pairs urgent definitive conservative treatment with amoxicillin or penicillin VK when fever, malaise, lymph-node involvement or fascial-space involvement is present. Rapidly spreading swelling or airway risk calls for urgent medical evaluation rather than routine office follow-up.

Antibiotic stewardship applies outside endodontic emergencies as well. The periodontal guidance says not to add systemic antibiotics routinely to subgingival instrumentation, though selected patients such as young adults with generalized stage III periodontitis may be considered. Unneeded antibiotic exposure raises adverse-event and interaction risk and can postpone definitive dental treatment.

Antifungals and antivirals

Inhaled corticosteroids are a common cause of oral candidiasis. Rinsing the mouth with water and spitting after each dose reduces risk; a spacer or valved holding chamber can help with a metered-dose inhaler. If thrush occurs, treat it with a local or systemic antifungal while usually continuing the inhaled corticosteroid, although the label allows interruption in some circumstances.

Nystatin suspension is one oral option. The adult and child dose is 4-6 mL four times daily, split between the two sides of the mouth and held in place as long as possible before swallowing; the infant amount is 2 mL four times daily. The label expresses the adult and child volume as 400,000-600,000 units per dose and the infant dose as 200,000 units.

Fluconazole is another regimen for oropharyngeal candidiasis: give 200 mg on day one, then use a daily 100-mg dose for a treatment period of two weeks or longer. The extended course is intended to reduce relapse. Before choosing it, check for interactions because fluconazole can raise warfarin effect, increase hypoglycemia risk with certain sulfonylureas and interact with susceptible statins; it can also raise calcineurin-inhibitor levels.

Drugs that change dental care

A medication list is a clinical tool, not a formality. Record current medicines, indication, timing and recent changes; pair each proposed dental prescription with the patient's other drugs and conditions. If a bleeding-risk decision or a medicine interruption is being considered, use the medically complex patients section for the procedure framework and coordinate changes with the relevant prescriber.

Warfarin is monitored with INR, while direct oral anticoagulants use fixed doses, shorter half-lives and little or no monitoring; INR does not track their effect. Dabigatran inhibits thrombin, while apixaban, rivaroxaban and edoxaban inhibit factor Xa. Use the Medically complex patients bleeding-risk section for procedure decisions and coordination guidance.

Antiplatelet medicines are not stopped for dental procedures as a routine step. This includes single or dual therapy; for a patient with a coronary stent, involve the cardiologist before any change because premature interruption risks stent thrombosis, myocardial infarction and death.

Cyclosporine and tacrolimus are calcineurin inhibitors with important dental antimicrobial interactions. Fluconazole, itraconazole, ketoconazole, voriconazole and several macrolides can raise calcineurin-inhibitor levels; tacrolimus can rise sharply with clarithromycin even when its dose has already been reduced. Higher levels increase nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity and QT-prolongation risk, so the transplant team follows drug levels and adjusts the regimen.

Cyclosporine can also increase hypertension and renal toxicity. NSAIDs taken with cyclosporine, especially during dehydration, can further impair kidney function; naproxen and sulindac have additive effects, and cyclosporine roughly doubles diclofenac levels. Monitor renal function when a potentially nephrotoxic medicine is combined; the labels don't name a preferred pain reliever, so choose it with the transplant team.

Product labels warn that epinephrine with monoamine oxidase inhibitors, nonselective beta-blockers or tricyclic antidepressants can cause severe, prolonged hypertension; propranolol can also be associated with hypertension and bradycardia. Phenothiazines may reduce or reverse epinephrine's pressor effect. Tricyclic antidepressants and SNRIs can prolong the response, and amphetamines or atomoxetine share this concern. Levonordefrin acts mainly on alpha receptors, so its effect is mostly on blood pressure. SSRIs, trazodone, mirtazapine and bupropion do not have significant epinephrine interactions. Minimize the epinephrine amount and inject slowly.

Antiresorptive medicines change the timing and risk discussion around dentoalveolar surgery. The 2022 position paper reports medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw risk below 5% with cancer-dose treatment and below 0.05% with osteoporosis treatment; before cancer-dose treatment, dental evaluation and needed surgery can be completed first when the medical condition allows. During cancer-dose treatment, avoid dentoalveolar surgery when possible, consider root retention for poor-prognosis teeth and do not place implants.

Drug holidays are controversial. The 2022 working group did not reach agreement on a single approach, and bone turnover marker CTX is not validated to decide whether treatment can be paused. For osteoporosis denosumab, stopping can trigger rebound bone loss and multiple vertebral fractures; if the prescriber chooses a hold, the 2022 position paper places surgery 3-4 months after the last dose and restarting 6-8 weeks after surgery. The older four-year oral-bisphosphonate rule is not a current recommendation.

The 2024 European Society of Endocrinology and Endocrine Society guideline says glucocorticoid-induced adrenal risk is expected when oral treatment lasts at least 3-4 weeks and exceeds a physiologic daily amount: hydrocortisone 15-25 mg, prednisone or prednisolone 4-6 mg, methylprednisolone 3-5 mg, or dexamethasone 0.25-0.5 mg. For oral courses shorter than 3-4 weeks, the guideline weakly suggests stopping without a taper; that recommendation is based on very low-quality evidence. Individual risk varies; adrenal crisis can also occur after inhaled, topical or intra-articular glucocorticoids.

The same 2024 European Society of Endocrinology and Endocrine Society guidance treats any procedure requiring local anesthesia as minor stress for a patient with or at risk of glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency. For a patient not on daily glucocorticoids or taking less than hydrocortisone 40 mg per day, the suggested oral plan is hydrocortisone 40 mg over the day, such as 20 mg one hour before and 10 mg at each of two later intervals. For someone taking less than prednisone 10 mg daily, the suggested amount is 10 mg one hour before; those already taking at least hydrocortisone 40 mg, prednisone 10 mg or dexamethasone 1 mg daily usually need no increase. For primary adrenal insufficiency, the 2016 Endocrine Society sick-day plan doubles the routine oral dose before a small outpatient procedure. Practice varies and robust evidence is lacking, so coordinate the plan with the clinician managing the condition.

For severe hypoglycemia when the patient is unconscious or cannot swallow, do not give anything by mouth; call EMS and give glucagon. The injection label gives adults and children at least 20 kg a 1 mg dose by subcutaneous, intramuscular or intravenous route; smaller children receive 0.5 mg or 20-30 mcg/kg. A nasal device gives 3 mg in one nostril to adults and children at least 1 year old, with a second equal dose after 15 minutes if there is no response.

Tranexamic acid is a useful example of why a dose must stay attached to its source. The WFH regimen is usually oral 25 mg/kg per dose three to four times daily or IV 10 mg/kg per dose two to three times daily, infused slowly; an oral rinse is also available. It is cleared by the kidneys, so reduce it in renal impairment. Avoid it in hematuria and in hemophilia B patients receiving prothrombin-complex concentrates. The FDA IV label lists a different schedule, so name the source when communicating a dose.

Adverse effects with oral signs

A patient's dry-mouth complaint can be a medicine effect. In older adults, the listed drug groups include urologic medicines, antidepressants and psycholeptics; asking about the medication list can reveal a likely contributor. The FDA has approved two oral sialogogues for dry mouth: pilocarpine and cevimeline.

Drug-influenced gingival enlargement is larger than inflammation alone would be expected to produce. The listed associations include phenytoin and sodium valproate, several calcium-channel blockers, cyclosporine and high-dose oral contraceptives. Calcium-channel examples include nifedipine, verapamil, diltiazem, amlodipine and felodipine. This is an association to consider during medication review, not proof that a single medicine explains every enlarged gingiva.

Tooth discoloration is a developmental effect of tetracyclines. Exposure in the last half of pregnancy, infancy or childhood through age 8 can leave permanent yellow, gray or brown staining; avoid those drugs in pregnancy and in children 8 and under unless the expected benefit outweighs risk in a severe infection.

Buprenorphine taken sublingually or buccally has an FDA label warning about caries, some severe enough to cause tooth fracture or loss, along with abscesses, erosion and lost fillings. Cases have been reported even in patients without previous dental problems. The ADA notes that the benefits of buprenorphine outweigh its dental risks, and that preventive oral care can help; do not frame dental risk as a reason to stop treatment for opioid use disorder.

Substance use and tobacco cessation

Ask about substance use without turning the history into a judgment. When an opioid is being considered, the ADA statement calls for asking about substance-use history and other medicines, reviewing interactions, and discussing safe storage and disposal. Nonopioid and multimodal care can reduce the need for an opioid prescription.

Methamphetamine use is associated with rampant caries, often called meth mouth, bruxism and temporomandibular disorders. The described mechanism is blocked reuptake of dopamine, norepinephrine and serotonin. These findings can guide a respectful conversation about symptoms, prevention and needed care.

Pregnancy and lactation

Pregnancy alone is not a reason to defer preventive, diagnostic or restorative dental care. ADA and ACOG agree that urgent care such as an extraction, root canal or restoration can be done during pregnancy and that waiting can let a problem worsen. The old teaching that the second trimester is a required window is not a reason to delay care. Radiographs are considered safe throughout pregnancy.

The ADA names penicillin, amoxicillin, cephalosporins, clindamycin and metronidazole among antibiotic options in pregnancy. For pain medicines, consult the obstetric clinician because the FDA said the evidence available in 2015 was too limited for its page to make specific recommendations. Do not apply the former prescription-label letter categories as if they were current labels.

At about 20 weeks, NSAID use can affect fetal kidney function and lead to low amniotic fluid. From about 20 to 30 weeks, if an NSAID is needed, keep exposure as limited as possible; when use continues beyond 48 hours, consider ultrasound assessment of amniotic fluid. At about 30 weeks and later, avoid NSAIDs because fetal ductal closure can occur prematurely.

Tetracyclines can permanently discolor teeth while they are developing. The avoid window includes the last half of pregnancy and children through age 8, unless the benefit outweighs the risk for a severe infection.

The FDA Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule replaced prescription-label letter categories with narrative sections: 8.1 for pregnancy, 8.2 for lactation, and 8.3 for females and males of reproductive potential. Newer drugs used the rule immediately, while earlier prescription labels were phased in; over-the-counter labels were not part of the change. For lactation-specific details, review section 8.2 or NLM LactMed rather than assuming a medicine is safe because it is compatible with pregnancy.
