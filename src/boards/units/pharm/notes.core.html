High-yield

  1. For acute dental pain in patients 12 and older, the ADA guideline puts an NSAID alone or with acetaminophen first.
  2. After simple extractions, the ADA panel advises against opioids; for other acute dental pain, it reserves them for rare cases when first-line treatment is not enough.
  3. The ADA dental-pain guideline sets daily ceilings of 2,400 mg ibuprofen, 1,100 mg naproxen sodium and 4,000 mg acetaminophen.
  4. In chronic kidney disease, avoid routine NSAID use; acetaminophen is a potential non-nephrotoxic alternative.
  5. In cirrhosis, avoid NSAIDs and use a reduced acetaminophen amount, about 2 g a day or less.
  6. Clarithromycin and itraconazole are strong CYP3A inhibitors; sensitive substrates such as simvastatin and midazolam can rise.
  7. Rifampin and carbamazepine can lower the levels of CYP3A substrates.
  8. Antibiotics do not treat pulpitis; definitive dental treatment is the priority when indicated.
  9. The AHA adult infective-endocarditis prophylaxis regimen is amoxicillin 2 g by mouth 30-60 minutes before care; do not use a cephalosporin after penicillin anaphylaxis, angioedema or hives.
  10. Inhaled corticosteroids can cause oral candidiasis; rinsing and spitting after use lowers risk.
  11. From about 20 weeks of pregnancy, NSAIDs can affect fetal kidneys; from about 30 weeks, avoid them because of ductal closure risk.

Drug interactions and CYP3A

A drug's half-life describes how long its serum concentration takes to fall to half its previous level; after roughly five half-life periods, around 97% is gone and the medicine is generally treated as cleared. Bioavailability describes how much unchanged drug reaches the circulation: intravenous doses are fully bioavailable and enter circulation directly, while first-pass metabolism in the liver can reduce the amount that reaches circulation after an oral dose. Only unbound drug acts at its target or moves into other compartments; weaker protein binding can increase the active free fraction. Lipid solubility helps a drug cross the blood-brain barrier. The liver handles much drug metabolism, and the kidneys remove many drugs, so dysfunction can allow some medicines to accumulate.

CYP3A inhibitors can slow the breakdown of medicines that are CYP3A substrates, raising their levels; strong inhibitors in the FDA interaction table include clarithromycin and itraconazole, while erythromycin, fluconazole and grapefruit juice are listed as moderate inhibitors. Simvastatin and midazolam are sensitive substrates, so a new inhibitor can matter even when the patient has taken the substrate for a long time.

Inducers move the interaction in the other direction: rifampin and carbamazepine can lower the levels of CYP3A substrates. For an opioid that depends on CYP processing, adding or stopping another medicine can alter both analgesia and toxicity, so review the whole medication list rather than treating the new prescription in isolation.

Analgesics and the ADA approach to acute dental pain

The ADA guideline covers adolescents 12 and older, adults and older adults after tooth extraction and for temporary relief of toothache before definitive care. It recommends starting with an NSAID alone, such as ibuprofen or naproxen sodium, or combining one with acetaminophen; when an NSAID is contraindicated, acetaminophen alone is an option.

ADA acute dental-pain doses and ceilings
MedicineGuideline dose or daily ceiling
IbuprofenStarting dose 400 mg; daily ceiling 2,400 mg for dental pain.
Naproxen sodiumStarting dose 440 mg; daily ceiling 1,100 mg.
AcetaminophenPairing dose 500 mg with an NSAID, or 1,000 mg alone when NSAIDs are contraindicated; daily ADA ceiling 4,000 mg.

Keep a guideline ceiling separate from a product label. An over-the-counter acetaminophen label warns of severe liver injury above 4,000 mg in 24 hours, but one common OTC caplet label limits adults to 3,000 mg a day unless a doctor directs otherwise; combination products also contribute to the daily total. In cirrhosis, narrative reviews recommend a reduced amount, about 2 g per day or less, and NSAIDs are best avoided.

NSAIDs can worsen kidney function, especially in chronic kidney disease, so routine dental use should favor acetaminophen when appropriate; they are not absolutely forbidden in every patient with kidney disease, because supervised use may sometimes be preferred to opioids. With asthma, avoid NSAIDs after a prior aspirin or NSAID allergic-type reaction; use caution in other patients with preexisting asthma.

NSAID labels also warn about gastrointestinal bleeding and cardiovascular thrombotic events, and they contraindicate use around coronary artery bypass surgery; ibuprofen can interfere with low-dose aspirin's antiplatelet effect.

For simple extractions, the ADA panel recommends against opioids. For surgical extractions or toothache, it allows an opioid only in rare cases after an NSAID with or without acetaminophen has not worked or cannot be used; the opioid should be a low dose, few tablets and short course.

Opioids: prescribing, safety and opioid use disorder

For dental pain, keep the ADA approach in view: nonopioid treatment comes first, and an opioid is a limited fallback for select acute pain when first-line care is insufficient or unsuitable. The CDC guideline applies to outpatients 18 and older and likewise favors nonopioid approaches for many acute pain conditions, including dental pain.

If an opioid is needed, the CDC recommends immediate-release medicine at the lowest effective dose and only enough for the period of pain severe enough to need it. The ADA guideline says the duration rarely exceeds 3 days; the CDC says a few days or less is often enough for many common causes of acute pain. ADA policy supports a statutory limit of no more than 7 days, which is a policy position rather than a clinical target.

Before prescribing, review current medicines, interactions, substance-use history and the state prescription drug monitoring program where practicable. Discuss dependence, misuse, breathing suppression, driving, storage and disposal, and use shared decision making. The CDC recommends offering naloxone especially when overdose risk is higher, including past overdose or substance use disorder, sleep-disordered breathing, higher opioid doses, or concurrent benzodiazepines.

Opioids combined with benzodiazepines, alcohol or other central nervous system depressants can cause profound sedation and respiratory depression. Opioid labels contraindicate use in acute or severe asthma without monitoring and resuscitation equipment; people with significant COPD or reduced respiratory reserve are also at risk, even at labeled doses.

For opioid use disorder, do not stop buprenorphine or methadone to manage routine dental pain; use nonopioid pain treatment and coordinate with the treating prescriber. Buprenorphine can sometimes be dosed more often for acute pain by its prescriber, and methadone maintenance does not treat new postoperative pain by itself.

Naloxone is an emergency response to suspected overdose, not a substitute for emergency help. Give a 4 mg nasal dose, call 911 right away, and repeat every 2-3 minutes if the person does not wake up, until the person responds or help arrives. For the office emergency sequence, see Medically complex patients.

Antibiotics for dental infections and stewardship

Antibiotics do not remove the dental source. Symptomatic irreversible pulpitis does not call for antibiotics, whether or not definitive care is immediately available; pulp necrosis with symptomatic apical periodontitis also does not call for antibiotics when definitive conservative treatment is unavailable.

Definitive conservative dental treatment includes pulpotomy, pulpectomy, root-canal debridement, nonsurgical root-canal treatment, or incision and drainage. The ADA antibiotic guideline does not cover extraction as its definitive-treatment pathway.

Antibiotics are also not routine adjuncts to subgingival instrumentation; the periodontal guideline leaves room for selected groups such as young adults with generalized stage III periodontitis. Avoiding unnecessary exposure reduces drug harms and interactions and avoids delaying definitive dental care.

Antibiotic prophylaxis regimens and penicillin allergy

First decide whether the patient and procedure meet an indication. Who needs infective-endocarditis prophylaxis: see the Medically complex patients section. The regimen is one preprocedure dose, ordinarily taken 30-60 minutes before care.

When a patient is already taking a short antibiotic course, the AHA approach is to select an agent from a different class; if elective care can wait, it may be delayed until 10 days after the course ends. For serial visits, leave 10 days after the last dose when possible.

Prosthetic joints are a separate topic: routine prophylaxis is generally not recommended, while a history of joint-replacement complications calls for shared planning with the patient and orthopedic surgeon. See Medically complex patients for the dental decision context.

Antifungals and antivirals

Inhaled corticosteroids commonly cause oral candidiasis. The patient can lower this risk by rinsing with water and spitting after each inhalation; a spacer with a metered-dose inhaler also helps. If candidiasis develops, treat it locally or systemically while usually continuing the inhaled steroid.

Drugs that change dental care

Medication review is part of treatment planning. Warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs differ in monitoring and interaction profiles. Use the Medically complex patients bleeding-risk section for procedure decisions and coordination guidance.

Warfarin has important antimicrobial interactions: metronidazole and fluconazole can raise its effect, so the INR or other bleeding signs may need closer attention. Direct oral anticoagulants do not use the INR for routine monitoring. Do not stop aspirin or other antiplatelet medicines for dental procedures. If a patient with a coronary stent needs any change, involve the cardiologist.

Cyclosporine and tacrolimus interact with macrolides and azole antifungals. These medicines can raise calcineurin-inhibitor levels and the risk of kidney or neurologic toxicity, so the transplant team monitors levels and makes dose changes. Cyclosporine can also worsen kidney injury when paired with NSAIDs, particularly during dehydration; a dental analgesic choice should account for the full regimen.

For drug-related effects on dental epinephrine, nonselective beta-blockers, tricyclic antidepressants and monoamine oxidase inhibitors warrant caution because prolonged blood-pressure changes can occur. Tricyclics and SNRIs can exaggerate or prolong responses; inject slowly and minimize the epinephrine amount. Anesthetic doses and limits are in the Local anesthesia unit.

Adverse effects with oral signs

Medication-associated dry mouth is linked with urologic drugs, antidepressants and psycholeptics. Pilocarpine and cevimeline are the two oral sialogogues approved by the FDA for dry mouth.

Drug-influenced gingival enlargement can be greater than inflammation alone would explain. Linked medicines include phenytoin and valproate, calcium-channel blockers such as nifedipine and amlodipine, cyclosporine and high-dose oral contraceptives.

Tetracycline exposure during tooth development can permanently discolor teeth; the risk period includes the latter half of pregnancy, infancy and childhood through age 8.

Transmucosal buprenorphine can also be associated with caries, fracture, tooth loss, abscesses and erosion. After the medicine fully dissolves, the patient should rinse gently with water and wait at least an hour before brushing; the benefits of buprenorphine outweigh these dental risks, and preventive care can help.

Substance use and tobacco cessation

Ask about tobacco and other substance use in a neutral, practical way, especially before considering an opioid. The ADA opioid statement advises reviewing a history of substance use, discussing safe storage and disposal, and using opioid-sparing strategies when possible.

Methamphetamine use is associated with rampant caries, often called meth mouth, bruxism and temporomandibular disorders. It blocks reuptake of dopamine, norepinephrine and serotonin; use those oral findings to guide a respectful conversation about symptoms and care.

Pregnancy and lactation

Preventive, diagnostic and restorative dental care is safe throughout pregnancy, and necessary emergency treatment should not be delayed solely because the patient is pregnant. Radiographs are considered safe at any point; ADA guidance also names penicillin, amoxicillin, cephalosporins, clindamycin and metronidazole as antibiotic options. Ask the obstetric clinician about pain relievers because the FDA cited limited evidence for specific recommendations.

The ADA recommends avoiding nitrous oxide exposure during pregnancy for patients and staff. The old pregnancy letter categories were removed from prescription drug labels; look instead to the narrative pregnancy and lactation sections.
