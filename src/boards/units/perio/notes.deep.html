The periodontium in health and gingivitis (2017)

Start with two questions when classifying a gingival finding: is there attachment loss, and has the patient previously had periodontitis? An intact periodontium has no attachment loss or radiographic bone loss, probing depths of 3 mm or less, and BOP below 10%. Gingivitis on an intact periodontium crosses the 10% BOP threshold while keeping probing depths at 3 mm or less and without periodontitis.

The percentage of bleeding sites also gives the extent label for gingivitis. From 10% through 30% is localized, while a value above 30% is generalized. These are proportions of examined sites, not counts of teeth.

Do not treat attachment loss as proof of current periodontitis without its context. Recession or crown lengthening can leave a non-periodontitis patient with attachment loss on a reduced periodontium. The health pattern can still include shallow depths and BOP below 10%, and bone loss may be present in that reduced setting.

A successfully treated periodontitis patient is another reduced-periodontium pattern, but the lifetime history remains relevant. Stable health allows depths through 4 mm provided no 4 mm site bleeds and BOP remains under 10%. If a 4 mm site bleeds, it no longer fits the closed-pocket description. In this patient group, gingivitis is identified by BOP at or above 10% at shallow sites of 3 mm or less.

Biofilm, calculus and risk factors

Think of plaque as an organized microbial biofilm interacting with the host. Periodontal attachment and bone loss arise through the immune response to this biofilm. Calculus develops after plaque mineralizes; its rough texture favors further bacterial attachment and growth. That makes calculus an important plaque-retentive surface, while the plaque biofilm remains central to the disease process.

This distinction helps explain why the therapy sequence uses two kinds of mechanical plaque control. Supragingival deposits and plaque-retentive factors are handled in the first step, while the second step reaches subgingival biofilm and calculus. Mineral composition and differences among calculus locations are outside these treatment distinctions.

Gingivitis and periodontitis have different reversibility in the current disease-course statement. When gingival inflammation resolves, the site returns to health. Once periodontitis has produced attachment loss, the loss is currently treated as irreversible in the general course of disease. This does not mean attachment can never be gained: the EFP S3 guideline describes regenerative procedures that can restore some attachment in selected defects. After successful treatment, the patient has a stable reduced periodontium and retains a history of periodontitis.

Local and systemic modifiers can change how much gingivitis appears. A prominent subgingival restoration edge or hyposalivation may make plaque accumulation easier. Potential systemic modifiers include hormonal states such as puberty and pregnancy, high glucose, leukemia, smoking and malnutrition. These categories are potential influences rather than a ranking of risk. Menstrual cycle-associated gingivitis was dropped from the classification because its signs are rarely visible, and today's low-dose oral contraceptives don't cause the changes reported with the old high-dose pills.

Smoking has a masking effect on visible inflammation: a smoker can have less gingival inflammation than the plaque level might lead one to expect. No bleeding percentage is assigned to this effect. Smoking is not protective; it is a periodontitis risk and a modifier used in grading. Diabetes is another major periodontitis risk and a grading modifier.

In children with poorly controlled type 1 diabetes, gingivitis is consistently seen, and glycemic control may matter more to its severity than plaque quality.

The first therapy step makes risk management actionable. Smoking cessation and better metabolic control are part of periodontal treatment planning and are revisited during supportive care. EFP S3 does not establish exercise, dietary counseling or weight reduction as proven periodontal therapy, so those should not be added as guaranteed interventions.

The periodontal exam: probing, CAL, BOP, mobility, furcation

Classify a periodontitis case by attachment loss pattern and by excluding look-alike causes. One route is interdental CAL at two or more non-adjacent teeth. The other is buccal or oral CAL of at least 3 mm together with pocketing over 3 mm at two or more teeth. In either route, the attachment loss cannot be explained by another condition.

Alternative causes named in the classification include traumatic recession, cervical caries, a second molar affected by third-molar position or removal, an endodontic lesion draining through marginal tissues, and vertical root fracture. They matter because attachment loss alone does not establish periodontitis if a different cause better explains that site.

Use gentle probing when applying the gingival health thresholds: their reference table assumes 0.2–0.25 N of force. Record the site findings as depth, CAL, BOP and mobility, with furcation measurements when relevant.

Furcation classifications describe different dimensions of a defect. Glickman grades progress from early involvement to a through-and-through opening; grade IV adds recession that makes the opening visible. The Hamp, Nyman and Lindhe system describes horizontal loss: less than 3 mm, more than 3 mm without complete passage, or a through-and-through defect. Tarnow and Fletcher add a vertical measurement from the furcation fornix, grouped at 3 mm or less, 4–6 mm, and 7 mm or more.

Keep those systems separate when reporting findings: one uses a descriptive grade, another measures horizontal reach, and the third records vertical depth. The values cannot be substituted for one another because each captures a different direction or clinical appearance.

Miller grade 3 is the one with vertical (depressible) movement; the 2017 staging chart counts mobility of degree 2 or more as a stage IV complexity factor.

Radiographic assessment

The ADA/FDA 2012 selection guidance bases periodontal imaging on examination findings and treatment history. That leaves room for clinical judgment: selected bitewings, periapicals or other appropriate images may be used when they answer the patient's question. The guidance describes views of alveolar support, the interproximal crest, roots, furcation bone and calculus as useful targets. Nonspecific gingivitis on its own is not a periodontal indication, although the patient may need images for caries, a new-patient assessment or another reason.

Do not convert the ADA/FDA selection guidance into a fixed recall schedule. It sets no standard interval for periodontal radiographs, and the requested images depend on the clinical exam. This is different from a blanket instruction to repeat images at every supportive visit.

Standard two-dimensional radiographs appear diagnostically adequate for periodontitis overall, but measurement uncertainty matters. The pooled estimate rests on three studies, and many included studies had unclear or high risk of bias. A 2025 review describes both underestimation of early bone loss and overestimation of severe loss, so a 2D image can err in either direction, and there is no fixed correction in millimeters.

Furcation assessment is a more specific limitation. Agreement between clinical examination and two-dimensional imaging varies substantially, with a tendency for the images to understate the furcation defect. CBCT is sensitive and specific for locating and classifying furcation involvement, but its use is a case-by-case choice that weighs risk, likely benefit and cost. CBCT is not routine imaging for every periodontitis stage.

Radiographic bone loss also enters staging and grading, but in defined roles. Stage uses the greatest interdental CAL first and relies on radiographic bone loss when CAL cannot be used. Severity ranges distinguish loss limited to the coronal third from loss extending into the middle third or farther. For grade, the percentage of bone lost divided by age is an indirect signal when longitudinal evidence is unavailable; use the classification cutoffs.

Periodontitis: staging, extent and grading

The 2017 World Workshop classification places three conditions under the periodontitis umbrella: periodontitis, necrotizing periodontitis and periodontitis as a direct manifestation of systemic disease. “Chronic periodontitis” and “aggressive periodontitis” are older terms; the current scheme no longer separates patients into those two categories. A diagnosis still depends on the case definition, including the required CAL pattern and exclusion of non-periodontal explanations.

Stage represents disease severity and management complexity. Begin at the interdental site with the greatest CAL. If that measurement is unavailable, use radiographic bone loss. Tooth loss attributable to periodontitis can modify the result, and complexity factors can raise it above the stage suggested by tissue loss alone. This order prevents a broad average from hiding the worst site.

Severity bands use both CAL and bone-loss location. Stage I has 1–2 mm CAL and bone loss under 15% in the coronal third, with no periodontitis-related tooth loss. Stage II has 3–4 mm CAL and 15–33% bone loss still within the coronal third, also without tooth loss. Stages III and IV begin at CAL of 5 mm or more with bone loss reaching the middle third or farther; tooth loss from periodontitis distinguishes up to four teeth lost for stage III from five or more for stage IV.

Complexity matters after severity. Stage I allows maximum probing depth up to 4 mm and mainly horizontal loss; stage II allows up to 5 mm with the same broad bone-loss pattern. Stage III complexity includes 6 mm or deeper pockets, vertical loss of at least 3 mm, class II or III furcation, or moderate ridge defects. Stage IV adds needs such as complex rehabilitation, secondary occlusal trauma with mobility degree 2 or greater, severe ridge defects, bite collapse or drifting, and fewer than 20 remaining teeth.

Extent is attached to the stage, not used in place of it. Describe disease as localized when fewer than 30% of teeth are involved, generalized when it is more widespread, or as a molar/incisor pattern when that distribution applies. A staging case therefore needs both the severity information and enough affected-tooth data to assign extent.

Grade estimates the likely rate of progression, response to ordinary treatment and possible systemic impact. Start from grade B, then look for evidence that supports moving toward A or C. Longitudinal change in bone level or CAL is direct evidence when available: no change over five years supports A, less than 2 mm supports B, and 2 mm or more supports C.

If there is no longitudinal comparison, use indirect evidence. Divide bone-loss percentage by patient age: below 0.25 points to A, 0.25 through 1.0 to B, and above 1.0 to C. The phenotype provides a second check: destruction that is modest relative to heavy biofilm fits A; destruction tracking the biofilm fits B; destruction out of proportion or a pattern suggesting rapid progression or early onset fits C.

Smoking and diabetes can modify grade. A nonsmoker without diabetes aligns with A; fewer than 10 cigarettes daily, or diabetes with HbA1c under 7.0%, aligns with B; 10 or more cigarettes daily, or HbA1c at least 7.0%, aligns with C. The patient details should include smoking quantity and A1C when grading depends on these modifiers.

The classification also updates the name “biologic width” to supracrestal tissue attachment, made up of junctional epithelium plus supracrestal connective tissue. A restorative margin placed into the connective-tissue portion is associated with inflammation and loss of support. This concept is related to maintaining periodontal tissues around restorations, while the stage and grade describe the periodontitis classification itself.

Abscesses, necrotizing disease and endo-perio lesions

Acute periodontal lesions are not all the same process. A periodontal abscess is a localized pus collection in the wall of a pocket or sulcus, with rapid tissue destruction and potential systemic spread. Most arise in a pre-existing periodontal pocket, so look for an established periodontal context when interpreting the acute change.

Necrotizing periodontal disease has a characteristic three-part clinical picture: papilla necrosis, bleeding and pain. It is associated with impaired host immune responses.

An endo-periodontal lesion represents a pathologic connection between pulpal and periodontal tissues. Root damage—such as fracture or perforation—is associated with the worst prognosis. This root condition should be kept in the assessment because it can explain the communication and affect the likely outcome.

Mucogingival conditions and recession

A mucogingival description records more than the amount of exposed root. The gingival phenotype includes tissue thickness and keratinized-tissue width. Describe recession depth, whether the cementoenamel junction is detectable, and whether a cervical step exceeds 0.5 mm. The CEJ code distinguishes detectable (A) from not detectable (B); the cervical-step notation records whether the step is present.

Cairo recession types compare attachment loss between the interproximal and buccal surfaces. RT1 has no interproximal attachment loss. RT2 has interproximal loss no greater than the buccal loss. RT3 has greater interproximal than buccal loss. In practical classification, determine the direction of that comparison before assigning a type; the labels encode the relationship, not simply recession depth.

Peri-implant health, mucositis and peri-implantitis

Use changes over time to distinguish peri-implant health from disease. Health has no clinical inflammation, no bleeding or suppuration with gentle probing, no increase in probing depth over previous exams, and no bone loss past initial remodeling. A single “normal depth” range cannot define health, and reduced bone support can coexist with health around an implant.

Mucositis is confined to inflamed soft tissue: bleeding or suppuration occurs on gentle probing, but there is no bone loss beyond initial remodeling. Plaque causes it, and the condition can resolve when plaque control is restored. This is why mucositis should not be assigned the same diagnostic label as peri-implantitis when bone support has not changed beyond remodeling.

Peri-implantitis includes inflammatory signs plus increased probing depth compared with earlier records and bone loss beyond the initial remodeling period. When prior measurements do not exist, the classification allows a combined threshold: bleeding or suppuration, probing depth at least 6 mm, and bone at least 3 mm apical to the coronal boundary of the implant's intraosseous portion. Each element contributes to the diagnosis; the threshold is not a stand-alone depth rule.

Baseline records make later comparison possible. Obtain probing and radiographs once the implant prosthesis is completed. Epidemiologic bone-loss thresholds should exceed the mean measurement error of 0.5 mm, so a small apparent difference should not automatically be interpreted as biological change.

Mucositis is assumed to precede peri-implantitis, but the latter is described as progressing faster than periodontitis and in a nonlinear, accelerating pattern. Severe periodontitis history, poor plaque control and missed maintenance have strong evidence linking them to peri-implantitis. Evidence for smoking and diabetes is inconclusive, so do not state those links with the same certainty.

Prognosis

A tooth prognosis summarizes expected retention, not a permanent property of a tooth. Conventional categories include good, fair, poor, questionable and hopeless. McGuire and Nunn's system weighs attachment, furcation, mobility, crown-to-root ratio and root form. Kwok and Caton's approach includes both local findings and patient-level considerations such as compliance, plaque control, smoking and diabetes.

Useful local factors include probing depth, attachment and bone loss, plaque-retentive anatomy, occlusal trauma, habits, mobility, furcation, tooth type and whether the tooth serves as an abutment. These variables help describe risk but do not make a single measurement a certain forecast. Studies disagree about the direction of age's effect, so do not use age alone to predict tooth loss.

Prognostic systems generally identify retained teeth better than teeth that will be lost. A five-year maintenance cohort found that loss was much more common in the worst category than in the best, yet most teeth called hopeless remained in the mouth. Other studies also found that hopeless and questionable labels sometimes predicted loss that did not occur. In short: a poor label signals concern, but it does not guarantee loss.

Studies of treated patients identify bone loss, furcation, probing depth, mobility, tooth type, crown-to-root ratio and abutment use as tooth-level predictors. Even very advanced bone loss does not make retention impossible; teeth with more than 75% loss have remained in service for a decade or longer in a maintenance cohort. These are cohort findings: they argue against writing a tooth off, not for promising it will survive.

Patient factors also matter. Smoking and age were the patient factors most consistently linked to tooth loss in a systematic review cited by a later model; diabetes predicted loss in another long-term cohort. A model also included heavy smoking, bruxism, severe disease and baseline tooth count. Findings on age pointed in different directions across studies, so avoid using older or younger age alone to assign prognosis.

Reassess prognosis during treatment and maintenance. Attachment or bone gain, less mobility or less furcation involvement can improve a tooth's status. Motivation, stress and diabetes control can change on the patient side. A questionable tooth in a motivated patient with supportive conditions may perform differently from the same tooth in someone who misses care and has other risks. Restorability and endodontic status must also be considered: most of the teeth extracted in one maintenance cohort were lost to fracture, caries or endodontic problems rather than periodontitis.

The EFP S3 guideline permits considering extraction at any step when a tooth is judged hopeless. The guideline does not say to extract every questionable tooth. For molars, Class II or III furcation involvement with residual pockets is a reason to provide periodontal therapy, not an extraction rule. Mandibular molars with residual pockets and Class II furcation have a recommendation for regenerative surgery, and survival has been reasonable after treatment, especially for Class II compared with Class III involvement.

Nonsurgical therapy, reevaluation and adjuncts

The EFP S3 treatment sequence begins with step 1 for every patient regardless of stage. Control supragingival biofilm, remove plaque-retentive factors, provide mechanical plaque removal and address risk factors such as smoking and diabetes. The patient should be engaged in behavior change, with oral hygiene instruction tailored to their needs and repeated during supportive care. Step 1 is revisited often rather than treated as a one-time orientation.

Step 2 is subgingival instrumentation to disrupt and remove subgingival biofilm and calculus. Hand instruments, powered sonic or ultrasonic instruments, and combinations of them are all supported. Delivery can be quadrant by quadrant or full mouth within 24 hours. Both schedules are accepted; one format is not required for every patient.

The distinction between steps follows deposit location. In step 1, professional mechanical plaque removal addresses supragingival plaque and calculus plus local factors that retain plaque. In step 2, subgingival instruments target deposits below the gingiva. Calculus matters as a rough, plaque-retentive surface; periodontitis is driven by the host response to the plaque biofilm.

Reevaluation asks whether the response has reached the therapy endpoint: there should be no bleeding pocket deeper than 4 mm and no pocket at least 6 mm deep. If either type of residual finding remains, step 3 is considered. When the endpoint is reached, transition into supportive care. This creates a decision point between continued nonsurgical care, possible surgery, and maintenance.

For moderately deep residual pockets of 4–5 mm, repeat subgingival instrumentation. For a residual pocket at least 6 mm deep in stage III periodontitis after steps 1 and 2, access flap surgery is suggested. Those paths are not interchangeable: the pocket depth and treatment history determine which recommendation applies.

Adjuncts should be interpreted by the strength and scope of the recommendation. Systemic antibiotics are not routine because of patient-health and public-health concerns, though the guideline allows consideration for selected groups, including young adults with generalized stage III disease. The existence of a selected-use category is not permission to prescribe antibiotics broadly.

The EFP S3 guideline says some locally delivered sustained-release antibiotics and chlorhexidine may be considered. The recommendation is weak, studies have high risk of bias and the reported benefit is short term; neither option is a required standard or proven better over the long term. Chlorhexidine rinse may be considered for a limited time in specific cases, not as routine care.

Other adjunct recommendations are against using a range of products with subgingival instrumentation. The guideline suggests not using lasers, probiotics or sub-antimicrobial-dose doxycycline, and recommends against local or systemic NSAIDs, local statin gels, omega-3 fatty acids and local metformin gel. A recommendation against is not proof of harm or a contraindication.

Local-delivery labels add product-specific details. Arestin places minocycline hydrochloride microspheres below the gingiva; each unit-dose cartridge provides 1 mg of minocycline free-base equivalent, and the total amount varies with the pockets treated. Its label indication is adjunctive use with scaling and root planing to reduce pocket depth. Sensitivity to minocycline or tetracyclines is a contraindication. Pregnancy, nursing and age under 8 are cautions that require benefit-risk consideration, not absolute contraindications. Trial use in pockets of 5 mm or more with repeated applications at 3-month intervals describes the study protocol, not a labeled minimum depth.

The PerioChip FDA label, effective in 2010, describes a biodegradable chlorhexidine chip containing 2.5 mg. It is placed with nonsurgical periodontal instrumentation in a pocket at least 5 mm deep, with repeat treatment every three months when the site remains at least 5 mm. The patient avoids flossing the treated site for 10 days. The EFP S3 recommendation that sustained-release chlorhexidine may be considered remains weak; the label does not recommend PerioChip in an acute periodontal abscess because this use was not studied.

Surgical therapy overview

Surgical therapy sits after nonsurgical treatment and a readiness check. The recommendation applies to periodontal and implant surgery: do not proceed when the patient has not achieved and maintained adequate self-performed oral hygiene. The guideline sets no plaque-score cutoff for 'adequate'.

The EFP S3 guideline recommends regenerative surgery for persistent deep pockets linked to intrabony defects at least 3 mm deep after steps 1 and 2. It also recommends it for mandibular molars with a residual pocket and Class II furcation. For a maxillary buccal Class II furcation, the EFP S3 wording is weaker: it suggests the approach. These distinctions depend on tooth location and furcation class.

For an intrabony defect, EFP S3 recommends guided tissue regeneration membrane or enamel matrix derivative; a bone-derived graft can be added. The guideline reports no statistically significant difference in CAL gain between enamel matrix derivative and guided tissue regeneration, which does not establish that the methods are equivalent. Choose the biomaterial based on defect configuration. An interdental papilla-preserving flap design is also recommended.

Step 3 distinguishes access from pocket reduction. For stage III disease with a remaining pocket of at least 6 mm after adequate step 2, EFP S3 suggests tissue reshaping to reduce the pocket, while recognizing that recession may increase. For a 4–5 mm residual pocket, repeat instrumentation is the suggested nonsurgical path. Resective surgery here means reshaping tissue; it is not root resection.

Furcation cases can have several acceptable approaches. For mandibular or maxillary Class III defects, or multiple Class II defects on one tooth, the EFP S3 guideline allows consideration of nonsurgical instrumentation or open-flap access, along with tunnel creation, root splitting or root removal. The underlying evidence is observational, so no single option is established as the correct choice. Nor is a tooth-survival advantage shown for these more involved options compared with instrumentation or open-flap debridement.

Furcation involvement alone is not an extraction indication. The guideline recommends therapy for molars with Class II or III furcation and residual pockets, and recommends regenerative surgery for mandibular molars with residual pockets and Class II involvement. Treated furcation-involved molars have shown reasonable survival over a wide follow-up period; Class II outcomes were better than Class III outcomes in the reviewed evidence.

Supportive periodontal therapy

Supportive care follows active therapy when the clinical endpoint has been reached. Schedule visits every 3 months up to a maximum interval of 12 months, choosing the interval according to the patient's risk profile and periodontal condition. The guidance is a range that can be tailored, not an instruction that every person wait a full year or return every three months forever.

EFP S3 strongly promotes adherence for long-term stability. It suggests routine professional mechanical plaque removal to limit tooth loss and maintain the periodontium. Continue patient instruction, including interdental cleaning, and tailor it to the person's needs.

Risk control continues after active treatment. Smoking cessation and diabetes control are part of risk-factor management, while the maintenance visit is also an opportunity to revisit patient behavior and reinforce oral hygiene. EFP S3 supports repeated instruction but does not establish motivational interviewing or cognitive behavioral therapy as proven ways to improve oral hygiene.

The treated patient's disease history remains important when deciding whether tissues are stable. Health is on a reduced periodontium: depths can be up to 4 mm, no site at 4 mm or deeper should bleed, and BOP should be under 10%. A bleeding 4 mm site no longer fits the closed-pocket description. Gingivitis in this patient group instead uses BOP at 10% or more at sites of 3 mm or less.

Some maintenance adjuncts are discouraged in the EFP S3 guideline. It suggests not adding sub-antimicrobial-dose doxycycline or photodynamic therapy to plaque removal and not replacing conventional plaque removal with an Er:YAG laser. These are weak recommendations against, not declarations that the options are contraindicated or harmful. EFP S3 permits a powered brush instead of a manual model, without claiming greater benefit.

Perio and systemic health; drug-influenced enlargement

Periodontitis is common among US adults with natural teeth, and the supporting-tissue damage reflects the host immune response to plaque biofilm. Periodontal disease is associated with cardiovascular disease and diabetes, but an association does not show that one directly causes the other. Smoking and poor diet are shared risk factors.

The diabetes relationship is described in both directions. Periodontitis is linked to higher A1C among people with type 2 diabetes, and scaling and root planing is associated with a reduction at three months that is larger at six months. Include a question about the most recent A1C in the health history. This relationship supports coordinating risk management, while it does not turn periodontal treatment into a substitute for diabetes care.

Several medication groups are associated with gingival enlargement that is more pronounced than inflammation alone would produce. The list includes phenytoin and sodium valproate; calcium channel blockers such as nifedipine, verapamil, diltiazem, amlodipine and felodipine; cyclosporine; and high-dose oral contraceptives. Recognizing the medication history helps distinguish drug-influenced enlargement from gingivitis caused by plaque or the potential modifiers in the 2017 classification.
