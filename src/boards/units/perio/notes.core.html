The periodontium in health and gingivitis (2017)

Describe gingival health by the periodontium underneath it. On an intact periodontium, health means no attachment loss, probing depths no greater than 3 mm, no radiographic bone loss, and bleeding on probing (BOP) at fewer than 10% of sites. Plaque-induced gingivitis begins at 10% BOP when depths remain 3 mm or less and there is no periodontitis.

Extent is based on the share of sites that bleed: 10% to 30% is localized gingivitis, and more than 30% is generalized. A patient can have attachment loss and still have gingival health on a reduced periodontium, such as after recession or crown lengthening, if depths are 3 mm or less and BOP is under 10%.

After successful periodontitis treatment, a patient can reach gingival health on a reduced periodontium, but the history of periodontitis remains part of the diagnosis. At this stable state, all depths are 4 mm or less, no 4 mm site bleeds, and BOP is below 10%. A bleeding 4 mm site is not a closed pocket; gingivitis in this group is BOP at 10% or more at sites no deeper than 3 mm.

Biofilm, calculus and risk factors

Plaque is a biofilm whose presence and host response matter more than calculus as a material. Calculus forms as plaque mineralizes; its irregular surface supports bacterial adhesion and growth. The disease process should not be reduced to calculus alone: the biofilm is the central microbial community, while calculus can provide a plaque-retentive surface.

Gingivitis can resolve when inflammation is treated. Once periodontitis has caused attachment loss, that loss is currently considered irreversible in the general course of disease, although the EFP S3 guideline recognizes attachment gain after regenerative care in selected defects. A treated patient therefore has a reduced periodontium rather than returning to the intact baseline.

Local plaque-retentive features, including a prominent restoration margin or reduced salivary flow, can modify plaque-induced gingivitis. Potential systemic modifiers include puberty and pregnancy, hyperglycemia, leukemia, smoking, and malnutrition. These are modifiers, not a ranked list of causes; current low-dose oral contraceptives and menstrual-cycle changes should not be used as stand-alone diagnoses of visible gingivitis.

Smoking and diabetes are important periodontitis risks and both affect grading. Smoking can mute the visible gingival response to plaque, so less redness or bleeding does not mean protection. Among pediatric patients whose type 1 diabetes remains poorly controlled, gingivitis is consistently seen, and glycemic control may matter more to its severity than plaque quality; do not generalize this finding beyond that group.

The periodontal exam: probing, CAL, BOP, mobility, furcation

Use attachment loss at more than one tooth to distinguish periodontitis from isolated gingival inflammation. A case is met by interdental CAL at two or more non-adjacent teeth, or by buccal or oral CAL of at least 3 mm with pocketing over 3 mm at two or more teeth, once non-periodontal explanations have been excluded. Possible explanations include traumatic recession, cervical caries, third-molar effects on a second molar, an endodontic drainage path, or vertical root fracture.

The 2017 gingival thresholds assume light probing force of 0.2 to 0.25 N. Record probing depth, CAL, BOP, mobility and furcation findings by tooth and site. A complete probing sequence and an arithmetic method for deriving CAL from probing depth and recession are not detailed here.

Radiographic assessment

For a patient with periodontal disease or a history of treatment, the ADA/FDA selection guidance makes the need and image type a clinical decision based on the exam. Selected bitewings or periapicals may show supporting bone level, the interproximal crest, root shape and length, furcation bone, and calculus. Nonspecific gingivitis alone does not establish a periodontal indication, but the guidance allows other images when another clinical reason exists.

Use radiographs with clinical measurements. A recent review found two-dimensional images reasonably useful for diagnosing periodontitis overall, but estimates have limits and may either understate initial loss or overstate severe loss. For furcation defects, clinical and two-dimensional findings agree inconsistently and the images tend to understate defect extent. CBCT can characterize furcations well, but the decision should weigh the patient's benefit, risk and cost rather than treating it as routine staging imaging.

Stage is based first on interdental CAL at the site of greatest loss; if CAL is unavailable, radiographic bone loss can be used. The stage criteria distinguish bone loss in the coronal third from extension into the middle third and beyond. The ADA/FDA selection guidance sets no fixed recall interval.

Periodontitis: staging, extent and grading

The 2017 classification recognizes periodontitis, necrotizing periodontitis, and periodontitis as a direct manifestation of systemic disease. The older split between “chronic periodontitis” and “aggressive periodontitis” was dropped. Confirm that the periodontitis case definition is met before assigning a stage.

Set stage severity from interdental CAL at the site with the greatest loss. Use radiographic bone loss if CAL is not available. Tooth loss due to periodontitis can raise the stage, and complexity can move a patient higher than severity alone would suggest. Add extent as localized, generalized, or a molar/incisor pattern. Localized disease involves fewer than 30% of teeth.

Grade estimates progression, expected response to standard treatment and possible systemic impact. Begin at grade B, then use direct evidence over five years when it is available: no loss supports A, under 2 mm supports B, and 2 mm or more supports C. Without direct evidence, the bone-loss percentage divided by age and the relationship between biofilm and destruction help distinguish the grades.

Smoking and diabetes are grade modifiers: no smoking or diabetes aligns with A, fewer than 10 cigarettes per day or HbA1c under 7.0% with diabetes aligns with B, and 10 or more cigarettes per day or HbA1c of 7.0% or greater aligns with C. The biologic-width term is now “supracrestal tissue attachment”; placing a restoration margin into its connective-tissue attachment is associated with inflammation and support loss.

Abscesses, necrotizing disease and endo-perio lesions

A periodontal abscess is a localized collection of pus within the gingival wall of a pocket or sulcus. It is an acute process with rapid destruction and possible systemic spread; most cases arise where a periodontal pocket already exists. Do not confuse a new acute lesion with the broader chronic history of attachment loss.

Necrotizing periodontal diseases form a distinct category. The typical pattern combines papilla necrosis, bleeding and pain, and these diseases are associated with an impaired host immune response. The three features should be considered together rather than treating any one as diagnostic by itself.

An endo-periodontal lesion is a pathologic connection between the pulp and periodontal tissues. Root damage, including fracture or perforation, is associated with the poorest prognosis among these lesions. This finding changes the outlook even when periodontal treatment is being considered.

Mucogingival conditions and recession

Describe the gingival phenotype using thickness and keratinized-tissue width. The recession description also records recession depth, whether the CEJ can be identified, and whether a cervical step greater than 0.5 mm is present. In the CEJ notation, A means detectable and B means not detectable.

Cairo recession types compare interproximal attachment loss with buccal attachment loss: RT1 has none interproximally, RT2 has no more than the buccal amount, and RT3 has more. The classification describes the tissue pattern; it does not by itself select treatment or predict root coverage.

Peri-implant health, mucositis and peri-implantitis

Peri-implant health is defined by the absence of clinical inflammation, bleeding or suppuration on gentle probing, a rise in probing depth from earlier examinations, and bone loss beyond initial remodeling. There is no single probing-depth range that defines health, and health can be present even when bone support is reduced.

Mucositis has bleeding or suppuration on gentle probing without bone loss beyond initial remodeling. Plaque is its cause, and restoring plaque control can resolve it. Peri-implantitis adds increased probing depth compared with prior measurements and bone loss beyond remodeling.

When prior records are unavailable, the diagnosis uses the combination of bleeding or suppuration, probing depth of at least 6 mm, and bone level at least 3 mm apical to the most coronal part of the implant's intraosseous portion. Record probing and a baseline radiograph after the implant prosthesis is completed so later change can be judged against a reference.

Mucositis is assumed to come before peri-implantitis, which progresses faster than periodontitis in an accelerating, nonlinear pattern. History of severe periodontitis, poor plaque control and missed maintenance have strong links with peri-implantitis; the evidence for smoking and diabetes is inconclusive.

Prognosis

Prognosis combines tooth-level findings and, in some systems, patient factors. Common categories are good, fair, poor, questionable and hopeless. Consider attachment, probing depth, bone loss, root form, furcation, mobility, crown-to-root ratio and abutment use, along with adherence, plaque control, smoking and diabetes.

A prognosis is a prediction rather than a guarantee. In maintenance cohorts, systems were better at identifying teeth likely to be retained than teeth that would be lost; many labeled hopeless remained in place.

Revisit prognosis as tooth support and patient risk control change. Restorability and endodontic status also matter. The EFP S3 guideline allows extraction for a tooth judged hopeless and says furcation alone is not a reason to extract.

Nonsurgical therapy, reevaluation and adjuncts

Periodontitis care begins with a first step for every patient, whatever the stage: control supragingival biofilm and manage risks such as smoking and diabetes. Professional plaque removal, correction of plaque-retentive factors and patient-tailored oral hygiene instruction belong here. Reinforce behavior change and repeat instruction during supportive care.

Step 2 is subgingival instrumentation to remove biofilm and calculus. Hand and powered instruments are both recommended, alone or together; treatment may be completed by quadrant or as a full-mouth course within 24 hours. The division matters: supragingival deposits and risk control start in step 1, while subgingival deposits are addressed in step 2.

Reevaluate after the first two steps. The endpoint is no pocket deeper than 4 mm that bleeds and no deep pocket of 6 mm or more. If those findings remain, consider step 3; if the endpoints are met, continue with supportive periodontal care. Moderately deep residual pockets of 4–5 mm call for repeated subgingival instrumentation, while deep residual pockets of 6 mm or more in stage III disease can lead to access flap surgery.

Adjuncts do not replace the core steps. Systemic antibiotics are not routine with instrumentation, though they may be considered for selected groups such as young adults with generalized stage III disease. EFP S3 says some sustained-release local antibiotics and chlorhexidine may be considered, while several other agents are advised against. These are recommendation strengths, not proof that an adjunct is mandatory or universally superior.

Surgical therapy overview

Surgery follows adequate nonsurgical care. Periodontal and implant surgery are not performed when the patient has not achieved and maintained adequate self-performed oral hygiene; no plaque-score threshold is specified.

After steps 1 and 2, the EFP S3 guideline recommends regenerative surgery for persistent deep pockets linked to intrabony defects at least 3 mm deep and for mandibular molars with residual pockets and Class II furcation. It suggests the approach for maxillary buccal Class II furcations. For intrabony defects, it recommends a membrane or enamel matrix approach, with an optional bone-derived graft; choose the material based on defect shape.

For a deep residual pocket in stage III disease after adequate step 2, the EFP S3 guideline suggests pocket-reduction surgery, recognizing that recession may increase. For selected Class III or multiple Class II furcations, its options include instrumentation, open flap access, root separation, root resection or tunneling. The evidence does not establish a tooth-survival advantage for these approaches over instrumentation or open flap debridement.

Furcation involvement alone is not a reason to remove a molar. For mandibular molars with residual pockets and Class II involvement, regenerative therapy is recommended; treated molars with furcations can survive for years, with better outcomes reported for Class II than Class III.

Supportive periodontal therapy

After active treatment reaches its endpoint, supportive periodontal care protects stability. Schedule visits every 3 months up to a maximum of 12 months, adjusting the interval to the patient's risk profile and periodontal condition. This is a range for individual scheduling, not a single universal recall interval.

Adherence should be strongly encouraged. EFP S3 suggests routine professional mechanical plaque removal to limit tooth loss and support stability. Repeat and tailor oral-hygiene instruction, including interdental cleaning. EFP S3 allows a powered brush instead of a manual model, without claiming greater benefit.

Supportive care still uses the treated-periodontitis health thresholds: depths up to 4 mm without bleeding at a 4 mm site, and BOP under 10%. A 4 mm site that bleeds is not a closed pocket. Periodontitis remains part of the patient's history even while the reduced periodontium is stable.

EFP S3 suggests against sub-antimicrobial-dose doxycycline or photodynamic therapy as maintenance adjuncts and against replacing conventional plaque removal with an Er:YAG laser. These are weak recommendations against, not statements that the options are harmful or contraindicated.

Perio and systemic health; drug-influenced enlargement

Periodontitis is associated with cardiovascular disease and diabetes, but the association does not show that one condition directly causes the other. Smoking and poor diet are shared risks. Periodontal disease affects a substantial share of US adults with teeth, and the supporting-tissue destruction reflects the immune response to plaque biofilm.

Diabetes and periodontitis have a bidirectional association: periodontitis is linked with higher A1C in type 2 diabetes, and scaling and root planing is associated with lower A1C at three months, with a larger reduction at six months. Ask for the most recent A1C as part of the medical history.

Some medicines are linked to gingival enlargement that exceeds what inflammation alone would explain. The list includes phenytoin and sodium valproate, several calcium channel blockers, cyclosporine, and high-dose oral contraceptives. Distinguish this drug-influenced enlargement from plaque-induced gingivitis and from the potential modifying factors used in gingivitis classification.
