### R01

**Patient:** Female, 53 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "I feel shaky and suddenly very hungry." (during a dental visit)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Type 2 diabetes treated with insulin
- Chronic kidney disease, stage G3b
- No food allergies; works as an office manager
**Current Findings:**
- Pretreatment glucose 66 mg/dL
- Awake, oriented, and able to swallow
- BP 122/76 mm Hg

**Stem:** After fast carbohydrate, which follow-up glucose meets the treatment target?

A. 68 mg/dL
B. 69 mg/dL
C. 70 mg/dL
D. 72 mg/dL

**Keyed answer:** D

**Explanation:** The 15-15 approach repeats fast carbohydrate until glucose rises above 70 mg/dL. The tempting wrong answer is exactly 70 mg/dL, which does not exceed the target.

**Why each option:**
- A: This result remains below the treatment target.
- B: This result remains below the treatment target.
- C: The target is above 70 mg/dL, not equal to 70 mg/dL.
- D: Correct. This result is above 70 mg/dL.

**Memory cue:** Recheck after 15 minutes; repeat fast carbohydrate until glucose exceeds 70 mg/dL.

**Tags:** cc CC9; fk FK6; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase tx

**Cited facts:**
- DM-4 [verified]: The 15-15 rule: give 15-20 g of fast carbohydrate (4 glucose tablets or 1 tube of glucose gel; 1/2 cup (4 oz) fruit juice or regular soda; 1 tablespoon sugar, honey or corn syrup), wait 15 minutes, recheck, and repeat until above 70 mg/dL. Patients with kidney disease should avoid orange juice (potassium); apple, grape or cranberry are fine.
  - Diabetes (Oral Health Topics) (Hypoglycemia): "Provide the patient with 15-20 grams of oral carbohydrates to eat or drink, such as: 12, 13 4 glucose tablets or one tube of glucose gel"
  - Low Blood Glucose (Hypoglycemia) (What should I do): "Wait 15 minutes and check your blood glucose level again. If your glucose level is still low, eat or drink another 15 to 20 grams of glucose or carbohydrates."

### R02

**Patient:** Male, 51 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "I need a filling before my work trip." (1 week)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency
- Takes prednisone 5 mg each morning
- No medication allergies; warehouse supervisor
**Current Findings:**
- BP 120/74 mm Hg, pulse 72/min
- One restoration with local anesthesia is planned
- No fever, vomiting, or hypotension

**Stem:** According to the Endocrine Society guideline for minor procedures under local anesthesia, what prednisone dose should he take one hour before treatment?

A. 5 mg
B. 10 mg
C. 20 mg
D. 40 mg

**Keyed answer:** B

**Explanation:** For a patient already taking prednisone below 10 mg daily, the guideline raises the dose to 10 mg one hour before a minor procedure under local anesthesia. The tempting wrong answer is 20 mg, the preprocedure hydrocortisone amount in a different regimen.

**Why each option:**
- A: This leaves him at his usual dose instead of the guideline’s minor-procedure dose.
- B: Correct. Raise prednisone to 10 mg one hour before the local-anesthetic procedure.
- C: The 20 mg figure is the preprocedure hydrocortisone dose in the divided hydrocortisone plan.
- D: This is not the recommended prednisone increase for a minor procedure.

**Memory cue:** On prednisone under 10 mg: raise to 10 mg one hour before local-anesthetic care.

**Tags:** cc CC8; fk FK8; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase plan

**Cited facts:**
- END-11 [verified]: Stress dosing for dental and other procedures under local anesthesia (Endocrine Society guidance): the 2024 ESE/Endocrine Society guideline treats minor surgery including any procedure requiring local anesthesia as minor stress for patients with or at risk of glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency. On prednisone under 10 mg: raise to 10 mg one hour before; on dexamethasone under 1 mg: raise to 1 mg. Patients already taking hydrocortisone 40 mg, prednisone 10 mg or dexamethasone 1 mg daily or more typically need no increase unless there are signs of hemodynamic instability. For patients with primary adrenal insufficiency on replacement therapy, the 2016 Endocrine Society guideline's sick day rule 1 is to double the routine oral glucocorticoid dose before a small outpatient procedure (e.g., dental work). (Conditions: The guideline says practice varies and robust evidence is lacking. Older dental teaching (no supplement for routine nonsurgical dental care; supplement only for major surgery) is not in an open source in this set and conflicts with these guidelines: items must name the Endocrine Society and must not key 'no supplementation is needed for dental care'.   - European Society of Endocrinology and Endocrine Society Joint Clinical Guideline: Diagnosis and Therapy of Glucocorticoid-induced Adrenal Insufficiency (Table 8): "Minor surgery including any procedure requiring local anesthesia If not on daily glucocorticoids : give oral hydrocortisone 40 mg total daily dose, to be given in three divided doses (eg, 20 mg one hour prior to the procedure, 10 mg six hours after the procedure, 10 mg after a further six hours)."
  - European Society of Endocrinology and Endocrine Society Joint Clinical Guideline: Diagnosis and Therapy of Glucocorticoid-induced Adrenal Insufficiency (Table 8): "If on hydrocortisone <40 mg total daily dose : increase to 40 mg total daily dose"
  - European Society of Endocrinology and Endocrine Society Joint Clinical Guideline: Diagnosis and Therapy of Glucocorticoid-induced Adrenal Insufficiency (Table 8): "If on prednisone <10 mg total daily dose : increase to 10 mg total daily dose, to be given one hour prior to the procedure."
  - European Society of Endocrinology and Endocrine Society Joint Clinical Guideline: Diagnosis and Therapy of Glucocorticoid-induced Adrenal Insufficiency (Table 8): "If on dexamethasone <1 mg total daily dose : increase to 1 mg total daily dose, to be given one hour prior to the procedure."
  - European Society of Endocrinology and Endocrine Society Joint Clinical Guideline: Diagnosis and Therapy of Glucocorticoid-induced Adrenal Insufficiency (Table 8, minor stress): "If the patient is already taking hydrocortisone ≥40 mg daily prednisone ≥10 mg daily, or dexamethasone ≥1 mg daily, there is typically no need to increase the dose unless there are signs of hemodynamic instability"

### R03

**Stem:** Each of the following reflects an appropriate first step for acute asthma bronchospasm in the dental chair EXCEPT one. Which is the EXCEPTION?

A. Keep the conscious patient upright as comfortable
B. Give supplemental oxygen
C. Use inhaled albuterol
D. Use fluticasone for immediate relief

**Keyed answer:** D

**Explanation:** Acute bronchospasm is treated with inhaled albuterol, while fluticasone is a maintenance controller. The tempting wrong answer confuses long-term inflammation control with fast relief.

**Why each option:**
- A: Upright positioning is part of the chairside approach for a conscious patient.
- B: Supplemental oxygen is included in dental-office acute asthma care.
- C: Albuterol is the short-acting medicine used for acute bronchospasm.
- D: Correct. Fluticasone is a controller and does not relieve acute bronchospasm.

**Memory cue:** Albuterol is the rescue medicine; fluticasone is a controller.

**Tags:** cc CC9; fk FK6; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase tx

**Cited facts:**
- RES-7 [verified]: An inhaled corticosteroid (e.g., fluticasone) is a maintenance controller, not a bronchodilator: it does not relieve acute bronchospasm and is contraindicated as primary treatment of status asthmaticus or other acute episodes that need intensive measures. Acute asthma symptoms are treated with an inhaled short-acting beta-2 agonist such as albuterol, the most effective medication for relieving acute bronchospasm. (Conditions: Not quoted: since the 2020 NHLBI focused update (and in GINA), some patients use one budesonide-formoterol inhaler as both controller and reliever; a corticosteroid-only inhaler such as fluticasone is never the reliever. For an in-office attack, albuterol is the expected answer.)
  - Fluticasone propionate HFA inhalation aerosol (authorized generic of Flovent HFA) (1 Indications and usage): "Fluticasone Propionate HFA is not indicated for the relief of acute bronchospasm."
  - Fluticasone propionate HFA inhalation aerosol (authorized generic of Flovent HFA) (4 Contraindications): "Primary treatment of status asthmaticus or acute episodes of asthma requiring intensive measures."
  - Fluticasone propionate HFA inhalation aerosol (authorized generic of Flovent HFA) (17 Patient counseling information): "Advise patients to treat acute asthma symptoms with an inhaled, short-acting beta 2 -agonist such as albuterol."
  - Expert Panel Report 3: Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Management of Asthma (Full Report 2007) (Section 3, Component 4: Safety of inhaled SABAs (key points)): "SABAs are the most effective medication for relieving acute bronchospasm (Evidence A)."
- RES-8 [verified]: Acute asthma attack (bronchospasm) in the dental chair: keep a conscious patient sitting as upright as is comfortable, give supplemental oxygen, and give a selective short-acting beta-2 agonist (albuterol) by metered-dose inhaler, about 2 to 4 inhalations; a spacer helps a patient who cannot coordinate the inhaler. Albuterol is preferred over epinephrine because it has little cardiac (beta-1) stimulation. Aminophylline and corticosteroids are not used for the initial acute treatment (limited efficacy or toxicity, and steroids take hours to act). (Conditions: Dental-office steps come from Anesthesia Progress CE reviews (Becker; Becker and Haas); the upright posture is the BTS general rule for conscious hypoxemic patients. Dose counts differ: albuterol label 2 inhalations (EM-8), Becker and Haas 2-4 inhalations, UK dental guidance up to 10 puffs via spacer: do not key one exact puff count without naming the source.)
  - Recognition and management of complications during moderate and deep sedation part 1: respiratory considerations (Bronchospasm): "Following primary assessment, including oxygen supplementation, a selective beta-2 agonist such as albuterol should be administered by metered inhaler."
  - Recognition and management of complications during moderate and deep sedation part 1: respiratory considerations (Bronchospasm): "This is preferred over epinephrine because it is less likely to produce positive cardiotonic side effects attributed to stimulation of cardiac beta-1 receptors."
  - Recognition and management of complications during moderate and deep sedation part 1: respiratory considerations (Pulmonary aspiration): "management should follow that for an asthmatic attack; a bronchodilator should be administered, eg, albuterol 2–4 inhalations."
  - Emergency drug kits: pharmacological and technical considerations (Drugs for allergy and asthma): "Spacer chambers can be attached to inhalers, and minimize the need for a coordinated effort on the part of the patient."
  - Recognition and management of complications during moderate and deep sedation part 1: respiratory considerations (Bronchospasm): "These are not recommended for initial acute treatment because of limited efficacy, significant toxicity (aminophylline), or delayed onset, eg, several hours for corticosteroids."
  - BTS guideline for oxygen use in adults in healthcare and emergency settings (Recommendation A5): "fully conscious hypoxaemic patients should ideally be allowed to maintain the most upright posture possible (or the most comfortable posture for the patient)"
- EM-8 [verified]: Albuterol metered-dose inhaler (a short-acting beta-2 agonist) treats or prevents bronchospasm in adults and children 4 years and older: 2 inhalations every 4 to 6 hours.
  - Albuterol sulfate inhalation aerosol (metered-dose inhaler) (Highlights, 2.1): "prevention of bronchospasm in adults and children 4 years of age and older: 2 inhalations every 4 to 6 hours."

### R04

**Patient:** Male, 32 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "I need a lower molar removed." (3 weeks)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Hemophilia A followed by a hematologist
- No anticoagulant or antiplatelet medicine
- No medication allergies; works in information technology
**Current Findings:**
- An inferior alveolar nerve block and extraction are being considered
- No active bleeding or facial swelling
- BP 122/76 mm Hg

**Stem:** Which plan is NOT appropriate before an inferior alveolar nerve block?

A. Coordinate the invasive procedure with his hematologist
B. Proceed without consultation because dental injections are all low risk
C. Consider buccal infiltration or intraligamentary anesthesia as alternatives
D. Arrange any needed systemic hemostatic measures before the block

**Keyed answer:** B

**Explanation:** An inferior alveolar block can create a deep hematoma, so systemic hemostasis should be planned with the hematologist. The tempting wrong answer generalizes the lower risk of slow fine-gauge infiltration to every injection.

**Why each option:**
- A: The hematologist should help plan invasive dental care.
- B: Correct. An inferior alveolar block is higher risk and should not proceed without a coordinated hemostasis plan.
- C: Buccal infiltration and intraligamentary anesthesia are lower-risk alternatives.
- D: Higher-risk blocks need systemic hemostatic measures arranged in advance.

**Memory cue:** Hemophilia plus a deep block: coordinate hemostasis with hematology first.

**Tags:** cc CC8; fk FK6; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase plan

**Cited facts:**
- BLD-1 [verified]: Hemophilia is a rare X-linked inherited bleeding disorder: hemophilia A is a deficiency of factor VIII and hemophilia B a deficiency of factor IX. Severity follows the factor level: severe under 1% of normal (spontaneous joint and muscle bleeding), moderate 1-5% (occasional spontaneous bleeding; prolonged bleeding after minor trauma or surgery), mild 5 to under 40% (severe bleeding with major trauma or surgery; spontaneous bleeding is rare).
  - WFH Guidelines for the Management of Hemophilia, 3rd edition (Ch. 2, Introduction): "Hemophilia is a rare X-linked congenital bleeding disorder characterized by a deficiency of coagulation factor VIII (FVIII), called hemophilia A, or factor IX (FIX), called hemophilia B."
  - WFH Guidelines for the Management of Hemophilia, 3rd edition (Table 2-1): "<1 IU/dL (<0.01 IU/mL) or <1% of normal Spontaneous bleeding into joints or muscles"
  - WFH Guidelines for the Management of Hemophilia, 3rd edition (Table 2-1): "1-5 IU/dL (0.01-0.05 IU/mL) or 1-5% of normal Occasional spontaneous bleeding; prolonged bleeding with minor trauma or surgery"
  - WFH Guidelines for the Management of Hemophilia, 3rd edition (Table 2-1): "5-40 IU/dL (0.05-0.40 IU/mL) or 5-<40% of normal Severe bleeding with major trauma or surgery; rare spontaneous bleeding"
- BLD-2 [verified]: Hemophilia: plan hemostasis individually with the patient's hematologist before any dental extraction or other invasive oral procedure (implant placement, periodontal surgery, gingival biopsy). Most restorative procedures, such as fillings, are low risk and can be done without factor replacement, and professional cleanings can be given with antifibrinolytics if needed. (Conditions: WFH gives no factor target level for extractions; the hematologist sets it. Dental guidance surveyed in a 2025 systematic review (haem-2025-dental-sr) varies widely, so don't key a factor percentage or U/kg dose for extractions.)
  - WFH Guidelines for the Management of Hemophilia, 3rd edition (Recommendation 2.7.7): "dental extraction or other invasive procedures within the oral cavity (e.g., dental implantation, periodontal surgery, or gum biopsy) be performed only with a personalized plan for hemostasis management in consultation with a hematologist"
  - WFH Guidelines for the Management of Hemophilia, 3rd edition (2.7, Dental surgery and invasive procedures): "Most restorative dental procedures such as dental fillings are low risk and can be carried out without the need for factor replacement therapy."
  - WFH Guidelines for the Management of Hemophilia, 3rd edition (2.7, Dental surgery and invasive procedures): "Professional dental cleanings can be provided with the use of antifibrinolytic agents, if necessary."
- BLD-3 [verified]: Local anesthesia in hemophilia: most dental injections are low risk when given with a vasoconstrictor, slowly, through a single-use fine-gauge needle. Higher-risk injections (inferior alveolar nerve block, superior alveolar nerve block, injections into the floor of the mouth or vascular lingual tissues) need systemic hemostatic measures before the procedure, arranged with the hematologist, to avoid hematoma. Intraligamentary (single-tooth) anesthesia or buccal infiltration, for example with 4% articaine, are effective alternatives to the inferior alveolar nerve block, even for mandibular molars. (Conditions: WFH does not give the factor level needed to cover a block; dental protocols reviewed in haem-2025-dental-sr differ, so don't key a number.)
  - WFH Guidelines for the Management of Hemophilia, 3rd edition (Recommendation 2.7.10): "Most dental injections pose a low risk for patients with hemophilia when delivered by a dental care professional using local anesthesia with a vasoconstrictor, and when the agent is delivered slowly with a single-use fine-gauge needle."
  - WFH Guidelines for the Management of Hemophilia, 3rd edition (Recommendation 2.7.11): "higher-risk intramuscular oral injections commonly associated with the provision of surgical dentistry (such as inferior alveolar dental block [IDB], superior alveolar nerve block, or injections in the floor of the mouth or vascular lingual tissues), the WFH recommends systemic hemostatic measures preoperatively to avoid the risk of hematoma"
  - WFH Guidelines for the Management of Hemophilia, 3rd edition (Recommendation 2.7.11, remark): "intraligamentary single-tooth anesthesia, or buccal infiltration injections with 4% articaine) are effective alternatives to IDB and permit dental procedures in primary and permanent mandibular molar teeth"

### R05

**Patient:** Male, 62 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "My lower tooth has broken and needs to come out." (1 month)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Cirrhosis with prior ascites, now clinically stable
- No anticoagulant or antiplatelet medicines
- No recent alcohol use; lives with his partner
**Current Findings:**
- INR 1.7 and aPTT 32 seconds from this week
- Platelets 47,000/mm³
- One simple extraction is planned; no active bleeding

**Stem:** How should these laboratory results guide preoperative planning?

A. Correct the INR to below 1.5 before every extraction
B. Use the INR as a validated predictor and proceed because it is below 2.0
C. Defer automatically until platelets exceed 50,000/mm³
D. Consult his physician and plan local hemostasis

**Keyed answer:** D

**Explanation:** In cirrhosis, INR and aPTT do not reliably predict procedural bleeding, and no validated dental extraction INR cutoff is established. A platelet count below 50,000/mm³ raises concern but is not an automatic contraindication, so the plan should be individualized with the physician and local hemostasis ready.

**Why each option:**
- A: Routine correction to a fixed INR target is not supported by the cited guidance.
- B: INR does not reliably predict bleeding in cirrhosis.
- C: The traditional 50,000/mm³ level is not an absolute contraindication below it.
- D: Correct. Review liver disease and labs with the physician, make a case-specific plan, and use local measures.

**Memory cue:** Cirrhosis labs do not give a dental INR cutoff; individualize and plan local hemostasis.

**Tags:** cc CC8; fk FK6; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase plan

**Cited facts:**
- LIV-1 [verified]: Liver disease can lower plasma coagulation factor levels, so coagulation and hemostasis tests can be informative before any invasive dental procedure. Consult the patient's physician on the degree of liver impairment and on drug choices, since liver disease can alter the metabolism of some drugs. (Conditions: ADA gives no INR or platelet cutoff; see LIV-2 (why the INR is a poor bleeding predictor in cirrhosis) and LIV-3 (platelets). Oral signs are in HEP-2.)
  - Hepatitis Viruses (Oral Health Topics) (Oral manifestations and dental considerations): "The degree of a patient's liver functional impairment can be determined via consultation with the medical physician, since liver disease has important implications for dental treatment."
  - Hepatitis Viruses (Oral Health Topics) (Oral manifestations and dental considerations): "Patients with liver disease may have decreased plasma coagulation factor concentrations; before any invasive procedure is performed in these patients, coagulation and hemostasis tests can be informative."
  - Hepatitis Viruses (Oral Health Topics) (Oral manifestations and dental considerations): "Liver disease may also result in alterations in the metabolism of certain drugs."
- LIV-2 [verified]: In cirrhosis, both coagulation and primary hemostasis are rebalanced (for platelet function, mainly by high von Willebrand factor levels), but the balance is unstable and can tip toward bleeding or thrombosis. The PT/INR and aPTT do not accurately describe hemostasis in cirrhosis and should not be used to predict bleeding; international guidelines do not recommend routine lab testing and correction of hemostatic factors before invasive procedures, preferring case-by-case decisions. In a large prospective study of decompensated cirrhosis, neither platelet count nor PT predicted procedure-related bleeding. (Conditions: From a 2026 review that summarizes current guidance (the AASLD, AGA and EASL documents themselves are not openly readable here). Traditional dental teaching and ADA (LIV-1) still say to get coagulation tests and consult the physician before surgery; items may key that step, but must not key 'the INR predicts bleeding in cirrhosis' or an INR cutoff for extractions in liver disease. In the same prospective study the review gives its highest bleeding figure for dental extractions (36.3%, vs 1.7-5.7% for other procedure types), so local hemostatic measures still matter; don't key the 36.3% figure itself.)
  - Managing Coagulation Abnormalities, Bleeding, and Thrombosis in Patients with Cirrhosis (Abstract): "prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time do not accurately describe hemostasis in patients with cirrhosis and should not be used to predict bleeding"
  - Managing Coagulation Abnormalities, Bleeding, and Thrombosis in Patients with Cirrhosis (Introduction): "both coagulation and primary hemostasis are rebalanced, though unstable, and may paradoxically tip toward hemorrhage or thrombosis"
  - Managing Coagulation Abnormalities, Bleeding, and Thrombosis in Patients with Cirrhosis (Introduction): "is rebalanced in cirrhosis, mainly due to the increased levels of the adhesive protein von Willebrand factor"
  - Managing Coagulation Abnormalities, Bleeding, and Thrombosis in Patients with Cirrhosis (Key points, invasive procedures): "International guidelines do not recommend the routine use of laboratory monitoring and management of hemostatic factors before invasive procedures, but suggest to consider decisions on a case-by-case basis in special settings"
  - Managing Coagulation Abnormalities, Bleeding, and Thrombosis in Patients with Cirrhosis (Invasive procedures): "Neither platelet count nor PT predicted adverse outcomes."
  - Managing Coagulation Abnormalities, Bleeding, and Thrombosis in Patients with Cirrhosis (Invasive procedures): "Bleedings occurred after dental extractions (36.3%), common bedside procedures (2.9%), vascular interventions (4.4%), endoscopic interventions (1.7%), and percutaneous interventions (5.7%)."
- LIV-3 [verified]: Platelets and dental surgery: a platelet count of 50,000/uL or more has traditionally been recommended before procedures that may bleed, such as extractions, and in many countries lower counts are corrected by platelet transfusion before surgery. A 2026 systematic review found more postoperative bleeding below 50,000/uL (12.9% vs 4.9%; risk ratio 2.95), mostly after oral surgery, but no evidence that a count under 50,000/uL is itself a contraindication to dental treatment; assess patients individually. For cirrhosis, an expert consensus set the lowest acceptable platelet count at 30 x 10^9/L (30,000/uL) for low-risk and 50 x 10^9/L (50,000/uL) for high-risk procedures; the same experts could not agree whether dental extractions are low- or high-risk. (Conditions: The review pooled thrombocytopenia from any cause (liver, hematologic, cancer), not only liver disease. Its evidence is case reports, case series, cohort studies and one trial; the risk ratio pools the 10 studies that had both a below- and an above-50,000 group with at least one bleed. The NIDCR oncology guide uses 75,000 for invasive dental care during chemotherapy (CH-2, CH-3); state the threshold in the stem or key 50,000 only as the traditionally recommended level. Don't key a cirrhosis platelet threshold specifically for extractions.)
  - Platelet Levels Associated With Bleeding Risk for Dental Interventions: A Systematic Review (Introduction): "it has been recommended that the platelet levels are ≥ 50,000/μL"
  - Platelet Levels Associated With Bleeding Risk for Dental Interventions: A Systematic Review (Introduction): "Hence the current practice in many countries is to correct thrombocytopenia with platelet transfusions before surgery"
  - Platelet Levels Associated With Bleeding Risk for Dental Interventions: A Systematic Review (Abstract, Results): "Bleeding rates for cases with platelet levels < 50,000/μL were 12.9%, and 4.9% for ≥ 50,000/μL. Risk ratio was 2.95"
  - Platelet Levels Associated With Bleeding Risk for Dental Interventions: A Systematic Review (Abstract, Conclusions): "There is no evidence that platelet counts < 50,000/μL is a contraindication for dental interventions, although findings suggest higher postoperative bleeding rates, particularly in oral surgery. Patients should be assessed individually"
  - Managing Coagulation Abnormalities, Bleeding, and Thrombosis in Patients with Cirrhosis (Invasive procedures): "agreement was reached on the lowest acceptable platelet count (30 × 10 9 /L and 50 × 10 9 /L for low- and high-risk procedures, respectively)"
  - Managing Coagulation Abnormalities, Bleeding, and Thrombosis in Patients with Cirrhosis (Invasive procedures): "Those without agreement included the placement of a transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt, esophagogastroduodenoscopy with band ligation, liver biopsy, and dental extractions."
  - Platelet Levels Associated With Bleeding Risk for Dental Interventions: A Systematic Review (Abstract, Results): "Six case reports, four case series, nine cohort studies and one trial fulfilled the inclusion."
  - Platelet Levels Associated With Bleeding Risk for Dental Interventions: A Systematic Review (Results): "allowing for pooled risk ratio analysis and heterogeneity assessments"

### R06

**Patient:** Male, 57 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "Two lower teeth have been hurting for a month." (1 month)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Atrial fibrillation treated with apixaban
- No antiplatelet therapy or prior bleeding
- No medication allergies; works as a chef
**Current Findings:**
- Two impacted molars require flap elevation and bone removal

**Stem:** What is the best next step for managing apixaban around this higher-bleeding-risk procedure?

A. Replace it with aspirin until the sockets heal
B. Stop it for a week without contacting the prescriber
C. Switch to warfarin and check an INR on the morning of surgery
D. Coordinate timing or a brief interruption with the physician

**Keyed answer:** D

**Explanation:** Higher-bleeding-risk procedures may call for scheduling late after the last dose or briefly delaying or interrupting a DOAC dose, but the plan needs physician coordination. The tempting wrong answer is an unsupervised week-long interruption.

- B: The dentist should not independently stop a DOAC for a week.

- D: Correct. Discuss procedure timing or a brief interruption with the prescriber.

**Memory cue:** Higher bleeding risk: coordinate any DOAC timing change with the physician.

**Tags:** cc CC8; fk FK8; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase plan

**Cited facts:**
- AC-3 [verified]: DOACs (dabigatran = direct thrombin inhibitor; apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban = factor Xa inhibitors): fixed doses, shorter half-lives, little or no monitoring (no INR), fewer interactions.   - Oral Anticoagulant and Antiplatelet Medications and Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Conclusions): "temporarily interrupting drug therapy for 24 to 48 hours"
  - Oral Anticoagulant and Antiplatelet Medications and Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Newer anticoagulants): "interventions not necessarily requiring discontinuation of the newer anticoagulants include extraction of 1 to 3 teeth"
