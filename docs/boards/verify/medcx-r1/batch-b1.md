### R01

**Patient:** Female, 46 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "This tooth is throbbing and my face feels swollen." (since yesterday)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Hypertension treated with amlodipine
- No written blood-pressure instructions from her physician
- No chest pain or neurologic symptoms
**Current Findings:**
- BP 168/104 mm Hg and 166/102 mm Hg on repeat
- Tooth 19 is tender to percussion with localized swelling
- Temperature 37.2°C

**Stem:** Urgent pain needs treatment. Under the ADA guide, what is the best approach for this adult?

A. Proceed unmonitored because diastolic BP is below 110
B. Postpone as elective care until BP is below 120/80
C. Treat symptoms; recheck pressure every 10 to 15 minutes
D. Use a 180/110 surgical threshold and ignore the pain

**Keyed answer:** C

**Explanation:** The repeated systolic pressure is in the ADA guide’s 160–180 mm Hg pain-emergency band, and her acute pain is driving the visit. The tempting wrong answer is the elective-care deferral rule, which does not describe this urgent pain pathway.

**Why each option:**
- A: A confirmed elevated reading still calls for the ADA guide’s emergency monitoring steps.
- B: That is not the emergency pathway when urgent pain relief is needed.
- C: Correct. For pain-driven emergency care in this systolic range, use anxiety reduction and check pressure every 10 to 15 minutes.
- D: The 180/110 threshold concerns possible deferral of elective major surgery, not this dental emergency.

**Memory cue:** Pain-driven emergency in the ADA band: reduce anxiety and recheck every 10–15 minutes.

**Tags:** cc CC8; fk FK6; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase plan

**Cited facts:**
- BP-3 [verified]: ADA Practical Guide thresholds for adult outpatient dental care: under 160/100: no modification for elective or emergency care. Over 160/100: repeat the reading; if it comes down, or is within a physician's written guidance, proceed; if confirmed, no elective care and refer to the physician. Emergency care at a confirmed 160-180 systolic and/or 100-109 diastolic when pain is driving the BP: treat, check BP every 10-15 minutes, use anxiety reduction. Confirmed >180 systolic and/or >109 diastolic: consult the physician before proceeding. Systolic >180 and/or diastolic >100: refer as soon as possible, or urgent medical evaluation if symptomatic. (Conditions: The ADA table itself is inconsistent: its body uses >109 diastolic for "consult before proceeding", its footnote >100 for "refer as soon as possible". Key items on the body thresholds (160/100 and 180/109) and avoid diastolic values of 101-109 where the two disagree.)
  - Hypertension (Oral Health Topics) (Table 3 (ADA Practical Guide)): "<160/100 No modification No modification >160/100* Repeat measurement If lowered or within written guidance from a physician, proceed If confirmed, no elective dental treatment"
  - Hypertension (Oral Health Topics) (Table 3): "initiate emergency care with blood pressure monitoring every 10 to 15 minutes during procedure; consider anxiety reduction techniques If confirmed systolic pressure >180 mmHg and/or diastolic pressure >109 mmHg, seek consultation with a physician before proceeding"
  - Hypertension (Oral Health Topics) (Table 3 footnote): "Patients with systolic pressure >180 mmHg and/or diastolic pressure >100 mmHg should be referred to their physician as soon as possible or sent for urgent medical evaluation, if symptomatic."
- BP-4 [verified]: Separately, the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline says deferring elective major surgery may be considered at >=180 systolic or >=110 diastolic. Many board review sources use "180/110 = defer elective dental care"; when a stem uses that number, frame it as the ACC/AHA surgical threshold, and key ADA's 160/100 only when the stem names the ADA guide. Yarows et al. (JADA 2020) note there is no evidence-based BP cutoff for deferring dental care. (Conditions: Yarows et al. (no evidence-based cutoff) is cited on the same page; quote not yet added.)
  - Hypertension (Oral Health Topics) (Dental treatment considerations): "deferring surgery may be considered in persons with hypertension and planned elective major surgery who have a systolic pressure of 180 mm Hg or higher or diastolic pressure of 110 mm Hg or higher"

### R02

**Stem:** What A1C threshold meets the laboratory criterion for diabetes?

A. At least 5.7%
B. At least 6.5%
C. At least 7.0%
D. At least 8.0%

**Keyed answer:** B

**Explanation:** An A1C of 6.5% or higher meets the diabetes criterion. A value near 7% is often discussed as an individualized treatment goal, which is the tempting mix-up here.

**Why each option:**
- A: This is within the prediabetes range, not the diabetes threshold.
- B: Correct. An A1C of 6.5% or higher meets the laboratory criterion for diabetes.
- C: This is a commonly used treatment goal for many patients, not the diagnostic cutoff.
- D: This higher value also indicates diabetes, but it is not the threshold asked for.

**Memory cue:** A1C 6.5% diagnoses diabetes; the treatment goal is individualized.

**Tags:** cc CC9; fk FK6; cognitive level 1; difficulty 1; phase dx

**Cited facts:**
- DM-1 [verified]: Diagnosis: A1C normal below 5.7%, prediabetes 5.7-6.4%, diabetes 6.5% or above. Fasting plasma glucose normal 99 mg/dL or below, prediabetes 100-125, diabetes 126 or above. 2-hour OGTT normal 139 or below, prediabetes 140-199, diabetes 200 or above. Random plasma glucose 200 or above (with symptoms) = diabetes.
  - Diabetes Tests & Diagnosis (Table: test results for diagnosis): "Normal below 5.7% 99 mg/dL or below 139 mg/dL or below N/A Prediabetes 5.7% to 6.4% 100 to 125 mg/dL 140 to 199 mg/dL N/A Diabetes 6.5% or above 126 mg/dL or above 200 mg/dL or above"
- DM-2 [verified]: A1C reflects average glucose over the past ~3 months; many people with diabetes aim for an A1C below 7%, but the goal is individualized (a higher goal may be safer with a history of severe hypoglycemia, limited life expectancy, etc.).
  - The A1C Test & Diabetes (What A1C goal should I have?): "some people with diabetes can reduce the risk of diabetes complications by keeping A1C levels below 7 percent"
  - The A1C Test & Diabetes (What A1C goal should I have?): "an A1C level that is safe for one person may not be safe for another"

### R03

**Stem:** At a glucose of 58 mg/dL, she is awake and can swallow. What is the best initial treatment?

A. Withhold carbohydrate because kidney disease raises potassium
B. Give a meal with protein and continue the appointment
C. Give 15 to 20 g of fast carbohydrate; recheck in 15 minutes
D. Give a sugar-free drink and continue the appointment

**Keyed answer:** C

**Explanation:** Her glucose is below 70 mg/dL, but she is alert and able to swallow, so the 15-15 approach applies. Because she has CKD, choose a fast carbohydrate such as glucose gel rather than orange juice; the tempting wrong answer is the slower snack, which will not correct the episode promptly.

**Why each option:**
- A: CKD calls for avoiding orange juice, not withholding fast carbohydrate.
- B: A meal is slower than the recommended fast carbohydrate, and treatment should pause until glucose recovers.
- C: Correct. Give fast oral carbohydrate, wait 15 minutes, recheck, and repeat until glucose is above 70 mg/dL.
- D: A sugar-free drink does not supply the fast carbohydrate needed to correct hypoglycemia.

**Memory cue:** Awake and swallowing: fast carbohydrate, then check again in 15 minutes.

**Tags:** cc CC9; fk FK6; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase tx

**Cited facts:**
- DM-3 [verified]: Hypoglycemia = blood glucose below 70 mg/dL. Symptoms: shaky, sweaty, fast or irregular heartbeat, dizzy, hungry, nervous, lip/tongue tingling, blurred vision, headache, confusion, irritable or combative, pale; severe = unable to eat or drink, seizures, unconsciousness (ADA dental page, "Hypoglycemia").
  - Low Blood Glucose (Hypoglycemia) (What should I do): "If your blood glucose level is below your target or less than 70 mg/dL"
  - Diabetes (Oral Health Topics) (Hypoglycemia): "Levels that are ≤70 mg/dL indicate hypoglycemia."
  - Diabetes (Oral Health Topics) (Hypoglycemia): "Shakiness Sweating Fast or irregular heartbeat Dizziness or lightheadedness Hunger Nervousness"
- DM-4 [verified]: The 15-15 rule: give 15-20 g of fast carbohydrate (4 glucose tablets or 1 tube of glucose gel; 1/2 cup (4 oz) fruit juice or regular soda; 1 tablespoon sugar, honey or corn syrup), wait 15 minutes, recheck, and repeat until above 70 mg/dL. Patients with kidney disease should avoid orange juice (potassium); apple, grape or cranberry are fine.
  - Diabetes (Oral Health Topics) (Hypoglycemia): "Provide the patient with 15-20 grams of oral carbohydrates to eat or drink, such as: 12, 13 4 glucose tablets or one tube of glucose gel"
  - Low Blood Glucose (Hypoglycemia) (What should I do): "Wait 15 minutes and check your blood glucose level again. If your glucose level is still low, eat or drink another 15 to 20 grams of glucose or carbohydrates."

### R04

**Stem:** She becomes less responsive and can no longer swallow safely. Which glucose-raising treatment should be given while help is called?

A. Oral glucose gel placed between the cheek and gum
B. A second dose of her usual insulin
C. Glucagon 1 mg by injection
D. Offer water by mouth

**Keyed answer:** C

**Explanation:** She cannot swallow safely, so the immediate glucose-raising intervention is injectable glucagon. The tempting wrong answer is oral glucose, which cannot be administered safely at this point.

**Why each option:**
- A: Oral glucose is not a safe immediate treatment when swallowing is impaired.
- B: Insulin would lower glucose further.
- C: Correct. Give glucagon by injection as the immediate glucose-raising treatment for an adult who cannot swallow.
- D: A drink is not the immediate glucose-raising treatment in this situation.

**Memory cue:** Unable to swallow safely: nothing by mouth.

**Tags:** cc CC9; fk FK6; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase tx

**Cited facts:**
- DM-5 [verified]: Unconscious or unable to swallow: nothing by mouth; call EMS; give glucagon. Glucagon for injection: adults and children 20 kg or more: 1 mg SC, IM (upper arm, thigh, buttocks) or IV; children under 20 kg: 0.5 mg (or 20-30 mcg/kg). If no response after 15 minutes, a second equal dose may be given while waiting for EMS. Turn the person on their side to prevent choking and give oral carbohydrate once they can swallow. Nasal glucagon: 3 mg in one nostril, adults and children 1 year and older; repeat 3 mg after 15 minutes if no response. Glucagon is contraindicated with pheochromocytoma and insulinoma.
  - Low Blood Glucose (Hypoglycemia) (Severe low blood glucose): "is the best way to treat severely low blood glucose"
  - Glucagon for Injection emergency kit, Amphastar (2 Dosage and Administration): "Adults and Pediatric Patients Weighing 20 kg or More: ■ The recommended dosage is 1 mg (1 mL) injected subcutaneously or intramuscularly into the upper arm, thigh, or buttocks, or intravenously."
  - Glucagon for Injection emergency kit, Amphastar (2 Dosage and Administration): "Pediatric Patients Weighing Less Than 20 kg: ■ The recommended dosage is 0.5 mg (0.5 mL) or dose equivalent to 20 to 30 mcg/kg"
  - Glucagon for Injection emergency kit, Amphastar (Patient instructions): "Turn the person on their side to prevent them from choking."
  - Baqsimi (glucagon) nasal powder, Amphastar (2.2): "The recommended dose of BAQSIMI is 3 mg administered as one actuation of the intranasal device into one nostril."
  - Baqsimi (glucagon) nasal powder, Amphastar (1 Indications): "severe hypoglycemia in adults and pediatric patients aged 1 year and older"
  - Glucagon for Injection emergency kit, Amphastar (4 Contraindications): "Pheochromocytoma because of the risk of substantial increase in blood pressure"

### R06

**Patient:** Male, 61 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "I get short of breath on the stairs." (2 months)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- COPD and type 2 diabetes
- Tiotropium inhaler and metformin 500 mg twice daily
- Hospital visit for breathing symptoms 3 weeks ago
- No medication allergies; retired teacher
**Current Findings:**
- BP 130/78 mm Hg, pulse 82/min
- Needs to stop after one flight of stairs
- Routine scaling and root planing is planned

**Stem:** What additional history should be clarified before assigning ASA status and planning care?

A. Only the patient’s age, because diagnoses are already listed
B. Review disease control, recent care, medicines, and function
C. The tooth number, because procedure site determines ASA class
D. Whether the patient prefers a morning appointment

**Keyed answer:** B

**Explanation:** ASA status depends on the severity of systemic disease and its effect on function, so the recent hospital visit and exertional limitation need follow-up. The tempting wrong answer is relying on the diagnosis list without checking how the disease is currently affecting the patient.

**Why each option:**
- A: Age alone does not describe systemic disease severity or functional limitation.
- B: Correct. Clarify current control, recent medical care, medicines, and functional status before describing systemic health.
- C: The procedure informs the dental plan but does not determine ASA status.
- D: Appointment preference does not clarify systemic disease or function.

**Memory cue:** Ask what changed, what treatment is advised, and what the patient can do day to day.

**Tags:** cc CC8; fk FK6; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase plan

**Cited facts:**

- ASA-3 [verified]: ASA III: severe systemic disease with substantive functional limitation, e.g., poorly controlled diabetes or hypertension, COPD, morbid obesity BMI 40 or more, active hepatitis, alcohol dependence or abuse, implanted pacemaker, moderately reduced ejection fraction, ESRD on regularly scheduled dialysis, premature infant under 60 weeks post-conceptual age, and a history more than 3 months ago of MI, stroke, TIA or CAD/stents.

- ASA-4 [verified]: ASA IV: severe systemic disease that is a constant threat to life, e.g., MI, stroke, TIA or CAD/stents within the last 3 months, ongoing cardiac ischemia, severe valve dysfunction, severely reduced ejection fraction, sepsis, DIC, ARD, or ESRD not on regularly scheduled dialysis.
  - ASA Physical Status Classification System (ASA statement, amended Dec 13, 2020), reproduced as Table A1 (Table A1): "A patient with severe systemic disease that is a constant threat to life Examples include (but not limited to): recent (<3 months) MI, CVA, TIA, or CAD/stents"

Penicillin or ampicillin allergy, oral: cephalexin 2 g (child 50 mg/kg) or another first- or second-generation oral cephalosporin in an equivalent dose; azithromycin or clarithromycin 500 mg (child 15 mg/kg); or doxycycline 100 mg (child under 45 kg: 2.2 mg/kg; 45 kg or more: 100 mg). Allergy and cannot take oral: cefazolin or ceftriaxone 1 g IM or IV (child 50 mg/kg). - IE-6 [verified]: Clindamycin is no longer recommended (oral or parenteral) for dental prophylaxis: it causes more frequent and severe reactions, including fatal C. difficile infection after a single dose; up to 15% of community-acquired C. difficile may trace to antibiotics prescribed for dental procedures.
  - Antibiotic Prophylaxis Prior to Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Additional Considerations): "no longer recommends use of clindamycin as an oral or parenteral alternative to amoxicillin or ampicillin in individuals with allergies to these drugs"
- IE-7 [verified]: Do not use a cephalosporin in someone whose penicillin/ampicillin reaction was anaphylaxis, angioedema or urticaria.
  - Antibiotic Prophylaxis Prior to Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Antibiotic regimens): "cephalosporins should not be used in an individual with a history of anaphylaxis, angioedema, or urticaria with penicillin or ampicillin"
