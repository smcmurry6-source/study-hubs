### R01

**Patient:** Male, 65 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "A lower tooth broke and is catching my tongue." (1 week)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Received 60 Gy of head and neck radiation 6 years ago
- No known jaw metastasis
- Former smoker; no medication allergies
**Current Findings:**
- Tooth 19 is nonrestorable in the irradiated field
- No exposed bone, fistula, or infection
- BP 132/80 mm Hg

**Stem:** Which intervention is NOT routinely recommended before extraction in an irradiated mandibular site?

A. Review the site-specific radiation dose and refer complex surgery when needed
B. Avoid elective bone surgery in the irradiated field
C. Consider a restorative or endodontic alternative when appropriate
D. Give prophylactic hyperbaric oxygen to every patient before extraction

**Keyed answer:** D

**Explanation:** Extraction in irradiated bone needs site-specific planning, and restorative or endodontic care may avoid higher-risk surgery. Routine prophylactic hyperbaric oxygen is not recommended by the newer evidence; the tempting wrong answer treats it as mandatory for everyone.

**Why each option:**
- A: Site-specific dose review and referral help guide surgery in irradiated bone.
- B: Elective oral surgery in irradiated bone is avoided when possible.
- C: Nonsurgical restorative and endodontic care can be considered to avoid extraction.
- D: Correct. Newer evidence does not support routine prophylactic hyperbaric oxygen for every extraction.

**Memory cue:** Irradiated bone: review dose, avoid elective surgery, and do not assume routine HBO.

**Tags:** cc CC8; fk FK6; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase plan

**Cited facts:**
- ORN-2 [verified]: Before head and neck radiation: exam about 1 month ahead if possible; finish invasive procedures at least 14 days before radiation starts. After radiation: avoid elective oral surgery on irradiated bone; if an extraction is unavoidable, keep it conservative with antibiotic coverage and possibly hyperbaric oxygen; lifelong daily fluoride gel for xerostomia; recall every 4-8 weeks for the first 6 months; trismus exercises. (Conditions: NIDCR guide is marked ARCHIVED. Its hyperbaric-oxygen advice is older teaching (see ORN-3).)
  - Oncology Pocket Guide to Oral Health (dental provider version; marked ARCHIVED) (Pre-cancer Treatment): "Complete invasive procedures at least 14 days before head/neck radiation therapy starts"
  - Oncology Pocket Guide to Oral Health (dental provider version; marked ARCHIVED) (After Radiation Therapy): "Recall the patient for prophylaxis and home care evaluation every 4 to 8 weeks or as needed for the first 6 months after cancer treatment."
  - Oncology Pocket Guide to Oral Health (dental provider version; marked ARCHIVED) (After Radiation Therapy): "Tooth extraction, if unavoidable, should be conservative, using antibiotic coverage and possibly hyperbaric oxygen therapy."
- ORN-3 [verified]: Newer evidence: the HOPON randomized trial found no reduction in ORN with prophylactic hyperbaric oxygen for extractions/implants in mandibular sites over 50 Gy, and the 2024 ISOO-MASCC-ASCO guideline does not recommend routine prophylactic HBO (selective use may be considered); it gives only a weak recommendation for perioperative pentoxifylline (with tocopherol) in selected high-risk patients. Risk rises with dose to the bone (implant failure strongly predicted above ~50 Gy at the site; same review). For exam keys: "HBO before extraction in irradiated bone" is the older NIDCR teaching; avoid making it the single right answer. (Conditions: Secondary source: the 2024 ISOO-MASCC-ASCO guideline itself was not opened. HOPON and pentoxifylline details are from the same review (quotes not yet added).)
  - Implants after head and neck radiotherapy (review; summarizes the 2024 ISOO-MASCC-ASCO ORN guideline) (Discussion): "the 2024 ISOO–MASCC–ASCO guideline does not recommend routine prophylactic HBO before invasive dental procedures after head and neck RT, although selective use may be considered in high-risk situations"
- ORN-5 [verified]: 2025 international consensus definition (69 experts; aligned with the 2024 ISOO-MASCC-ASCO guideline): ORN of the jaw is bone death from loss of blood flow, caused by ionizing radiation, occurring some time after radiation with no active cancer at the site. Findings may be clinical (exposed bone) and/or radiographic (sclerosis, pathologic fracture). ORN can be diagnosed with intact mucosa if imaging shows bone death; a minimum duration of exposed bone is not required; and not all exposed bone in an irradiated patient is necrotic. (Conditions: Consensus definition for reporting and research (Delphi study), newer than most textbooks; textbooks still use the time-based definition (ORN-4).)

  - International Expert-Based Consensus Definition, Classification Criteria, and Minimum Data Elements for Osteoradionecrosis of the Jaw: An Interdisciplinary Modified Delphi Study (Results): "recent International Society of Oral Oncology-Multinational Association for Supportive Care in Cancer and the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ISOO-MASCC-ASCO) guideline recommendations"

Findings of bone death may be clinical (i.e., exposed bone) and/or radiographic (i. e., sclerosis, pathologic fracture)."
  - International Expert-Based Consensus Definition, Classification Criteria, and Minimum Data Elements for Osteoradionecrosis of the Jaw: An Interdisciplinary Modified Delphi Study (Consensus-based definition): "It is caused by exposure to ionizing radiation and may occur at some point in time after radiation and in the absence of active disease (i.e., cancer) in the site of bone death."
  - International Expert-Based Consensus Definition, Classification Criteria, and Minimum Data Elements for Osteoradionecrosis of the Jaw: An Interdisciplinary Modified Delphi Study (CS 2): "the time or duration of exposed bone is not a mandatory diagnostic feature for ORNJ"
  - International Expert-Based Consensus Definition, Classification Criteria, and Minimum Data Elements for Osteoradionecrosis of the Jaw: An Interdisciplinary Modified Delphi Study (CS 3): "Not all cases of exposed bone are necessarily considered to be exposed necrotic bone."
  - International Expert-Based Consensus Definition, Classification Criteria, and Minimum Data Elements for Osteoradionecrosis of the Jaw: An Interdisciplinary Modified Delphi Study (CS 4): "ORNJ can be diagnosed in a patient treated with RT presenting with intact mucosa (ie, no clinical bone exposure) if there is supporting radiographic evidence of bone death/necrosis."

Orthograde root canal treatment has no contraindications and is sometimes done on an unrestorable tooth to avoid a high-risk extraction in the irradiated field. ### R02

**Patient:** Male, 60 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "I have pressure in my chest that is not easing." (15 minutes)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Stable angina treated with sublingual nitroglycerin
- No recent change in usual symptoms
- No PDE-5 inhibitor use; retired bus driver
**Current Findings:**
- Chest pressure began during the dental visit
- Three nitroglycerin tablets were taken 5 minutes apart
- Pain persists; BP 138/82 mm Hg

**Stem:** What should the team do now under the nitroglycerin guidance?

A. Take a fourth tablet before seeking help
B. Wait until the end of the appointment to reassess
C. Double the next nitroglycerin dose
D. Arrange prompt medical attention

**Keyed answer:** D

**Explanation:** Persistent pain after three nitroglycerin tablets in 15 minutes requires prompt medical attention. The tempting wrong answer is another tablet, which exceeds the stated maximum.

**Why each option:**
- A: The guidance limits dosing to three tablets in 15 minutes; ongoing pain needs prompt attention.
- B: Waiting delays evaluation of persistent chest pain.
- C: Doubling a dose is not the listed nitroglycerin regimen.
- D: Correct. Persistent pain after three tablets in 15 minutes requires prompt medical attention.

**Memory cue:** Three tablets in 15 minutes without relief: seek prompt medical attention.

**Tags:** cc CC1; fk FK8; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase tx

**Cited facts:**
- EM-2 [verified]: Nitroglycerin sublingual tablets (0.3, 0.4 or 0.6 mg) for an anginal attack: let the tablet dissolve without swallowing; one more tablet may be given every 5 minutes until relief, but no more than 3 tablets in 15 minutes; pain that persists after 3 tablets in 15 minutes (or is different from usual) needs prompt medical attention. Contraindicated with PDE-5 inhibitors (avanafil, sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil) or sGC stimulators, severe anemia and increased intracranial pressure.
  - Nitrostat (nitroglycerin sublingual tablets), Viatris (2 Dosage and Administration): "One additional tablet may be administered every 5 minutes until relief is obtained. No more than three tablets are recommended within a 15-minute period. If the pain persists after a total of 3 tablets in a 15-minute period"
  - Nitrostat (nitroglycerin sublingual tablets), Viatris (3 Dosage Forms): "Sublingual tablets, 0.3 mg; 0.4 mg; 0.6 mg"
  - Nitrostat (nitroglycerin sublingual tablets), Viatris (4 Contraindications): "Use of phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE-5) inhibitors, such as avanafil, sildenafil, tadalafil, or vardenafil, or soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC) stimulators."
- EM-10 [verified]: In a heart attack, aspirin is given to keep more clots from forming (it can cause stomach bleeding in some people), and nitroglycerin improves blood flow to the heart. (Conditions: NHLBI patient page; the aspirin dose for a suspected heart attack is not in it.)
  - Heart Attack: Treatment (Emergency treatment): "Aspirin or other medicines can prevent more blood clots from forming. In some people, aspirin may cause bleeding in the stomach."

**Stem:** What is the most time-sensitive next step after this occupational exposure?

A. Start post-exposure prophylaxis promptly, within 72 hours
B. Wait for symptoms before seeking care
C. Begin prophylaxis only if the source patient develops symptoms
D. Use antibiotics for several days instead of reporting the exposure

**Keyed answer:** A

**Why each option:**

**Tags:** cc CC1; fk FK5; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase plan

**Cited facts:**

### R04

**Patient:** Female, 72 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "A broken molar needs to come out." (3 weeks)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Osteoporosis treated with denosumab every 6 months for 4 years
- Last injection was 2 months ago
- No prior jaw necrosis; retired bank teller
**Current Findings:**
- Tooth 30 is nonrestorable without acute swelling
- BP 124/76 mm Hg
- Extraction is being planned with her physician

**Stem:** Which counseling best reflects the evidence about a denosumab holiday?

A. Hold the next two injections after extraction to prevent rebound bone loss
B. Stop denosumab for a year before extraction
C. Coordinate timing with the prescriber; do not stop it casually
D. Use CTX testing to decide when extraction is safe

**Keyed answer:** C

**Explanation:** Drug holidays remain controversial, and stopping denosumab can cause rebound bone loss and vertebral fractures. If it is held, the cited timing is surgery 3–4 months after the last dose and restarting 6–8 weeks after surgery; the tempting wrong answer is a long interruption.

**Why each option:**
- A: Holding two injections risks a prolonged interruption and rebound bone loss.
- B: A year-long interruption is not the cited timing and can increase rebound fracture risk.
- C: Correct. Coordinate the decision; if held, use the stated interval and resume after healing.
- D: CTX is not validated for making the timing decision.

**Memory cue:** Denosumab holiday: controversial; avoid a long stop because of rebound fracture risk.

**Tags:** cc CC8; fk FK6; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase plan

**Cited facts:**
- MR-3 [verified]: Risk by indication: much higher in cancer (<5%) than in osteoporosis (<0.05%). Cancer: zoledronate risk is 2-10 times placebo; ranges about 1.6-4% after 2 years and 3.8-18% beyond 2 years of treatment (Ng et al. review); denosumab 0-6.9% (most studies <5%), 1.9% under 24 months and 6.9% over 24 months of exposure, comparable to zoledronate. Osteoporosis: IV zoledronate <=0.02% (<=2 per 10,000); oral bisphosphonates <=0.05% (<=5 per 10,000); denosumab 0.3% after 10 years (almost an order of magnitude higher than bisphosphonates); romosozumab 0.03-0.05%. Placebo groups in osteoporosis trials: 0-0.02%. (Conditions: The per-drug percentages come from the paper's risk tables (pp. 6-8), which PDF extraction scrambles; key an item on the cancer (<5%) vs osteoporosis (<0.05%) contrast, not on a single table cell.)
  - AAOMS Position Paper on Medication-Related Osteonecrosis of the Jaws, 2022 Update (Risk, pp. 6-8): "The risk of MRONJ is considerably higher in the malignancy group (<5%) than in the osteoporosis group (<0.05%)."
- MR-5 [verified]: Drug holidays: still controversial; the 2022 working group could not reach consensus (split evenly between case-by-case holidays using prior recommendations and never offering them). Factors: cancer vs osteoporosis, dosing frequency, duration, comorbidities, other drugs (chemotherapy, steroids, antiangiogenics), infection, extent of surgery. Denosumab in osteoporosis: stopping risks rebound bone loss and multiple vertebral fractures; if held, do the surgery 3-4 months after the last dose and restart 6-8 weeks after surgery. Bone turnover markers (CTX) are not validated for decisions. The older "4 years of oral bisphosphonate" holiday trigger is from the 2014 paper and is not a 2022 recommendation.
  - AAOMS Position Paper on Medication-Related Osteonecrosis of the Jaws, 2022 Update (Drug holidays, p. 11): "the working group was unable to reach a consensus regarding a recommendation on drug holidays"
  - AAOMS Position Paper on Medication-Related Osteonecrosis of the Jaws, 2022 Update (Drug holidays, p. 11): "The planned dentoalveolar surgery can be completed 3-4 months following the last dose of DMB when the level of osteoclast inhibition is waning. It can then be reinstituted 6-8 weeks postsurgery."
- MR-6 [verified]: Prevention: before antiresorptives for cancer, a full dental exam with radiographs; extract nonrestorable or poor-prognosis teeth and finish needed surgery first; delay the drug, if the systemic condition allows, until sites mucosalize or bone heals. For osteoporosis, dental optimization can run concurrently with therapy. During cancer-dose therapy: avoid dentoalveolar surgery if possible, consider keeping roots (root retention) instead of extracting, implants are contraindicated. Modifiable risk reducers: surgery before therapy, pre/postoperative antibiotics and antimicrobial rinses, primary closure, good oral hygiene.
  - AAOMS Position Paper on Medication-Related Osteonecrosis of the Jaws, 2022 Update (Prevention, p. 11): "including performing high-risk surgical procedures prior to initiating therapy"
  - AAOMS Position Paper on Medication-Related Osteonecrosis of the Jaws, 2022 Update (Table 2, p. 18): "Consider root retention techniques to avoid extractions. • Dental implants are contraindicated."

### R05

**Patient:** Female, 49 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "I need an antibiotic for this tooth infection." (2 days)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Kidney transplant 2 years ago
- Takes tacrolimus to prevent rejection
- No medication allergies; works as a paralegal
**Current Findings:**
- Tooth 30 has localized swelling and tenderness

- An antimicrobial is being considered

**Stem:** What effect can clarithromycin have when taken with tacrolimus?

A. It prevents tacrolimus from affecting kidney function
B. It has no relevant interaction with tacrolimus
C. It can sharply raise tacrolimus levels and toxicity risk
D. It reliably lowers tacrolimus levels and prevents toxicity

**Keyed answer:** C

**Explanation:** Clarithromycin inhibits CYP3A4 and can sharply increase tacrolimus concentrations, raising nephrotoxicity and other toxicity risks. The tempting wrong answer predicts a lower tacrolimus level.

**Why each option:**
- A: A higher tacrolimus concentration can increase nephrotoxicity rather than prevent it.
- B: The interaction is clinically important and needs prescriber coordination.
- C: Correct. Clarithromycin can raise tacrolimus levels and increase toxicity risk.
- D: The cited interaction is an increase in levels, not a decrease.

**Memory cue:** Clarithromycin can raise tacrolimus; coordinate antimicrobial choices and monitoring.

**Tags:** cc CC8; fk FK6; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase plan

**Cited facts:**

A rapid, sharp rise in tacrolimus has been reported with clarithromycin even after the tacrolimus dose was reduced. Higher levels raise the risk of nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity and QT prolongation. Cyclosporine levels are raised by fluconazole, itraconazole, ketoconazole, azithromycin, clarithromycin and erythromycin. The labels call for dose adjustment and close monitoring of drug levels when these are combined; avoid grapefruit juice with tacrolimus. ### R06

A. Stage 1, exposed bone without symptoms or infection
B. At risk, without clinical evidence of necrotic bone
C. Stage 0, with no exposed bone
D. Stage 2, exposed bone with infection or inflammation

**Keyed answer:** A

**Explanation:** She has antiresorptive exposure, exposed bone for more than eight weeks, and no jaw radiation or metastasis, meeting the MRONJ case definition. With exposed bone but no symptoms or infection, this is stage 1; the tempting wrong answer is stage 2.

**Why each option:**
