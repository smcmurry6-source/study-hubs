### R01

**Stem:** Each of the following statements matches the AHA dental prophylaxis recommendations for an adult with a prosthetic valve EXCEPT one. Which is the EXCEPTION?

A. The oral regimen is given 30 to 60 minutes before treatment
B. Clindamycin is still recommended for penicillin allergy
C. Amoxicillin is an oral option given as a single preprocedure dose
D. Cephalosporins are avoided after penicillin-associated hives

**Keyed answer:** B

**Explanation:** AHA recommendations use a single preprocedure dose, and cephalosporins are avoided after urticaria, angioedema, or anaphylaxis. The tempting wrong answer is clindamycin, which is no longer recommended for dental prophylaxis.

**Why each option:**
- A: The AHA timing is 30 to 60 minutes before the procedure.
- B: Correct. Clindamycin was removed because it causes more frequent and severe reactions.
- C: This matches the usual oral regimen when prophylaxis is indicated.
- D: Urticaria is one of the reactions that rules out a cephalosporin.

**Memory cue:** Clindamycin is no longer an AHA dental prophylaxis option.

**Tags:** cc CC1; fk FK8; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase clear

**Cited facts:**
- IE-5 [verified]: Regimen: a single dose 30 to 60 minutes before the procedure. Oral: amoxicillin 2 g (child 50 mg/kg). Cannot take oral: ampicillin 2 g IM or IV (child 50 mg/kg), or cefazolin or ceftriaxone 1 g IM or IV (child 50 mg/kg). Penicillin or ampicillin allergy, oral: cephalexin 2 g (child 50 mg/kg) or another first- or second-generation oral cephalosporin in an equivalent dose; azithromycin or clarithromycin 500 mg (child 15 mg/kg); or doxycycline 100 mg (child under 45 kg: 2.2 mg/kg; 45 kg or more: 100 mg). Allergy and cannot take oral: cefazolin or ceftriaxone 1 g IM or IV (child 50 mg/kg). (Conditions: The doses in the table (2 g / 50 mg/kg, etc.) rest on AHA Table 5, which the quote checker cannot open (ahajournals blocks automated access); the ADA quotes confirm only the drug choices.)
  - Antibiotic Prophylaxis Prior to Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Antibiotic regimens): "cephalexin (or other first- or second-generation cephalosporins), azithromycin, clarithromycin, or doxycycline be used as alternatives"
  - Antibiotic Prophylaxis Prior to Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Antibiotic regimens): "the AHA recommends cefazolin or ceftriaxone as alternatives"
- IE-6 [verified]: Clindamycin is no longer recommended (oral or parenteral) for dental prophylaxis: it causes more frequent and severe reactions, including fatal C. difficile infection after a single dose; up to 15% of community-acquired C. difficile may trace to antibiotics prescribed for dental procedures.
  - Antibiotic Prophylaxis Prior to Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Additional Considerations): "no longer recommends use of clindamycin as an oral or parenteral alternative to amoxicillin or ampicillin in individuals with allergies to these drugs"
- IE-7 [verified]: Do not use a cephalosporin in someone whose penicillin/ampicillin reaction was anaphylaxis, angioedema or urticaria.
  - Antibiotic Prophylaxis Prior to Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Antibiotic regimens): "cephalosporins should not be used in an individual with a history of anaphylaxis, angioedema, or urticaria with penicillin or ampicillin"

### R02

**Stem:** Which plan for apixaban best fits this single simple extraction?

A. Replace apixaban with aspirin for one week
B. Check an INR on the morning of extraction
C. Continue the prescribed apixaban regimen
D. Stop apixaban for a week and bridge with heparin

**Keyed answer:** C

**Explanation:** Most dental procedures, including a simple extraction, do not require a DOAC change. The tempting wrong answer is an INR check, which is not used to monitor apixaban.

**Why each option:**
- A: Changing to another antithrombotic is not a dental-office substitute for the prescribed regimen.
- B: Apixaban is not monitored with an INR.
- C: Correct. Continue apixaban for most dental procedures.
- D: A week-long interruption and bridging are not the routine plan for a simple extraction.

**Memory cue:** For most dental procedures, continue the prescribed DOAC.

**Tags:** cc CC8; fk FK8; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase plan

**Cited facts:**
- AC-3 [verified]: DOACs (dabigatran = direct thrombin inhibitor; apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban = factor Xa inhibitors): fixed doses, shorter half-lives, little or no monitoring (no INR), fewer interactions. For most dental procedures, no change to the regimen; with higher bleeding risk, and only with the physician: delay the daily dose until after the procedure, schedule as late as possible after the last dose, or interrupt 24-48 hours. The European Heart Rhythm Association lists extraction of 1-3 teeth, periodontal surgery, abscess incision and implant placement as not necessarily requiring DOAC interruption.
  - Oral Anticoagulant and Antiplatelet Medications and Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Newer anticoagulants): "consideration may be given, in consultation with and on advice of the patient's physician, to postponing the timing of the daily dose"
  - Oral Anticoagulant and Antiplatelet Medications and Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Conclusions): "temporarily interrupting drug therapy for 24 to 48 hours"
  - Oral Anticoagulant and Antiplatelet Medications and Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Newer anticoagulants): "interventions not necessarily requiring discontinuation of the newer anticoagulants include extraction of 1 to 3 teeth"

### R03

**Stem:** What should the dental team do with clopidogrel before this extraction?

A. Stop it for five days without contacting the cardiologist
B. Continue it; consult cardiology before any change
C. Replace it with aspirin until the socket heals
D. Skip the dose on the day of extraction and restart next week

**Keyed answer:** B

**Explanation:** Antiplatelet therapy is usually continued for dental procedures, and a recent stent makes coordination with the cardiologist important. The tempting wrong answer is a short drug holiday, which still changes dual therapy without the cardiologist.

**Why each option:**
- A: Unilateral interruption changes the prescribed regimen without cardiology input.
- B: Correct. Continue antiplatelet therapy; discuss any change with the cardiologist.
- C: Substituting aspirin is not a dentist-directed replacement for the prescribed regimen.
- D: Skipping a dose is still an uncoordinated interruption of therapy.

**Memory cue:** After a coronary stent, do not change antiplatelet therapy without the cardiologist.

**Tags:** cc CC8; fk FK8; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase plan

**Cited facts:**
- AC-4 [verified]: Antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticagrelor, ticlopidine), single or dual: do not stop for dental procedures; bleeding rates after minor oral surgery were clinically similar with dual, single or no antiplatelet therapy and were controlled locally. Patients with coronary stents on dual antiplatelet therapy: talk to the cardiologist before any change; premature stopping risks stent thrombosis, MI and death.
  - Oral Anticoagulant and Antiplatelet Medications and Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Antiplatelets): "found clinically similar rates of bleeding across the three groups"
  - Oral Anticoagulant and Antiplatelet Medications and Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Antiplatelets): "should contact the patient's cardiologist regarding the patient's antiplatelet regimen and discuss optimal patient management, before discontinuing the antiplatelet medications"

### R05

**Stem:** Which local approach is appropriate while her antithrombotic medicines are continued?

A. Use systemic vitamin K to reverse both medicines
B. Avoid packing or suturing so bleeding can drain
C. Skip local measures because antithrombotic therapy is continued
D. Use pressure and local hemostatic measures

**Keyed answer:** D

**Explanation:** Pressure, absorbable hemostatic material, and sutures are local options for controlling dental bleeding. The tempting wrong answer skips local bleeding control just because medicines are continued.

**Why each option:**
- A: Vitamin K is not the local hemostasis approach described for these antithrombotic medicines.
- B: Pressure and local hemostatic methods are useful; leaving the socket unmanaged is not the plan.
- C: Antithrombotic medicines do not replace local hemostatic measures.
- D: Correct. Use pressure, absorbable hemostatic material, sutures, or tranexamic acid as appropriate.

**Memory cue:** Keep prescribed antithrombotics and control bleeding locally.

**Tags:** cc CC8; fk FK6; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase tx

**Cited facts:**
- AC-5 [verified]: Local measures: pressure, Gelfoam or Surgicel, sutures, and antifibrinolytics such as tranexamic acid. The risks of stopping (thromboembolism, stroke, MI) generally outweigh prolonged bleeding.
  - Oral Anticoagulant and Antiplatelet Medications and Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Conclusions): "which can be controlled with local measures such as mechanical pressure, hemostatic agents (e.g., Gelfoam® or Surgicel®), suturing, and/or antifibrinolytics"
  - Oral Anticoagulant and Antiplatelet Medications and Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Background): "treatment regimens with these older anticoagulants/antiplatelet agents should not be altered before dental procedures"

### R06

**Patient:** Male, 70 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "I am here for a cleaning." (routine visit)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Coronary stent placed 2 years ago
- Dual-chamber pacemaker for bradycardia
- Takes aspirin and atorvastatin
- No history of endocarditis; retired accountant
**Current Findings:**
- BP 126/74 mm Hg, pulse 70/min
- No fever or oral infection
- Routine prophylaxis and cleaning are planned

**Stem:** Which listed cardiac history meets an AHA high-risk category for infective endocarditis prophylaxis?

A. Aspirin use
B. Coronary stent
C. Neither
D. Pacemaker

**Keyed answer:** C

**Explanation:** A coronary stent and pacemaker are not among the four AHA high-risk categories. The tempting wrong answer is the stent, which can be confused with a prosthetic cardiac valve.

**Why each option:**
- A: Aspirin use does not establish a qualifying cardiac condition.
- B: A coronary stent does not qualify for IE prophylaxis by itself.
- C: Correct. Neither the stent nor the pacemaker is an indication by itself.
- D: An implanted electronic device is not an AHA high-risk category.

**Memory cue:** A stent and pacemaker are not prosthetic cardiac valves.

**Tags:** cc CC8; fk FK8; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase clear

**Cited facts:**
- IE-1 [verified]: The 2021 statement made no changes to the 2007 AHA recommendations; prophylaxis is still limited to the 4 categories at highest risk of a bad outcome from viridans streptococcal IE.
  - Antibiotic Prophylaxis Prior to Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Background): "These guidelines were updated by a 2021 scientific statement by the AHA that recommended no changes to the 2007 guideline recommendations."
- IE-2 [verified]: Conditions for which prophylaxis is suggested: (a) prosthetic cardiac valve or prosthetic material: a prosthetic valve, transcatheter-implanted prosthetic valves, valve repair with devices (annuloplasty rings, clips), and LVADs or implantable hearts; (b) previous, relapsed or recurrent IE; (c) congenital heart disease: unrepaired cyanotic CHD (including palliative shunts and conduits); a CHD defect completely repaired with prosthetic material or a device (surgical or transcatheter) during the first 6 months after the procedure; repaired CHD with residual defects at or next to a prosthetic patch or device; surgical or transcatheter pulmonary artery valve or conduit (e.g., Melody valve, Contegra conduit); (d) cardiac transplant recipients who develop valvulopathy.
  - Antibiotic Prophylaxis Prior to Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Key Points / patient selection list): "prosthetic cardiac valves, including transcatheter-implanted prostheses and homografts; prosthetic material used for cardiac valve repair, such as annuloplasty rings and chords; a history of infective endocarditis"
  - Antibiotic Prophylaxis Prior to Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (patient selection list): "with valve regurgitation due to a structurally abnormal valve"
  - Antibiotic Prophylaxis Prior to Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (patient selection list): "completely repaired with prosthetic material or a device for the first six months after the repair procedure"
- IE-3 [verified]: Prophylaxis is not suggested for: pacemakers and similar implantable electronic devices, septal closure devices once closure is complete, peripheral vascular grafts and patches (including hemodialysis grafts), coronary or other vascular stents, CNS ventriculoatrial shunts, vena cava filters, pledgets. Rheumatic heart disease, bicuspid aortic valve, mitral valve prolapse and other native valve disease were reviewed and not added.
  - Antibiotic Prophylaxis Prior to Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (patient selection list, footnote b): "Except for the conditions listed above, antibiotic prophylaxis is no longer recommended for any other form of congenital heart disease."

### R07

**Stem:** Under the 2017 ACC/AHA adult categories, how is his repeated pressure of 172/98 mm Hg classified?

A. Stage 2 hypertension
B. Hypertensive crisis
C. Elevated blood pressure
D. Stage 1 hypertension

**Keyed answer:** A

**Explanation:** The 2017 ACC/AHA categories use the more severe of the systolic and diastolic values; 172 systolic is in stage 2. The tempting wrong answer is stage 1, which fits a lower systolic or diastolic range.

**Why each option:**
- A: Correct. A systolic pressure of at least 140 or a diastolic pressure of at least 90 is stage 2.
- B: Crisis is above 180 systolic and/or above 120 diastolic.
- C: Elevated pressure requires systolic 120–129 with diastolic below 80.
- D: Stage 1 is 130–139 systolic or 80–89 diastolic; his systolic value is higher.

**Memory cue:** Use the more severe component; 172 systolic is stage 2.

**Tags:** cc CC8; fk FK6; cognitive level 1; difficulty 1; phase plan

**Cited facts:**
- BP-1 [verified]: Adult BP categories: normal <120 and <80; elevated 120-129 and <80; stage 1 hypertension 130-139 or 80-89; stage 2 >=140 or >=90; hypertensive crisis >180 and/or >120.
  - Hypertension (Oral Health Topics) (Table 1 (2017 ACC/AHA categories)): "Elevated 120-129 and <80 Hypertension Stage 1 130-139 or 80-89 Stage 2 > 140 or > 90 Hypertensive Crisis >180 and/or >120"
  - Hypertension (Oral Health Topics) (Diagnosis): "hypertension is defined as a systolic pressure of 130 mmHg or greater or a diastolic blood pressure of 80 mmHg or greater"

### R08

**Stem:** For confirmed pressure before a routine cleaning, what does ADA recommend?

A. Proceed with checks every 10 to 15 minutes
B. Proceed because he has no oral pain
C. Defer elective care and refer him to his physician
D. Use the 180/110 mm Hg major-surgery threshold for dental care

**Keyed answer:** C

**Explanation:** His repeat systolic value remains above 160 mm Hg, and a cleaning is elective. The tempting wrong answer borrows the monitoring plan for pain-driven emergency care.

**Why each option:**
- A: Checks every 10 to 15 minutes belong to selected pain-driven emergency treatment.
- B: Absence of pain does not remove the ADA guide’s threshold for confirmed high pressure before elective care.
- C: Correct. The ADA guide says to defer elective care and refer when a reading above 160/100 is confirmed.
- D: The 180/110 threshold is described for possible deferral of elective major surgery, not the ADA dental pathway.

**Memory cue:** Confirmed above 160/100 before elective dental care: refer.

**Tags:** cc CC8; fk FK6; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase plan

**Cited facts:**
- BP-3 [verified]: ADA Practical Guide thresholds for adult outpatient dental care: under 160/100: no modification for elective or emergency care. Over 160/100: repeat the reading; if it comes down, or is within a physician's written guidance, proceed; if confirmed, no elective care and refer to the physician. Emergency care at a confirmed 160-180 systolic and/or 100-109 diastolic when pain is driving the BP: treat, check BP every 10-15 minutes, use anxiety reduction. Confirmed >180 systolic and/or >109 diastolic: consult the physician before proceeding. Systolic >180 and/or diastolic >100: refer as soon as possible, or urgent medical evaluation if symptomatic. (Conditions: The ADA table itself is inconsistent: its body uses >109 diastolic for "consult before proceeding", its footnote >100 for "refer as soon as possible". Key items on the body thresholds (160/100 and 180/109) and avoid diastolic values of 101-109 where the two disagree.)
  - Hypertension (Oral Health Topics) (Table 3 (ADA Practical Guide)): "<160/100 No modification No modification >160/100* Repeat measurement If lowered or within written guidance from a physician, proceed If confirmed, no elective dental treatment"
  - Hypertension (Oral Health Topics) (Table 3): "initiate emergency care with blood pressure monitoring every 10 to 15 minutes during procedure; consider anxiety reduction techniques If confirmed systolic pressure >180 mmHg and/or diastolic pressure >109 mmHg, seek consultation with a physician before proceeding"
  - Hypertension (Oral Health Topics) (Table 3 footnote): "Patients with systolic pressure >180 mmHg and/or diastolic pressure >100 mmHg should be referred to their physician as soon as possible or sent for urgent medical evaluation, if symptomatic."
- BP-4 [verified]: Separately, the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline says deferring elective major surgery may be considered at >=180 systolic or >=110 diastolic. Many board review sources use "180/110 = defer elective dental care"; when a stem uses that number, frame it as the ACC/AHA surgical threshold, and key ADA's 160/100 only when the stem names the ADA guide. Yarows et al. (JADA 2020) note there is no evidence-based BP cutoff for deferring dental care. (Conditions: Yarows et al. (no evidence-based cutoff) is cited on the same page; quote not yet added.)
  - Hypertension (Oral Health Topics) (Dental treatment considerations): "deferring surgery may be considered in persons with hypertension and planned elective major surgery who have a systolic pressure of 180 mm Hg or higher or diastolic pressure of 110 mm Hg or higher"

  - Antibiotic Prophylaxis Prior to Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (patient selection list): "completely repaired with prosthetic material or a device for the first six months after the repair procedure"

### R10

**Stem:** Apart from his separate cardiac history, what does the knee prosthesis itself require before dental care?

A. No routine prophylaxis for the prosthetic joint alone
B. Automatic antibiotics if a crown is present
C. Give prophylaxis before routine dental care solely because a joint was replaced
D. A one-year delay after joint replacement

**Keyed answer:** A

**Explanation:** A prosthetic joint alone is not a reason for routine dental antibiotic prophylaxis. The tempting wrong answer applies the cardiac-valve rule to a knee replacement.

**Why each option:**
- A: Correct. Prior joint-surgery complications warrant consultation; without them there is no routine joint-based prophylaxis.
- B: A crown does not change the joint-prophylaxis recommendation.
- C: Routine antibiotics are not recommended for a prosthetic joint alone.
- D: Routine delays after joint replacement are not supported by the cited ADA guidance.

**Memory cue:** A prosthetic joint and a prosthetic cardiac valve have different prophylaxis rules.

**Tags:** cc CC8; fk FK6; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase plan

**Cited facts:**
- PJ-1 [verified]: ADA 2015 clinical practice guideline (Sollecito et al., JADA 2015, from a 2014 systematic review): in general, prophylactic antibiotics are not recommended before dental procedures to prevent prosthetic joint infection; the review found no association between dental procedures and prosthetic joint infection.
  - Antibiotic Prophylaxis Prior to Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Prevention of Prosthetic Joint Infection): "The updated systematic review undertaken in 2014 and published in 2015 found no association between dental procedures and prosthetic joint infections."
  - Antibiotic Prophylaxis Prior to Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Prevention of Prosthetic Joint Infection): "not recommended prior to dental procedures to prevent prosthetic joint infection"
- PJ-2 [verified]: For a patient with a history of complications from the joint replacement surgery who needs procedures with gingival manipulation or mucosal incision, prophylaxis is only considered after consulting the patient and the orthopedic surgeon; if it is needed, the orthopedic surgeon should recommend the regimen and, when reasonable, write the prescription.
  - Antibiotic Prophylaxis Prior to Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Key Points (ADA Chairside Guide)): "for patients with a history of complications associated with their joint replacement surgery who are undergoing dental procedures that include gingival manipulation or mucosal incision, prophylactic antibiotics should only be considered after consultation with the patient and orthopedic surgeon"
  - Antibiotic Prophylaxis Prior to Dental Procedures (Oral Health Topics) (Key Points (ADA Chairside Guide)): "it is most appropriate that the orthopedic surgeon recommend the appropriate antibiotic regimen and, when reasonable, write the prescription"
