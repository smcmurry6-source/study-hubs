# Batch P1

### R01

**Patient:** Female, 29 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "My tooth has been throbbing for three days and kept me up all night." (3 days)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Pregnant, 10 weeks (first pregnancy)
- Prenatal vitamin daily
- No known drug allergies
- Elementary school teacher; never smoked
**Current Findings:**
- BP 112/70, pulse 84
- Tooth 19: deep distal caries; pain to cold that lingers about 40 seconds; tender to percussion
- Periapical radiograph: caries approaching the pulp; widened PDL space at the mesial root apex

**Stem:** Root canal treatment of tooth 19 is indicated. When is the most appropriate time to do it?

A. In the second trimester, about 4 weeks from now
B. Now, during the first trimester
C. Now, but without radiographs or local anesthesia
D. Once her obstetrician has given written clearance

**Keyed answer:** B

**Explanation:** Symptomatic irreversible pulpitis is a dental emergency. The ADA and ACOG agree that emergency care such as root canal treatment can be done safely during pregnancy, that radiographs and local anesthesia are safe at any point, and that delaying treatment can make problems worse. The tempting wrong answer is waiting for the second trimester, a convention for elective care that does not apply to an emergency.

**Why each option:**
- A: Waiting is not needed for emergency care; "second trimester" is a convention for elective treatment.
- B: Correct. Emergency treatment is safe in any trimester; radiographs and local anesthesia are safe at any point, and delay risks a more complex problem.
- C: Radiographs and local anesthesia are safe at any point in pregnancy; root canal treatment needs both.
- D: Consulting the obstetrician is advised for medicines beyond the ones the ADA lists as safe, but emergency care does not wait for written clearance.

**Memory cue:** Pregnant with a toothache: treat now. The second-trimester habit is for elective care.

**Tags:** cc CC1 (+CC20); fk FK6; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase plan

**Cited facts:**
- PG-1 [verified]: Timing: preventive, diagnostic and restorative care is safe throughout pregnancy; radiographs and local anesthesia are safe at any point; ADA and ACOG agree that emergency care (extractions, root canals, restorations) can be done during pregnancy and that delay can make problems worse. The traditional "second trimester is the most comfortable time for elective care" is a teaching convention, not in the opened ADA page; don't key a question that says care must wait for it. (Conditions: 'Second trimester is the most comfortable time for elective care' is a teaching convention not in the ADA page: never key an item that says care must wait for it.)
  - Pregnancy (Oral Health Topics) (Key Points): "Preventive, diagnostic and restorative dental treatment is safe throughout pregnancy."
  - Pregnancy (Oral Health Topics) (Introduction): "Oral health care, including having dental radiographs taken and being given local anesthesia, is safe at any point during pregnancy."
  - Pregnancy (Oral Health Topics) (Dental Treatment): "agree that emergency treatments, such as extractions, root canals or restorations can be safely performed during pregnancy and that delaying treatment may result in more complex problems"
- PG-4 [verified]: Safe choices named by ADA: local anesthetics with or without epinephrine (e.g., lidocaine, mepivacaine, bupivacaine); antibiotics penicillin, amoxicillin, cephalosporins, clindamycin, metronidazole. For pain relievers, consult the obstetrician: FDA said in 2015 that research was too limited to make recommendations.

  - Pregnancy (Oral Health Topics) (Medication Selection): "published research is "too limited to make any recommendations" on pain reliever use in this population"
- EN-4 [verified]: Symptomatic irreversible pulpitis: vital, inflamed pulp that cannot heal; descriptors are lingering thermal pain, spontaneous pain and referred pain. BDJ 2025 operationalizes "lingering" as thermal pain lasting more than 30 seconds; EPT usually still positive; percussion pain in some cases.
  - Diagnosis consensus among endodontic specialists and general practitioners: an international survey and a proposed modification (Table 3): "Symptomatic irreversible pulpitis A clinical diagnosis based on subjective (lingering pain to thermal changes for extended period)"

### R02

**Patient:** Male, 15 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "My back tooth really hurts and my cheek is swollen." (2 days)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Acute lymphoblastic leukemia, receiving chemotherapy
- Indwelling central venous catheter
- Ondansetron 4 mg as needed for nausea
- No known drug allergies
**Current Findings:**
- Temperature 37.0 °C (98.6 °F); pulse 88
- Tooth 30: large carious lesion into the pulp, nonrestorable; tender to percussion
- Fluctuant buccal swelling next to tooth 30; periapical radiolucency at both roots
- Blood counts drawn this morning: platelets 55,000/mm3; absolute neutrophil count 1,600/mm3

**Stem:** Following the AAPD best practices for patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy, which is the most appropriate plan for tooth 30?

A. Extract today after a single dose of amoxicillin; his neutrophils are below 2,000/mm3
B. Coordinate with his physician and extract in a hospital setting with planned supportive measures
C. Extract today in the office with local hemostatic measures; his platelets are above 50,000/mm3
D. Defer all dental treatment until chemotherapy ends; elective care waits during immunosuppression

**Keyed answer:** B

**Explanation:** Below 60,000 platelets/mm3, the AAPD best practices defer elective care and avoid invasive procedures when possible; when treatment is medically necessary, it belongs in a hospital setting after the dentist and physician agree on supportive measures such as platelet transfusion. An abscessed, nonrestorable tooth with swelling is a dental emergency, so it cannot wait for chemotherapy to end. His neutrophil count (1,600/mm3) falls in the AAPD band where the dentist uses clinical judgment (1,000-2,000/mm3). The tempting wrong answer uses 50,000/mm3, a platelet floor many teaching sources give; the question names the AAPD guidance, which uses 60,000.

**Why each option:**
- A: A neutrophil count of 1,000-2,000/mm3 calls for clinical judgment under AAPD, and an antibiotic dose does nothing about his low platelets.
- B: Correct. Under 60,000/mm3, AAPD places medically necessary care in a hospital setting, after supportive measures such as platelet transfusion are planned with the physician.
- C: 50,000/mm3 is a common teaching floor, but the AAPD guidance named in the question treats counts under 60,000/mm3 as needing hospital-based care.
- D: An abscessed, swollen, nonrestorable tooth is an emergency, not elective care; AAPD has the physician help plan its treatment rather than waiting out chemotherapy.

**Memory cue:** AAPD chemo counts: platelets under 60,000 = hospital + physician; ANC 1,000-2,000 = judgment; ANC under 1,000 = defer elective care.

**Tags:** cc CC8 (+CC9); fk FK6 (+FK5); cognitive level 2; difficulty 3; phase plan
