### R01

**Patient:** Female, 49 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "I come in every three months to keep my gums in check." (recall visit)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Generalized stage III periodontitis, treated with scaling and root planing and osseous surgery (2023)
- Hypothyroidism
- Levothyroxine 75 mcg daily
- No known drug allergies
- Accountant; never smoked
**Current Findings:**
- BP 118/76, pulse 66
- Probing depths 2-4 mm at all sites
- Bleeding on probing at 6% of sites, all at 2-mm and 3-mm sites
- Bone levels unchanged from radiographs taken 2 years ago

**Stem:** Which additional finding at this visit would mean she no longer meets the definition of periodontal health for a successfully treated periodontitis patient?

A. Bleeding on probing at 8% of sites
B. Attachment loss remaining from before treatment
C. A 4-mm site that bleeds on probing
D. Several 4-mm sites that do not bleed

**Keyed answer:** C

**Explanation:** In a treated, stable periodontitis patient, health allows probing depths up to 4 mm as long as no 4-mm site bleeds and bleeding stays under 10% of sites; a 4-mm site that bleeds is no longer a "closed pocket". Attachment loss from the past never goes away, so it cannot be what changes her status. The tempting wrong answer is bleeding at 8% of sites, which is still under the 10% threshold.

**Why each option:**
- A: Still under 10% of sites, the bleeding threshold for health.
- B: Past attachment loss persists in every treated periodontitis patient; it does not decide health versus disease today.
- C: Correct. A 4-mm site with bleeding on probing is no longer a closed pocket, so the stable-health definition is not met.
- D: Probing depths of 4 mm are compatible with health in a treated periodontitis patient when they do not bleed.

**Memory cue:** Treated perio patient: 4 mm is fine if it is dry. 4 mm plus bleeding = not healthy.

**Tags:** cc CC22 (+CC37); fk FK6 (+FK5); cognitive level 2; difficulty 3; phase dx

**Cited facts:**

### R03

**Stem:** No earlier radiographs are available. Which grade should be assigned?

A. Grade A
B. Grade B
C. Grade C
D. No grade until radiographs from 5 years apart exist

**Keyed answer:** C

**Explanation:** Without earlier radiographs, start from grade B and use indirect evidence: about 50% bone loss at age 58 gives a ratio of 0.86, which alone stays in grade B. Then apply the modifiers: 10 or more cigarettes a day and an HbA1c of 7.0% or higher each move the grade to C. The tempting wrong answer is grade B, reached by stopping at the bone-loss-to-age ratio.

**Why each option:**
- A: Grade A needs no loss over 5 years or a bone-loss-to-age ratio under 0.25; he has a ratio of 0.86 and two grade C modifiers.
- B: A ratio of 0.86 is in the grade B range (0.25-1.0), but smoking 15 cigarettes a day and an A1C of 8.1% each shift the grade to C.
- C: Correct. Smoking 10 or more cigarettes a day and an HbA1c of 7.0% or higher are grade C modifiers.
- D: Direct evidence is used when it exists, but indirect evidence (% bone loss divided by age) grades him without old radiographs.

**Memory cue:** Grade: start at B. Smoking 10+ a day or A1C 7.0%+ = C.

### R04

**Stem:** He asks whether treating his gums could affect his diabetes. Which answer is best supported by current evidence?

A. Scaling and root planing is linked to a lower A1C about 3 months later
B. Scaling and root planing briefly raises A1C, so his physician should adjust metformin first
C. Diabetes worsens periodontitis, but periodontitis does not affect glucose control
D. Periodontal treatment improves the gums but has not been shown to change A1C

**Keyed answer:** A

**Explanation:** Diabetes and periodontitis affect each other in both directions: periodontitis is linked to a higher A1C in type 2 diabetes, and systematic reviews show that scaling and root planing lowers A1C at 3 months, with more reduction at 6 months. The tempting wrong answer is the one-way link, which leaves out the effect of periodontitis on glucose control.

**Why each option:**
- A: Correct. Systematic reviews found a lower HbA1c 3 months after scaling and root planing, with more reduction at 6 months.
- B: A1C falls, rather than rises, after scaling and root planing, so no change to metformin is called for.
- C: The relationship is bidirectional: periodontitis is linked to a higher A1C in type 2 diabetes.
- D: Reviews found a measurable A1C reduction after scaling and root planing.

**Memory cue:** Perio and diabetes are a two-way street: SRP, then A1C down at 3 months.

**Tags:** cc CC9 (+CC22); fk FK5 (+FK6); cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase plan

**Cited facts:**
- DM-7 [verified]: Diabetes and periodontitis are bidirectional: periodontitis is linked to higher A1C in type 2 diabetes, and scaling and root planing lowers A1C at 3 months, with more reduction at 6 months. Ask for the most recent A1C at the health history.
  - Diabetes (Oral Health Topics) (Periodontal disease): "periodontal treatment (i.e., scaling and root planing) significantly reduces HbA1c levels at 3 months, with even more reduction after 6 months"

### R05

**Stem:** At his first scaling and root planing visit two weeks later, his blood pressure is 166/102, and 164/100 on a repeat reading 5 minutes later. He has no symptoms and no written guidance from his physician. According to the ADA guide for adult outpatients with hypertension, which is the most appropriate action?

A. Proceed, limiting epinephrine to 0.04 mg for the visit
B. Proceed, checking his blood pressure every 10 to 15 minutes
C. Proceed, since elective care is deferred only at 180/110 or higher
D. Postpone the scaling and root planing; refer him to his physician

**Keyed answer:** D

**Explanation:** Scaling and root planing is elective care. By the ADA guide, a reading over 160/100 is repeated, and when it is confirmed, elective treatment is postponed and the patient is referred to his physician. The tempting wrong answer is the 180/110 cutoff, which the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline gives for deferring elective major surgery, not the ADA dental threshold.

**Why each option:**
- A: The 0.04-mg epinephrine limit applies when care goes ahead in a patient who needs caution; it does not make elective care appropriate at this pressure.
- B: Checks every 10-15 minutes are the ADA approach for emergency care in the 160-180/100-109 band, not for elective care.
- C: 180/110 is the ACC/AHA threshold for deferring elective major surgery; the ADA guide uses 160/100 for elective dental care.
- D: Correct. Confirmed over 160/100 with no physician guidance: no elective dental treatment; refer to the physician.

**Memory cue:** Elective care: confirmed over 160/100 = reschedule and refer.

**Tags:** cc CC8 (+CC9); fk FK6; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase tx

**Stem:** She is seen on a Saturday at a clinic that cannot drain the abscess or start root canal treatment until Monday. Which antibiotic regimen is most appropriate until then?

A. Clindamycin 600 mg as one dose 1 hour before the Monday visit
B. Amoxicillin 500 mg three times a day for 3-7 days
C. Cephalexin 1,000 mg on day 1, then 500 mg four times a day for 4 more days
D. Azithromycin 500 mg on day 1, then 250 mg daily for 4 more days

**Keyed answer:** D

**Explanation:** Pulp necrosis with a localized acute apical abscess, when definitive care is not immediately available, is one of the few situations where the ADA suggests an antibiotic. Her penicillin reaction was hives with lip swelling, which rules out amoxicillin and also cephalexin; azithromycin (500 mg, then 250 mg daily for 4 days) or clindamycin 300 mg four times a day are the options. The tempting wrong answer is cephalexin, the choice only when the penicillin reaction was not anaphylaxis, angioedema or hives.

**Why each option:**
- A: A single pre-visit dose is a prophylaxis pattern and does not treat the abscess; when clindamycin is used here, it is 300 mg four times a day for 3-7 days.
- B: Amoxicillin is first line only without a penicillin allergy; she had hives and lip swelling.
- C: Cephalexin (500 mg four times a day in the ADA guideline) is the alternative only when the penicillin reaction did not include anaphylaxis, angioedema or hives.
- D: Correct. After hives and swelling with penicillin, the ADA suggests azithromycin 500 mg on day 1, then 250 mg for four more days (or clindamycin 300 mg four times a day).

**Memory cue:** Hives or swelling with penicillin: skip the cephalosporin too. Azithromycin 500, then 250 for 4 days.

**Tags:** cc CC26 (+CC20); fk FK8 (+FK7); cognitive level 3; difficulty 3; phase tx

**Cited facts:**

- C: Metronidazole increases warfarin's effect; it does not lower the INR.
- D: Correct. The metronidazole label warns that it potentiates warfarin, prolonging the prothrombin time; monitor the INR.

### R08

**Patient:** Female, 72 years old

**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Osteoporosis
- Denosumab (Prolia®) 60 mg injection every 6 months; last dose 3 weeks ago
- Hypothyroidism
- Levothyroxine 50 mcg daily
- No known drug allergies

- No exposed bone anywhere in the mouth

**Stem:** She and her physician decide to time the extraction of tooth 3 around her denosumab injections. Which schedule follows the AAOMS 2022 position paper?

A. Extract only once her serum CTX level has risen above 150 pg/mL
B. Extract 3-4 months after the last injection; resume 6-8 weeks after surgery
C. Extract now and hold her next two injections so that the socket can heal
D. Stop denosumab for 1 year before extracting, then restart it once the socket heals

**Keyed answer:** B

**Explanation:** Denosumab's effect on osteoclasts wanes toward the end of each dosing interval, so AAOMS 2022 suggests doing planned dentoalveolar surgery 3 to 4 months after the last dose and restarting the drug 6 to 8 weeks after surgery; a longer stop risks rebound bone loss and vertebral fractures. Bone turnover markers such as CTX are not validated for this decision. The tempting wrong answer is a long drug holiday, which for denosumab risks rebound bone loss and vertebral fractures.

**Why each option:**
- A: Bone turnover markers such as CTX are not validated for timing dental surgery.
- B: Correct. AAOMS 2022: complete the surgery 3-4 months after the last denosumab dose, when osteoclast inhibition is waning, and restart it 6-8 weeks after surgery.
- C: Holding two injections (a year off denosumab) risks rebound bone loss and fractures and is not the AAOMS timing.
- D: A long pause in denosumab risks rebound bone loss and multiple vertebral fractures; AAOMS times surgery within the dosing cycle instead.

**Memory cue:** Denosumab: extract 3-4 months after the shot, restart 6-8 weeks later.

**Tags:** cc CC8 (+CC33); fk FK8; cognitive level 3; difficulty 3; phase plan

**Cited facts:**
- MR-5 [verified]: Drug holidays: still controversial; the 2022 working group could not reach consensus (split evenly between case-by-case holidays using prior recommendations and never offering them). Factors: cancer vs osteoporosis, dosing frequency, duration, comorbidities, other drugs (chemotherapy, steroids, antiangiogenics), infection, extent of surgery. **Patient:** Male, 66 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "There is a rough spot of bone where my tooth was pulled." (4 months)

- Squamous cell carcinoma of the floor of the mouth, treated with surgery and radiation therapy to the jaws (2021)
- Osteoporosis
- Alendronate (Fosamax®) 70 mg weekly since 2022

- Amlodipine 5 mg daily
- Former smoker; quit in 2021
**Current Findings:**
- BP 132/80, pulse 72
- Exposed yellow-white bone, 6 mm across, in the socket of tooth 30, extracted 4 months ago
- No pain, pus, swelling or fistula
- Panoramic radiograph: no fracture

**Stem:** Which classification fits the exposed bone best?

A. Medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw, stage 1
B. Delayed socket healing that does not yet meet a necrosis definition
C. Osteoradionecrosis rather than medication-related osteonecrosis
D. Medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw, stage 2

**Keyed answer:** C

**Explanation:** MRONJ needs all three parts of the AAOMS definition: antiresorptive exposure, bone exposed for more than 8 weeks, and no history of radiation therapy to the jaws or jaw metastasis. He has the first two, but radiation to the jaws in 2021 excludes MRONJ by definition, which points to osteoradionecrosis. The tempting wrong answer is MRONJ stage 1, which fits the bone and the drug until the radiation history is read.

**Why each option:**
- A: Exposed bone with no infection would be stage 1, but a history of jaw radiation excludes MRONJ by definition.
- B: Bone still exposed 4 months after an extraction in an irradiated jaw is well past ordinary delayed healing.
- C: Correct. The MRONJ definition requires no history of radiation therapy to the jaws; exposed bone in an irradiated jaw is osteoradionecrosis.
- D: Stage 2 needs infection or inflammation with symptoms; he has none, and the jaw radiation rules out MRONJ anyway.

**Memory cue:** Radiation to the jaw? Then it is not MRONJ.
