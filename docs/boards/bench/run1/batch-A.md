### A01

**Patient:** Female, 57 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "This tooth has been killing me since last night; I couldn't sleep." (1 day)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Hypertension
- Hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg daily; missed this morning's dose
- Osteoarthritis of the knees
- Allergy: codeine (vomiting)
- Office manager; never smoked
**Current Findings:**
- BP 174/104 on arrival, 172/102 on a repeat reading 5 minutes later; pulse 92
- No headache, chest pain, shortness of breath or vision changes
- Tooth 14: deep caries; lingering pain to cold; tender to percussion

**Stem:** She asks to have the tooth treated today. According to the ADA guide for adult outpatients with hypertension, which is the most appropriate plan?

A. Proceed with emergency care and recheck her blood pressure at the end of the visit
B. Defer all treatment and refer her to her physician, since her pressure is over 160/100
C. Give a sublingual nitroglycerin tablet and start once her pressure is below 140/90
D. Start emergency care, checking her blood pressure every 10 to 15 minutes during the procedure with anxiety reduction, as the ADA guide allows in this band when pain contributes

**Keyed answer:** D

**Explanation:** Her confirmed reading is in the ADA band of 160-180 systolic and/or 100-109 diastolic, and pain is likely driving it. In that band, emergency care may begin with blood pressure checks every 10 to 15 minutes and anxiety reduction; only above 180/109 should the dentist consult the physician first. The tempting wrong answer is to defer everything at 160/100, which is the rule for elective care.

**Why each option:**
- A: Monitoring is needed during the procedure, every 10 to 15 minutes, not only at the end.
- B: Over 160/100 rules out elective care; emergency care in this band can proceed with monitoring.
- C: Sublingual nitroglycerin is for an anginal attack; it is not used to bring blood pressure down before dental care.
- D: Correct. For confirmed 160-180/100-109 readings when pain contributes, the ADA guide allows emergency care with blood pressure checks every 10 to 15 minutes and anxiety reduction.

**Memory cue:** 160-180 / 100-109 with pain: treat the emergency, cuff every 10-15 minutes.

**Tags:** cc CC8 (+CC16); fk FK6 (+FK8); cognitive level 3; difficulty 3; phase plan

### A02

**Stem:** Which statement about the FDA Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule (PLLR) for prescription drug labels is accurate?

A. The rule changed over-the-counter labels in the same way as prescription labels
B. Category X remains on the labels of drugs known to cause birth defects
C. Prescription labels no longer carry the letter categories A, B, C, D and X
D. Labels keep the letter category and add a narrative summary beneath it

**Keyed answer:** C

**Explanation:** The rule (effective June 30, 2015) removed the pregnancy letter categories from prescription labels and replaced them with narrative subsections: 8.1 Pregnancy, 8.2 Lactation and 8.3 Females and Males of Reproductive Potential. Over-the-counter labels are not affected. The tempting wrong answer keeps category X for teratogens, but no letter survived, including X.

**Why each option:**
- A: Over-the-counter drug labels are not affected by the rule.
- B: Category X was removed with the others; the risk is described in the 8.1 Pregnancy narrative.
- C: Correct. All five letter categories were removed and replaced with narrative subsections.
- D: The letters were removed, not kept alongside the narrative.

**Memory cue:** PLLR: letters out, narrative in (8.1, 8.2, 8.3). OTC untouched.

**Tags:** cc CC25; fk FK8; cognitive level 1; difficulty 2; phase clear

**Cited facts:**
- PG-8 [verified]: FDA labeling: the Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule (PLLR), published Dec 2014, effective June 30, 2015, removed the letter categories A, B, C, D and X from prescription drug labels and replaced them with narrative subsections 8.1 Pregnancy (with pregnancy exposure registry information), 8.2 Lactation (replacing "Nursing mothers") and 8.3 Females and Males of Reproductive Potential (pregnancy testing, contraception, infertility). New drugs after June 30, 2015 use it immediately; labels approved on or after June 30, 2001 were phased in. OTC labels are not affected (FDA PLLR page). ADA gives a full compliance date of June 2020.
  - Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling (Drugs) Final Rule (Overview): "The PLLR removes pregnancy letter categories – A, B, C, D and X."
  - Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling (Drugs) Final Rule (Implementation): "Prescription drugs and biologic products submitted after June 30, 2015, will use the new format immediately, while labeling for prescription drugs approved on or after June 30, 2001, will be phased in gradually."
  - Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling (Drugs) Final Rule (Implementation): "OTC drug products are not affected by the final rule"
  - Pregnancy (Oral Health Topics) (Medication Selection): "with a full compliance date of June 2020"

### A03

**Patient:** Female, 34 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "My lower left back tooth hurts when I bite down." (5 days)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Asthma, mild
- Albuterol inhaler as needed
- Root canal treatment of tooth 19 two weeks ago, by another dentist
- No known drug allergies
- Graduate student; does not smoke
**Current Findings:**
- Tooth 19: temporary filling intact; root canal filling seen on the radiograph
- Percussion and biting on tooth 19: sharp pain
- Palpation of the buccal mucosa: normal; no sinus tract
- Periapical radiograph: no periapical radiolucency
- Probing depths 2-3 mm

**Stem:** Which apical diagnosis fits tooth 19?

A. Normal apical tissues
B. Symptomatic apical periodontitis
C. Chronic apical abscess
D. Asymptomatic apical periodontitis

**Keyed answer:** B

**Explanation:** Apical diagnoses come from symptoms and tests, not from the radiograph alone. A painful response to biting and percussion is symptomatic apical periodontitis, and that diagnosis may or may not show an apical radiolucency. The tempting wrong answer is normal apical tissues, chosen when the clean radiograph is read before the percussion test.

**Why each option:**
- A: Normal apical tissues are not tender to percussion or biting; tooth 19 is.
- B: Correct. A painful response to biting or percussion; an apical radiolucency may or may not be present.
- C: A chronic apical abscess drains through a sinus tract with little or no discomfort; there is no sinus tract here.
- D: Asymptomatic apical periodontitis shows an apical radiolucency and no clinical symptoms; this tooth has symptoms and no radiolucency.

**Memory cue:** Tap tells the apex: tender = symptomatic apical periodontitis, with or without a radiolucency.

**Tags:** cc CC20; fk FK6; cognitive level 2; difficulty 2; phase dx

**Cited facts:**
- EN-9 [verified]: Symptomatic apical periodontitis: apical inflammation of pulpal origin with a painful response to biting and/or percussion or palpation; a radiolucency may or may not be present.
  - Diagnosis consensus among endodontic specialists and general practitioners: an international survey and a proposed modification (Table 3): "producing clinical symptoms including a painful response to biting and/or percussion or palpation. It might or might not be associated with an apical radiolucent area"
- EN-10 [verified]: Asymptomatic apical periodontitis: apical inflammation and destruction of pulpal origin that shows as an apical radiolucency and causes no clinical symptoms.
  - Diagnosis consensus among endodontic specialists and general practitioners: an international survey and a proposed modification (Table 3): "appears as an apical radiolucent area, and does not produce clinical symptoms"
- EN-11 [verified]: Acute apical abscess: rapid onset, spontaneous pain, tooth tender to pressure, pus and swelling. Chronic apical abscess: gradual onset, little or no discomfort, intermittent pus discharge through a sinus tract.
  - Diagnosis consensus among endodontic specialists and general practitioners: an international survey and a proposed modification (Table 3): "characterized by rapid onset, spontaneous pain, tenderness of the tooth to pressure, pus formation and swelling of as"
  - Diagnosis consensus among endodontic specialists and general practitioners: an international survey and a proposed modification (Table 3): "characterized by gradual onset, little or no discomfort and the intermittent discharge of pus through an associated sinus tract"
- EN-14 [verified]: Percussion/bite tenderness points to the apical diagnosis (symptomatic apical periodontitis), not the pulp diagnosis; a radiolucency with no tenderness = asymptomatic apical periodontitis; a sinus tract = chronic apical abscess; swelling with rapid onset = acute apical abscess.
  - Diagnosis consensus among endodontic specialists and general practitioners: an international survey and a proposed modification (Table 3): "a painful response to biting and/or percussion or palpation"
  - Diagnosis consensus among endodontic specialists and general practitioners: an international survey and a proposed modification (Table 3): "intermittent discharge of pus through an associated sinus tract"

### A05

**Stem:** She is seen on a Saturday at a clinic that cannot drain the abscess or start root canal treatment until Monday. Which antibiotic regimen is most appropriate until then?

A. Cephalexin 500 mg four times a day for 3-7 days
B. Azithromycin 500 mg on day 1, then 250 mg daily for 4 more days
C. Amoxicillin 500 mg three times a day for 3-7 days
D. Clindamycin 600 mg as one dose 1 hour before the Monday visit

**Keyed answer:** B

**Explanation:** Pulp necrosis with a localized acute apical abscess, when definitive care is not immediately available, is one of the few situations where the ADA suggests an antibiotic. Her penicillin reaction was hives with lip swelling, which rules out amoxicillin and also cephalexin; azithromycin (500 mg, then 250 mg daily for 4 days) or clindamycin 300 mg four times a day are the options. The tempting wrong answer is cephalexin, the choice only when the penicillin reaction was not anaphylaxis, angioedema or hives.

**Why each option:**
- A: Cephalexin is the ADA alternative only when the penicillin reaction did not include anaphylaxis, angioedema or hives.
- B: Correct. After hives and swelling with penicillin, the ADA suggests azithromycin 500 mg on day 1, then 250 mg for four more days (or clindamycin 300 mg four times a day).
- C: Amoxicillin is first line only without a penicillin allergy; she had hives and lip swelling.
- D: A single pre-visit dose is a prophylaxis pattern and does not treat the abscess; when clindamycin is used here, it is 300 mg four times a day for 3-7 days.

**Memory cue:** Hives or swelling with penicillin: skip the cephalosporin too. Azithromycin 500, then 250 for 4 days.

**Tags:** cc CC26 (+CC20); fk FK8 (+FK7); cognitive level 3; difficulty 3; phase tx

**Cited facts:**
- AB-3 [verified]: Pulp necrosis with localized acute apical abscess (no fascial space spread, fever or malaise): if definitive care is not immediately available, give amoxicillin 500 mg three times a day, 3-7 days, or penicillin VK 500 mg four times a day, 3-7 days (conditional, possibly delayed prescription).   - Evidence-based clinical practice guideline on antibiotic use for the urgent management of pulpal- and periapical-related dental pain and intraoral swelling (Table 2 footnotes): "the panel suggests dentists prescribe oral cephalexin (500 milligrams, four times a day, 3–7d)"
