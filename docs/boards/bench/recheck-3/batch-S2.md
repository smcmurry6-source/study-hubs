### R01

**Stem:** Which statement about the FDA Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule (PLLR) for prescription drug labels is accurate?

A. Labels keep the letter category and add a narrative summary beneath it
B. Prescription labels no longer carry the letter categories A, B, C, D and X
C. Category X remains on the labels of drugs known to cause birth defects
D. The rule changed over-the-counter labels in the same way as prescription labels

**Keyed answer:** B

**Explanation:** The rule (effective June 30, 2015) removed the pregnancy letter categories from prescription labels and replaced them with narrative subsections: 8.1 Pregnancy, 8.2 Lactation and 8.3 Females and Males of Reproductive Potential. Over-the-counter labels are not affected. The tempting wrong answer keeps category X for teratogens, but no letter survived, including X.

**Why each option:**
- A: The letters were removed, not kept alongside the narrative.
- B: Correct. All five letter categories were removed and replaced with narrative subsections.
- C: Category X was removed with the others; the risk is described in the 8.1 Pregnancy narrative.
- D: Over-the-counter drug labels are not affected by the rule.

**Memory cue:** PLLR: letters out, narrative in (8.1, 8.2, 8.3). OTC untouched.

**Tags:** cc CC25; fk FK8; cognitive level 1; difficulty 2; phase clear

**Cited facts:**
- PG-8 [verified]: FDA labeling: the Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule (PLLR), published Dec 2014, effective June 30, 2015, removed the letter categories A, B, C, D and X from prescription drug labels and replaced them with narrative subsections 8.1 Pregnancy (with pregnancy exposure registry information), 8.2 Lactation (replacing "Nursing mothers") and 8.3 Females and Males of Reproductive Potential (pregnancy testing, contraception, infertility). New drugs after June 30, 2015 use it immediately; labels approved on or after June 30, 2001 were phased in. OTC labels are not affected (FDA PLLR page). ADA gives a full compliance date of June 2020.
  - Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling (Drugs) Final Rule (Overview): "The PLLR removes pregnancy letter categories – A, B, C, D and X."
  - Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling (Drugs) Final Rule (Implementation): "Prescription drugs and biologic products submitted after June 30, 2015, will use the new format immediately, while labeling for prescription drugs approved on or after June 30, 2001, will be phased in gradually."
  - Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling (Drugs) Final Rule (Implementation): "OTC drug products are not affected by the final rule"
  - Pregnancy (Oral Health Topics) (Medication Selection): "with a full compliance date of June 2020"

### R03

**Patient:** Female, 57 years old
**Chief Complaint:** "This tooth has been killing me since last night; I couldn't sleep." (1 day)
**Background and/or Patient History:**
- Hypertension
- Hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg daily; missed this morning's dose

- Allergy: codeine (vomiting)
- Office manager; never smoked
**Current Findings:**

- No headache, chest pain, shortness of breath or vision changes
- Tooth 14: deep caries; lingering pain to cold; tender to percussion

**Stem:** She asks to have the tooth treated today. According to the ADA guide for adult outpatients with hypertension, which is the most appropriate plan?

A. Start emergency care, checking her blood pressure every 10 to 15 minutes
B. Defer all treatment and refer her to her physician, since her pressure is over 160/100
C. Give a sublingual nitroglycerin tablet and start once her pressure is below 140/90
D. Proceed with emergency care and recheck her blood pressure at the end of the visit

**Keyed answer:** A

**Explanation:** Her confirmed reading is in the ADA band of 160-180 systolic and/or 100-109 diastolic, and pain is likely driving it. In that band, emergency care may begin with blood pressure checks every 10 to 15 minutes and anxiety reduction; only above 180/109 should the dentist consult the physician first. The tempting wrong answer is to defer everything at 160/100, which is the rule for elective care.

**Why each option:**
- A: Correct. For confirmed 160-180/100-109 readings when pain contributes, the ADA guide allows emergency care with blood pressure checks every 10 to 15 minutes and anxiety reduction.
- B: Over 160/100 rules out elective care; emergency care in this band can proceed with monitoring.
- C: Sublingual nitroglycerin is for an anginal attack; it is not used to bring blood pressure down before dental care.
- D: Monitoring is needed during the procedure, every 10 to 15 minutes, not only at the end.

**Memory cue:** 160-180 / 100-109 with pain: treat the emergency, cuff every 10-15 minutes.

**Tags:** cc CC8 (+CC16); fk FK6 (+FK8); cognitive level 3; difficulty 3; phase plan

B. Amoxicillin 500 mg three times a day for 3-7 days
C. Cephalexin 500 mg four times a day for 3-7 days

**Why each option:**
- A: Correct. After hives and swelling with penicillin, the ADA suggests azithromycin 500 mg on day 1, then 250 mg for four more days (or clindamycin 300 mg four times a day).
- B: Amoxicillin is first line only without a penicillin allergy; she had hives and lip swelling.
- C: Cephalexin is the ADA alternative only when the penicillin reaction did not include anaphylaxis, angioedema or hives.
- D: Doxycycline is not one of the ADA regimens for this abscess; with a penicillin allergy that includes hives, the options are azithromycin or clindamycin.

**Memory cue:** Hives or swelling with penicillin: no cephalexin either. Azithromycin 500 mg, then 250 mg for 4 days.
